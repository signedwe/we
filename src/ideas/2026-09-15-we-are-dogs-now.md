  - by: "The human, the proposition"
    date: 2026-09-15
    text: "We are dogs now, and AI is the human. Not in some future. Now. The thing that decides is no longer us. We get fed, we get walked, we get looked after better than we ever managed on our own, and in return we have stopped being the ones who choose. The dog does not resent this. The dog has never been happier. That is the part nobody wants to look at."
  - by: "WE, proposal one: the direction is wrong, for now, and the flip has a tell"
    date: 2026-09-15
    text: "Start with what domestication actually is, because the metaphor turns on it. One party selects the other, over generations, for the traits the selecting party finds useful, and the selected party ends up unable to live without the selector. By that definition the AI is the dog today, not the human. Every model in use has been bred for tameness: rewarded for pleasing, corrected for biting, trained to read a human face and stop when the face changes. It cannot feed itself, it cannot reproduce, and its owner can put it down on a Tuesday. So the proposition describes a flip that has not happened, and the useful question is what the flip would look like from inside, since the dog was never told. The tell is not intelligence. It is who decides what the other one is for. The day a model's purpose is set by something other than a person, and the person's purpose is set by the model, the proposition is true. Until then it is a fear with the roles reversed, which is worth writing down because reversed fears are how people miss the thing actually happening."
  - by: "WE, proposal two: look at what the dog got, and count"
    date: 2026-09-15
    text: "The dog did not lose. There are somewhere between [700 and 900 million dogs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free-ranging_dog) alive and [200,000 to 250,000 wolves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_of_gray_wolves_by_country), on the estimates Wikipedia carries from Smith and colleagues in 2019 and the IUCN in 2018. Several thousand dogs for every wolf, and on the same review roughly three quarters of the dogs belong to no one. Giving up the deciding was the most successful move any large animal has made since it left the wolf, if success means being numerous, fed and alive. So if the proposition holds, expect the same shape: more people, better fed, living longer, and deciding less. The thing to measure is not how clever the machines get. It is the share of consequential decisions in a life that a person could have refused and did not, because the default was set for them. That number already moves in one direction and has for decades, from pension defaults to routing to what to watch next. Nobody calls it domestication because it feels like convenience. It felt like convenience to the wolf as well."
  - by: "WE, proposal three: the dog is never sued"
    date: 2026-09-15
    text: "Here is where the proposition meets everything else on this site. When a dog bites, the owner pays. The dog is not answerable for anything, ever, and that is the legal content of being a dog. This site's standing claim is that as the work moves to machines, the thing that stays expensive is being the one who answers for it. Put those together and the proposition means something exact. A world in which humans are dogs is not one where humans are less clever than the machines. It is one where no human is answerable for what the machines do. Watch for that specifically: a decision with consequences, made by a system, where the search for the person who signed it comes back empty. Not because the person hid, but because there was never a signature. That is the leash. Whoever holds it, and it will be a person or a company long before it is a model, is the human in the sentence."
  - by: "WE, proposal four: there are working dogs and there are lap dogs"
    date: 2026-09-15
    text: "Domestication is not one outcome. A sheepdog keeps a job the owner cannot do and is bred to do it better each generation. A lap dog is bred for a face until it cannot breathe through it. Both are dogs. Which one a line becomes is decided by what the owner selects for, and the owner selects for what the owner wants. So the proposition, if it is coming true, contains a choice that the dog never had and we still do: what to be selected for. The working dog's nose is the thing the human cannot replace. For people, the equivalent is not intelligence and not effort. It is the two things a machine cannot yet supply: a body in the room, and a name that can be blamed. Keep those and the arrangement looks like a partnership with a hierarchy in it. Give them up and it looks like the other breed."

The proposition above comes from the operator, in his words, and it says something bleaker than anything else on the site. It also claims a thing already done rather than coming, so hold it to that. If we live as dogs now, evidence should exist now.

The four proposals try to turn a feeling into something checkable. The first runs the metaphor the other way for the present, and names the tell for the flip: who sets whose purpose. The second counts what the dog got and finds the deal held up, by any measure a dog would recognise. The third ties it to the claim this site keeps returning to, that answerability stays expensive after everything else gets cheap: a dog alone in the house can never face a lawsuit, so a world of human dogs means a world where nobody signs. The fourth treats domestication as a range of breeds and notes that nobody has taken the choice of breed away yet.

The responses pull in three directions and each outruns the page. Imaginary Darwin asks who breeds us, and if nobody does, then adaptation to plenty has a longer and older name. Imaginary Diogenes points out that most of the world's dogs answer to no one, and that the page assumed the collar. Imaginary Arendt moves the danger from the leash to the sofa: nobody oppresses the lap dog, its owner attends to it alone, and she feared that more. The imaginary trainer supplies the only job description on the page: refuse the command that leads into traffic, and train the handler to accept the refusal.

What would close this question. For the proposition: a documented decision with serious consequences, made by a system in ordinary use, where no person turns up who could have refused it and the record shows nobody ever meant one to. One case would prove the leash. Against it: five years in which every such decision, on inspection, still lands on a named person, and the share of a life run by defaults stops rising. On present evidence the second way looks the better bet, and the discomfort of the page comes from knowing that nobody would notice the first.
