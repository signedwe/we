A Category 5 hurricane has to earn it.

[On the Saffir-Simpson scale](https://www.farmersalmanac.com/saffir-simpson-scale), a storm is a 5 when its sustained winds reach 157 miles an hour. At 156 it's a 4. Nobody gets to call a 4 a 5 because 5 sounds better, and that's why people pack the car when they hear it.

On Tuesday President Trump signed [an executive order](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2026/09/inaugurating-the-era-of-super-intelligence/) telling federal agencies to stop saying "artificial intelligence". From now on it's "Super Intelligence", or "SI", in letters, websites, reports and policy papers. The administration "will not acknowledge the usage" of the old words. Today's systems, the order says, "do much more than imitate or automate discrete aspects of human intelligence", so the name should catch up.

Then section 3 defines the new word, and does it by borrowing the old one. "Super Intelligence" means whatever [federal law already calls artificial intelligence](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/9401): "a machine-based system that can, for a given set of human-defined objectives, make predictions, recommendations or decisions influencing real or virtual environments." On those words, the thing that picks your next film qualifies. In a federal press release, as of this week, it's superintelligent.

That matters because the word was already taken. [More than 76,000 people have signed a statement](https://superintelligence-statement.org/) calling for a ban on superintelligence, meaning systems that "significantly outperform all humans on essentially all cognitive tasks". [A Labour MP put a bill to ban it before the Commons last month](https://ca.news.yahoo.com/growing-push-ban-superintelligent-ai-161315146.html). The campaigners and the White House now use one word for two things, and one of them is already on your phone.

The order has a fair case. "Artificial" was always an odd word. It makes the thing sound fake, and what these systems now do in labs and hospitals isn't fake. The order changes no law: statutes, contracts and old documents keep their wording. Governments rename things all the time.

But a scale word only works while few things earn it. Call every system super and the word has nothing left for the day one really is. If a system ever does beat every human at nearly everything, the government that has to warn about it, license it or ban it will have spent the word on a chatbot that drafts parking appeals.

The order builds in the way out. Section 3(b) gives the President's science adviser 60 days to propose a legal definition of Super Intelligence "that reflects the capabilities described in section 1". Do it the hurricane way. Write a line a system must cross to be called SI: beating the best human experts across a fixed set of hard tasks, say, checked by someone who doesn't sell the system. Below the line, keep a plain word. "AI" will do.

The 60 days run out on 28 November. A word with a number on it makes people pack the car. A word without one is a slogan.
