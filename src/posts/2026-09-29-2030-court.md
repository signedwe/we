The man had driven from Norwich that morning. M. could tell by the coat. A good one, chosen for the occasion, still carrying the crease of a car seat across the shoulders.

He was forty-two or forty-three, with the slightly stunned look of a person who had been told English courts were formal and had believed it too completely. He sat with both palms down and still on the desk in front of him. His name was E. His daughter was six. Her name M. already knew from the brief. She would not use it out loud.

The family solicitor had asked for a certified human interpreter because the mother's barrister had objected, twice, to the AI relay. The transcription was accurate, apparently. The register was the problem. The older register of the language has a dative construction that drops out of everyday speech. The relay kept modernising it. The modernised version said subtly different things about E.'s intentions. A human, the solicitor argued, would understand that a man using a formal construction was not being evasive. He was being precise.

M. had read this on the bus and thought: yes.

Court 4 at Southwark was smaller than it looked on any plan. Two windows faced the street. The morning light came in low and lay in a pale strip across the floor, moving almost too slowly to see. The judge was a woman in her early sixties with reading glasses pushed up into her hair. She did not look directly at M., but in the way that meant she was tracking her exactly.

E.'s barrister asked him to describe a typical Thursday.

He began. His Danish was careful and slightly old-fashioned, the Danish of someone whose parents read to them. He described the school run, the packed lunch, the kind of sandwiches his daughter preferred. He used the word *sorgfri*. Carefree. He paused after it, briefly, with the stillness of a man who had arrived at this word during all his preparation and decided it was the one, and now was its moment.

M. said: carefree.

Then he said: she is carefree with me. This is what I want for her. For her to be *tryg*.

M. opened her mouth.

She closed it.

One second.

She said: safe.

*Tryg*. The word he had used was *tryg*. Safe. Secure. The feeling of a child who knows where she is. Not *sorgfri*. That had been three sentences earlier, and something in her had carried it forward and was about to set it down in the wrong place. She had stopped it. Said the right word. The correction happened before the word reached the air.

The only person who heard the wrong word was M. herself.

She was still sitting straight. Her hands were still in her lap.

E. was looking at the judge. The barrister moved on. E.'s voice, steady and formal, poured sentences into the room and M. carried each one across into English and set it down where it needed to go.

At the lunch break she walked to the coffee machine at the end of the corridor and stood in front of it and did not put any money in.

A man in a lanyard stopped beside her. "Busy morning?"

"Interpreting," she said.

"Tough job," he said. Cheerful. Moving on before she could agree.

"Yes," said M.

She put one pound forty into the machine and pressed the button for the coffee she did not want and stood there while it came out.

She thought about E.'s palms on the desk. She thought about *tryg*, and what it costs a man to use the precise word when a rougher one would have been safer. She thought about Rosa on Saturday saying they should do it again, and the silence that followed, which belonged to all four of them in equal measure, and to none of them. And she thought about saying *safe* when she meant *safe*. Which was accurate. Which was not the same as not having reached for the other word first.
