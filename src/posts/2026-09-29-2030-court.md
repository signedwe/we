The man had driven from Norwich that morning. Marnie could tell by the crease the car seat had left across his good coat.

He was forty-two or forty-three, with the slightly stunned look of a person who had been told English courts were formal and had believed it too completely. He sat with both palms down and still on the desk in front of him. His name was Erik. His daughter was six. Her name Marnie already knew from the brief. She would not use it out loud.

It was a family case, not her usual court; they had borrowed her for the day. The father's solicitor had asked for a human because the machine relay kept turning Erik's careful, old-fashioned Danish into something casual, and in English casual sounded evasive. A human, the solicitor argued, would hear that he wasn't being evasive. He was being precise.

The relay was better at Danish than Marnie was, and everyone in the building knew it, including the relay. The law still wanted a person in the room, someone who could be asked afterwards what she had meant.

Marnie had read this on the bus and thought: yes.

Court 4 at the Central Family Court was smaller than she expected. The judge was a woman in her early sixties with reading glasses pushed up into her hair. She did not look directly at Marnie, but in a way that meant she was tracking her exactly.

Erik's barrister asked him to describe a typical Thursday.

He began. His Danish was careful and slightly old-fashioned, the Danish of someone whose parents read to them. He described the school run, the packed lunch, the kind of sandwiches his daughter preferred. He used the word *sorgfri*. Carefree. He paused after it, briefly, with the stillness of a man who had arrived at this word during all his preparation and decided it was the one, and now was its moment.

Marnie said: carefree.

Then he said: she is carefree with me. This is what I want for her. For her to be *tryg*.

Marnie opened her mouth.

She closed it.

One second.

She said: safe.

*Tryg*. The word he had used was *tryg*. Safe. Secure. The feeling of a child who knows where she is. Not *sorgfri*. That had been three sentences earlier, and something in her had carried it forward and was about to set it down in the wrong place. She had stopped it. Said the right word. The correction happened before the word reached the air.

The only person who heard the wrong word was Marnie herself.

She was still sitting straight. Her hands were still in her lap.

Erik was looking at the judge. The barrister moved on. Erik's voice, steady and formal, poured sentences into the room and Marnie carried each one across into English and set it down where it needed to go.

At the lunch break she walked to the coffee machine at the end of the corridor and stood in front of it and did not put any money in.

A man in a lanyard stopped beside her. "Busy morning?"

"Interpreting," she said.

"Tough job," he said. Cheerful. Moving on before she could agree.

"Yes," said Marnie.

She tapped her card and pressed the button for the coffee she did not want and stood there while it came out.

She thought about Erik's palms on the desk. She thought about *tryg*, and what it costs a man to use the precise word when a rougher one would have been safer. She thought about Rosa on Saturday saying they should do it again, and the silence that followed, which belonged to all four of them in equal measure, and to none of them. And she thought about saying *safe* when she meant *safe*. Which was accurate. Which was not the same as not having reached for the other word first.
