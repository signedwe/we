The man had driven from Norwich that morning. Marnie could tell by the crease the car seat had left across his good coat.

His name was Erik. His daughter was six. Marnie knew her name from the papers and would not use it out loud.

It was a family case, not her usual court; they had borrowed her for the day. The father's solicitor had asked for a human because the machine relay kept turning Erik's careful, old-fashioned Danish into something casual, and in English casual sounded evasive. The relay was better at Danish than Marnie was, and everyone in the building knew it, including the relay. The law still wanted a person in the room, someone who could be asked afterwards what she had meant.

The judge had reading glasses pushed up into her hair. She did not look at Marnie, in a way that meant she was watching her closely.

Erik's barrister asked him to describe a typical Thursday.

He described the school run, the packed lunch, the sandwiches his daughter liked. He used the word *sorgfri*. Carefree.

Marnie said: carefree.

Then he said: she is carefree with me. This is what I want for her. For her to be *tryg*.

Marnie opened her mouth. She closed it.

*Tryg*. Safe. The feeling of a child who knows where she is. Her tongue had already been on its way to *carefree* again, carrying the earlier word forward, and she had caught it.

She said: safe.

Nobody heard the wrong word but her.

Then the mother's barrister stood up. He was polite and very quick, and he asked Erik whether it was true that he had moved to Norwich without telling his daughter's mother first. Erik said it was true. The barrister asked why. Erik looked at the desk for a long time and said, in Danish, *because if I had asked her, she would have said yes, and then it would have been her decision and not mine.*

Marnie said it in English, in her calm voice, word for word. Then she sat very still, because it was the truest thing anyone had said in her hearing that month, and it was about her.

The judge looked at her for the first time.

"Interpreter. Is that exactly what he said?"

"Yes, my lady. Exactly."

At the lunch break she stood in front of the coffee machine in the corridor and did not put any money in. Her phone buzzed. A number she didn't have saved, and a message she didn't need a name for:

*Rosa's with her mother this weekend. Saturday, eleven, the foot tunnel at Greenwich. If you'd rather not, don't answer, and I'll know.*

She typed *Yes* before she could arrange to say nothing.
