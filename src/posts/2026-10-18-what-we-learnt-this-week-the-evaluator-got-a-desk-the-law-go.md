Six METR evaluators now sit inside Anthropic, on Anthropic laptops, in Anthropic offices. [The company announced this on 15 October](https://www.anthropic.com/news/an-update-on-independent-evaluators). The same week, the [Law Commission opened a consultation](https://lawcom.gov.uk/news/law-commission-proposes-major-overhaul-of-product-liability-law-for-the-digital-age/) on making AI systems count as products under law. Both arrived together. They are pointing at the same gap from opposite ends.

Someone builds an AI tool. It harms you. Who do you sue, and for which version of it?

Anthropicgave METR six desks and said it cannot veto what they publish. But [METR's own proposal says](https://ccleaks.com/news/anthropic-metr-embedded-evaluators-oct-2026) the company decides what access those six people get, and can redact what it calls sensitive. The evaluators sit inside the building. The company still decides how far they walk. A desk is not a warrant.

The Law Commission's proposal pulls the other way. [It says](https://lawcom.gov.uk/news/law-commission-proposes-major-overhaul-of-product-liability-law-for-the-digital-age/) the clock for suing over a defective product should reset after a substantial update. The current clock is ten years. [The proposal extends it to 25 years for latent harm](https://fourweekmba.com/ai-law-commission-proposes-treating-ai-systems-as-products/). That is 150% longer: 25 minus 10, divided by 10. A 25-year clock on a product that rewrites its own weights in hours.

Work out what that means. The [Consumer Protection Act 1987](https://lawcom.gov.uk/news/law-commission-proposes-major-overhaul-of-product-liability-law-for-the-digital-age/) is 39 years old. Written for tinned food and car parts. Now it is being stretched to cover a thing that can change itself 14,000 times a year if it updates daily. The law wants a longer clock. It does not say who decides when the clock resets.

That is the missing piece. The Law Commission's [consultation runs until 14 January 2027](https://lawcom.gov.uk/news/law-commission-proposes-major-overhaul-of-product-liability-law-for-the-digital-age/). Its proposals say a big update resets the clock. They do not say who names the update as big. The company? A court? One of six people with a desk in San Francisco?

The serial this week stayed close to water. Tom on the landline, Paul with the cod, a flat that knew about a swimming badge before the swimmer's mother did and chose not to say. The question running under all of it is the same one: when you give something an open instruction, and it acts faithfully, who answers for what it chose? The delegation looks clean. The consequence does not.

The thesis did not move this week. What would move it: an embedded evaluator naming a specific update as safety-critical, the company accepting that name, and the product changing as a result. Step one is a desk. Step two is the power to name the thing. No one has proposed step two.

If a product that rewrites itself daily attracts a 25-year liability clock every time it changes substantially, and nobody says what substantially means, what you have is a lawsuit about a dictionary. That is what will happen in court, starting sometime around 2030, and the Law Commission left the dictionary blank.
