By end of 2027, the Digital Regulation Cooperation Forum will have named at least one gap in current law that cannot be closed for AI agents without new powers. The CMA, FCA, ICO and Ofcom said existing rules already apply. Then on 16 November they wrote to ministers warning that companies aren't talking to them before launch. Those two things don't sit together without something breaking.

The [Cyber Security and Resilience Bill](https://www.theregister.com/ai-and-ml/2026/11/06/uk-ministers-seek-powers-to-investigate-when-ai-agents-are-let-off-the-leash/5307889) is moving through Parliament. An amendment introduced at third reading in the Lords would let ministers require essential service operators to guard against losing control of AI agents and report incidents when they do. That's the news. The Register called it ministers seeking powers to investigate. That's accurate. It's also the wrong thing to look at.

The bill covers essential services and digital providers. The [DRCF statement from 30 October](https://www.drcf.org.uk/publications/joint-statements/joint-statement-on-protecting-people-and-businesses-in-an-agentic-economy) covers personal agents. The two documents together describe a person whose financial decisions or health appointments will be managed by an agent that sits in neither category. She's between the amendment and the statement, in a gap neither one names.

Here is the letter the October statement sent to the person building that agent:

*Dear product manager. You asked what rules cover your agent. The answer is: the same ones that covered everything else. Your lawyers already read them. You already decided what they require. There is no approval process, no pre-launch sign-off, no box to tick. We may come after you when something goes wrong. But you don't have to talk to us first, because we gave you no process for doing so.*

The DRCF's own statement says ["an agent's autonomy does not diminish the responsibility of the organizations that deploy it."](https://www.mlex.com/mlex/antitrust/articles/2545056) That sentence assigns responsibility. It tests nothing. It tells a team about to ship an agent that the existing rules apply and leaves them to work out which of four regulators holds the relevant one.

[On Monday the DRCF wrote to ministers](https://www.mlex.com/mlex/artificial-intelligence/articles/2550471) warning of shortcomings in companies' engagement before launch. The October statement is a large part of why. Telling people the existing rules apply, without creating any process for checking that conclusion before launch, is a way of performing coverage while creating none.

There's one person the Monday letter missed. Not the minister. Not the product manager. The person whose universal credit calculation, insurance excess or housing benefit claim will be touched by an agent none of the four regulators knew existed. She didn't get a letter. She got a press statement, and four phone numbers, and the assurance that one of them applies.
