She chose a Wednesday because she had no court.

She went to Frankham Street, to the flat over the pharmacy. The fruit man was setting out his sevens. She bought a flat white from the place on the corner and stood at the bottom of the outside stairs and waited, which was something she had learned to do in courtrooms.

He came down the stairs at eight forty with his work bag.

He looked at her. The bag did not drop.

"Marnie"

"I won't be long," she said.

She had rehearsed four versions of this on the bus. None of them had started with *I won't be long*, and now she had said it and could not unsay it, which was already more honest than any of the four versions.

"I'm not going to keep doing this," she said. "I wanted to say it to your face because I've been saying it in my head for two weeks and that turns out not to count."

"Paul doesn't know," Tom said.

"He knows."

Silence.

"He hasn't said anything. He won't. That's just how he works." She looked at his bag. "Rosa's assembly is in November. We're both coming. It's already in the diary."

"Okay."

"One more thing."

He waited.

"You were the person I thought about. For years. I don't know what to do with that now except say it, because I didn't on the boat and I didn't in the tunnel and I'm not going to be someone who has three chances at a sentence and misses all of them."

He said nothing. He looked at the street behind her.

"That's it," she said. "That's everything."

She set the flat white on the low wall beside her. She had not drunk any of it and she did not take it with her.

She walked back toward the main road without turning. Behind her: a bus, a window open somewhere, someone's radio on a kitchen sill. The ordinary sounds of a Wednesday morning.

At the corner the pedestrian signal was red. She waited. It changed. She crossed.

She took the train to London Bridge, for no reason except that it was going somewhere.

On the concourse a man swore at the voice in his lapel over a pram that would not fold. Then it folded.

She watched him go.

Paul had put the assembly in the diary and coloured it and that was the shape of what she had chosen. She could see it from the concourse: a school hall, Rosa doing the poem with the actions, Paul beside her clapping at the right moment. Not a tragedy. A life of nine years that would keep going, with the other thing said out loud first, so she would always know she had said it. That was the only point of saying it at all.
