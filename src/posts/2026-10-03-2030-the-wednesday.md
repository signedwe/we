She chose a Wednesday because she had no court.

She went to Frankham Street, to the flat over the pharmacy, and stood at the bottom of the outside stairs with a coffee she didn't want. The fruit man was setting out his sevens. He nodded to her as if she were a regular. She supposed she was.

Tom came down at twenty to nine with his work bag and saw her and stopped on the third step.

"I won't be long," she said.

She had rehearsed four versions of this on the bus. None of them began with *I won't be long*, and now it was said, and it was more honest than any of the four.

"I'm stopping," she said. "Thursdays, all of it. I wanted to say it to your face, because I've been saying it in my head for two weeks and that turns out not to count."

He came down the last three steps.

"Paul doesn't know," he said.

"Paul knows."

"He's said something?"

"He won't. That's how he does it." She heard it as she said it, and was surprised by how bitter it sounded, and by how true. "Rosa's assembly's in November. We're both coming. It's in the diary. Green."

"Okay."

"One more thing."

He waited. He was very good at waiting.

"You were the one I thought about. For eleven years. I don't know what to do with that now except say it, because I didn't on the boat and I didn't in the tunnel, and I'm not going to be someone who had three chances at a sentence and missed them all."

He said nothing. He looked relieved, and she disliked him for it, and then thought she might be being unfair, and couldn't tell.

"That's it," she said. "That's everything."

She set the coffee on the low wall and walked to the main road without turning round.

At Deptford station she meant to take the train to London Bridge and go to the office and sit in the library with the Danish dictionaries, as she did on days without court. The board said London Bridge, four minutes. She went down the other stairs instead, changed at London Bridge for Liverpool Street, and at Liverpool Street bought a ticket to Harwich, which the machine sold her without a word.

The terminal had been rebuilt. The bench had not. She sat on it with her coat buttoned up and watched the Hook boat go out at a quarter past two, as it had fifty-one Fridays in a row eleven years ago, and she waited, honestly this time, to see whether anyone would come down the ramp who knew her.

Nobody did.

Fifty-two, she thought, and got the train home, and was back in Deptford before Paul, and made the supper herself for once, and did not leave him a note.
