The Greenwich foot tunnel is the right length for an argument and the wrong length for a silence.

Tom was waiting at the top of the south stairs at eleven, with two coffees and the look of a man who had been there since half past ten.

"Rosa says the tunnel's the only underground river in England," he said, on the way down. "I said it's a tunnel under a river. She said, is the river above us? I said yes. She said, then that's what I said."

"She's right," said Marnie.

"She's eight."

"She's still right."

"Last week she asked the house assistant if it was cleverer than her. It said yes. She said, at everything? It said nearly everything." He shook his head. "She's been looking for the thing ever since. So far it's handstands."

The tunnel curved ahead of them, white tiles and long lights. A woman in a delivery vest passed the other way, talking to her wrist. Their footsteps came back at them from both ends.

They came up on the north side and walked along the river to where a bench faced the water. The self-driving river taxi went by, low and empty. Nobody looked at it any more.

"Rosa said something about you, after the supper," Tom said. "That you ask questions without wanting the answer."

"What did you say?"

"I said she was wrong. She said, how would you know, you've known her five minutes."

Marnie laughed, which surprised her.

"She's not wrong," she said.

They sat. He talked about Harwich, the terminal, the second time he had gone back and stood there and left again. "There wasn't anything to finish," he said, "because nothing had started."

"I never got on it either," she said. "All those Fridays."

"No."

"I thought about it every time."

He turned to look at her. Neither of them moved. A man went past with a dog that wanted to go the other way.

She kissed him.

It lasted long enough to become a thing that had happened rather than a thing that was happening.

"Right," he said.

"Yes," said Marnie.

Then he stood up, quite suddenly, took her coffee out of her hand, and threw both cups, full, into the bin, and walked to the river wall and stood with his back to her, gripping it.

"I'm not this," he said, to the Thames. "I'm not the man who does this to someone."

"You didn't," she said. "I did. That's what you wanted."

He did not turn round. She left him there, went down the stairs alone, and walked back under the river, and counted every step to the other side.
