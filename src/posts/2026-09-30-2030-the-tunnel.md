The Greenwich foot tunnel is the right length for an argument and the wrong length for a silence.

M. had been told this by the tunnel itself, effectively, at age twenty-two, when she walked through it with a man she was trying to break up with and found they were still talking when they came out the other side. She had not broken up with him in the tunnel. She had done it at the DLR platform, in the rain, slightly out of breath.

T. did not know this story. She had not told it. They were three minutes in, walking south to north, and he was telling her that Rosa had informed him, with total confidence, that the tunnel was the only underground river in England. "I said it's not a river, it's a tunnel under a river. She said: is the river above us? I said: yes. She said: then that's what I said."

"She's right," said M.

"She's eight."

"She's still right."

T. stopped walking. The tunnel curved ahead of them, white tiles and long lights. A woman in a delivery vest walked past in the opposite direction, talking to her wrist.

"I looked it up after," T. said. "She was describing it the way she saw it and she was accurate."

He looked at the wall. "It's a bad habit. Explaining things to her and then finding out I was wrong."

"It's a good habit," M. said. "She'll spend her whole life telling men they've got it backwards."

He looked at her. He was smiling, but the kind of smile that is also something else, and he didn't look away.

They came up on the north side and walked along the embankment. The river was brown and purposeful, doing what the Thames does, which is move. Someone had moored a small working boat against the pier and painted it an unlikely green. The self-driving river taxi came past heading east, low in the water. Nobody on the upper deck. It had been running this route for two years and M. still slightly failed to believe in it each time.

"Rosa said something, after the supper," T. said. "That you were the kind of person who asks questions without wanting an answer."

M. said: "What did you say?"

"I said she was wrong." He paused. "She said: how would you know, you've known her for five minutes."

M. laughed. Actually laughed, which surprised her.

"She's not wrong," M. said.

"I know." T. stopped. They were at the end of the walkable stretch, where a bench faced the water. He sat down on it without asking if she wanted to sit, which she did, so she sat. The river went past. A gull stood on a bollard twenty feet away.

"I went back to Harwich," he said. "I told you that."

"You told me."

"I went twice. The second time I walked to the terminal and stood there and then walked back." He was looking at the gull. "I didn't know what I was doing. Something about finishing it properly. Except there wasn't anything to finish, because nothing had started. So."

M. said: "I never got on it, though. All those Fridays."

"No."

"I thought about it every time."

He turned to look at her. He was close enough that she could see his eyes, which she had been carefully not looking at for three weeks.

"I know," he said. "I could tell."

She said: "T."

He said: "Yes."

She kissed him.

It lasted long enough to become a thing that had happened rather than a thing that was happening. The gull lifted off the bollard and was gone. Somewhere on the river the taxi ran on eastward, carrying nobody, doing the right thing anyway.

When she moved back he was looking at her with the particular expression of a man who has been wrong about something for eleven years and has just found out what it was.

"Right," he said.

"Yes," said M.

They sat for another minute, looking at the river, which continued to have no opinion. Then she stood up. He stood up. They walked back to the tunnel entrance and went down, and she counted every step back to the other side. She could not have stopped counting if she had tried.
