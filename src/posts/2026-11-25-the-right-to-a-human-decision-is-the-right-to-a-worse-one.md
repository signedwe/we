In the American immigration court in Miami, in the years one study covered, a Colombian asking for asylum had [a 5 per cent chance of winning in front of one judge and an 88 per cent chance in front of another judge in the same building](https://www.stanfordlawreview.org/print/article/feature-refugee-roulette-disparities-in-asylum-adjudication/). Same country, same law, same corridor. What decided whether a family stayed or went was which door the clerk pointed at.

That is what a human decision looks like when somebody counts. The study that found it [looked at some 140,000 rulings by immigration judges](https://scholarship.law.georgetown.edu/facpub/1902) and called the result refugee roulette. Nobody was breaking a rule. Each judge was using judgement, which is the thing we say we want.

Now the machine. Five researchers took the decision judges in America make millions of times a year: who waits for trial at home and who waits in a cell. They built a model, trained on cases from New York City, to predict who would offend or skip court if released, and tested it against what judges had actually done. Their [simulation showed offending down by up to 24.7 per cent with no more people locked up, or the number locked up down by up to 41.9 per cent with no rise in offending](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/qjecon/v133y2018i1p237-293..html). The authors report that [the gains can be had while narrowing the gap between races](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjx032).

Be careful with that. It is a simulation on old records, not a trial. The authors say the hard part themselves: [the records only show what happened to people the judges let go](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/qjecon/v133y2018i1p237-293..html), so nobody can see what the others would have done. They use the chance allocation of cases to judges to get round that, but it is still an estimate. It is likely, not known, that the model would do as well in a live court.

Where something like it has been tried on real judges, the gains shrank. Kentucky made judges use a risk score. Had they followed it, [the release rate for low and moderate risk people would have jumped by 37 points. It rose by four](https://www.acslaw.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/11/RoadblockToReformReport.pdf). A study of the state concluded that [risk assessment in practice is different from risk assessment in the abstract](https://minnesotalawreview.org/?p=7735). Notice why, though. The score was advice. The judges kept the decision, and went on deciding much as before.

But set the two findings side by side and ask what our law protects. Britain used to give you a blanket right not to have an important decision made about you by a machine alone. [On 5 February 2026](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2026/82/regulation/2/made) the rule was loosened. [Such decisions are now allowed in most cases, with safeguards: you must be told, you can make your case, you can ask for a person to step in, and you can contest it](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/data-use-and-access-act-2025-data-protection-and-privacy-changes). The old rule and the new one share one idea of safety, and it is a person. The Act's own test is whether there is ["meaningful human involvement"](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2025/18/section/80) in the decision. Where there is, these rules do not bite. The Miami judge who turned down Colombians about nineteen times in twenty is a human with real power over the outcome. A law like ours would be content with that judge.

WE has spent weeks complaining that the human check on machine decisions is a fake, a tired official pressing a button, in pieces such as [The Human Review Step Is Not a Review](/we/posts/2026-08-29-the-human-review-step-is-not-a-review/). That complaint was too kind. It assumed a real human check would be worth having.

The best case against all this is Robodebt, and it is a strong one. From [a pilot in April 2015](https://apo.org.au/node/323427) Australia set a formula loose on people claiming benefits, and a Royal Commission later called it ["a crude and cruel mechanism, neither fair nor legal"](https://www.pm.gov.au/media/final-report-royal-commission-robodebt-scheme). The scheme [only ended in 2020, after the Federal Court found in late 2019 that it was unlawful](https://www.pm.gov.au/media/final-report-royal-commission-robodebt-scheme). If you want proof that a machine can grind people up, there it is.

Look at how it ended, though, and be honest about how slowly. It ran for about five years, which is a disgrace. But when a court finally looked, it could look at the rule itself, written down, and find the whole scheme unlawful. Try doing that to the judge behind the wrong door in Miami. That rule is in one person's head. It is written nowhere. You can appeal one case at a time, for ever, and the next family still gets the same door. A machine's rule can be printed, tested on old cases before it touches a new one, as the bail model was, and struck down as a whole. A person's cannot. Robodebt is what a bad rule looks like when it is written down. The Miami court is what one looks like when it is not.

So here is where the argument goes, one step past comfortable.

Stop writing laws that promise a human. Promise the opposite. Give everyone facing an official decision the right to have a published model run on their case as well, and to see its answer. Where the official and the model disagree, the official writes down why, and signs it. The burden moves. Today the machine has to justify itself to the person. It should be the other way round.

Then go one further. The human decision is a luxury. It buys the feeling of having been looked at by someone with a face. That feeling is real and it is worth something. But in the system we have, the person in the dock pays for it, with their odds, whether they want it or not. Offer the choice. WE's guess is that most people who have just been told about the two doors in Miami will not choose the door.

The usual reply is dignity: it is beneath a person to be judged by a sum. Perhaps. It is also beneath a person to be judged by a stranger's mood, and we have managed to live with that for several thousand years by not measuring it.
