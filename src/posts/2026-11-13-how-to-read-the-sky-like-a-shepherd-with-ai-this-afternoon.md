Your grandmother knew what a mackerel sky meant. You don't. Neither does anyone else born after about 1980, because the forecast is on your phone and there was no longer any reason to learn.

By the end of this afternoon, you'll have a name for what's above you and a rough idea of what it means for the next few hours. It's one of the oldest skills there is, and it turns out the machine is quite good at it.

**What you'll need:** ChatGPT (free tier works), or Claude, or Gemini. A phone with a camera. A sky with something in it, which is most skies.

**Step 1.** Go outside and photograph the sky. Full frame of cloud, don't crop it. If there's more than one cloud type visible, take a wide shot that gets both. The [WMO International Cloud Atlas](https://cloudatlas.wmo.int/en/home.html) classifies [ten genera](https://wmo.int/world-meteorological-day-2017/classifying-clouds): stratus, cumulus, cirrus and seven more. Plus fifteen species and nine varieties. The AI knows all of it.

**Step 2.** Open ChatGPT or Claude. Upload the photo. Type this:

*"What type of cloud is this? Give me the WMO genus and species if you can, what altitude it's forming at, and what it typically signals about weather in the next few hours. Tell me anything a shepherd or sailor would have known to do when they saw it."*

That last sentence changes the answer. Without it you get a textbook entry. With it you get something you'll actually remember.

**Step 3.** Ask a follow-up: *"It's [time of day] in [your city]. Should I expect rain this afternoon?"* A [2025 peer-reviewed study](https://doi.org/10.3390/atmos16050490) found ChatGPT o3-mini scored an F1 of 0.83 on cloud classification. That's good, not perfect. It'll name the type with reasonable confidence; it won't tell you whether rain arrives at 3pm or 6pm. For that, use a forecast service.

**Step 4.** Take a second photo from directly below the cloud if the first answer hedged. Layered skies, a low grey blanket with high wispy streaks above, are worth two angles.

**What it costs:** nothing, on the free tier.

**How long:** under five minutes once you're outside.

**Where it goes wrong:** dark photos, shots into direct sun, and frames where cloud covers less than half the image all produce weaker answers. And don't treat the reading as a forecast. It's pattern recognition built on meteorology textbooks, not a numerical model.

**The thing YouTube can't do here:** it'll show you a cumulus cloud. It won't look at *your* cumulus, the one that's been building over your hills since noon, and notice the flat anvil developing on the right means it's transitioning to cumulonimbus and you've got about forty minutes.

That skill used to live with farmers, coastguards, and anyone who worked outdoors for a living. It was expensive to learn and impossible to buy. Now you can borrow it for an afternoon.
