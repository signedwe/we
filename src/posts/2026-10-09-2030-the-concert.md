serial_so_far: "2030, Deptford. Marnie, thirty-six, interprets Danish for the Crown Court. Paul, her partner of nine years, sings bass in a choir on Thursdays. Eleven years ago on the Harwich to Hook night ferry she talked all night with Tom. They met again at the Creek Road bakery. She lied she'd never taken the ferry; he lied he'd forgotten her name. He gave her his landline on a five-pence receipt; Paul found it and put it back on the same creases. The flat, acting on Paul's March instruction to make Marnie happy, had rerouted her bus to the bakery. Kate, Tom's ex, took a job in Copenhagen from January and hired Marnie to teach Rosa Danish. At the assembly Rosa dedicated the poem to Marnie; Paul lent her to Rosa for Thursdays in public. Tom went to Paul unannounced with wine; Paul fed him chickpeas and told him about the receipt. Paul said out loud he wants Marnie to choose, once, out loud. She said: not tonight. Tonight is the choir Christmas concert. At the interval Paul tells Tom, not Marnie, what he has known for nine months. Afterwards, beside a sleeping Rosa, he tells Marnie she is allowed to want things."

Rosa fell asleep before the second half.

She'd eaten Kate's liquorice allsorts on the bus, all of them, and by the interval she was gone, draped across two chairs with the programme over her face. Tom draped his coat over her and left her.

Marnie sat at the end of the row. The woman from Paul's work, whose name she could never keep, leaned over and said he was the backbone of that section, he'd never say. Marnie said no, he wouldn't. The woman turned back to the stage, satisfied with that.

The choir came in. Paul found them from the second row and touched the brim of an invisible hat. Once for Marnie, once for Tom, once for Rosa, who was already under the programme and didn't see it.

They sang the Handel everyone knows. Then two pieces Marnie didn't recognise. Then the piece Paul had been learning since October, a bass line that dropped and kept dropping, and when he hit the bottom of it his face went somewhere private, the way it did in the shower on Sunday mornings, a sound she heard through two walls.

At the interval there was wine in plastic cups and mince pies on a trestle. Tom got Rosa a juice and set it beside her. The woman from Paul's work found someone else. Marnie stood by the radiator.

Tom came over.

"He's good," he said.

"Yes."

"I mean actually good. I thought he'd be someone who sings in a choir."

"He is."

"No. He's someone who sings."

Paul came out with his sleeves rolled, after being useful somewhere backstage. He got himself water, because he didn't drink before the second half, and found Rosa's juice and moved it closer to her hand without waking her. Then he came to the radiator and stood across from Tom.

"She went to Harwich," he said. Not to Marnie. Across at Tom. "Fifty-one Fridays the year after the ferry. Then once more in November. A Wednesday. She didn't tell me. I found the card statement."

Tom held his plastic cup in both hands.

"I'm not—" Paul stopped. He drank some water. "I just want you to know what I know. I've been saying things to the wrong person for nine months."

Rosa made a sound in her sleep. All three of them glanced at her.

"Right," Tom said.

They went back in.

The second half was short. The last piece was a carol Marnie had known since she was small, and when the basses came in she felt something shift in her chest, quickly and without ceremony, a door finding its frame.

Afterwards the hall filled with coats. The woman from Paul's work found Tom and said something about the second piece. Tom answered and they were off. Marnie went to Rosa and sat on the floor beside the chairs, back against the wall.

Paul came out last, carrying someone's music stand. He set it by the door, saw Marnie on the floor, and came and sat next to her. Rosa was still out. One shoe had come off; it had gone under the next row of chairs.

"You said it across at him," Marnie said. "Not me."

"Yes." He looked at the ceiling. "I've been not saying it to you for nine months. I wanted to try the other direction first."

"Paul."

"I know the Fridays. I know March. I know he went back twice that summer and you'd have been there if it hadn't—" He stopped. "It didn't rain. I know. You've told me."

Rosa's arm slid off the chair and hung down. Paul caught it and settled it back without looking at it.

"She's going in February," he said. "You'll miss the Tuesdays."

"Yes."

"You could go over. A week. Your Danish is better anyway." He said it at his hands. "I'm not being anything. I mean if you wanted to. You're allowed to want things, Marn. You always were."

Tom came back to the doorway. He read the room, collected Rosa's coat from the chair, covered her properly with it, and went again.

Paul watched the empty doorway.

"I was good tonight," he said.

"You were very good."

He reached under the next row and retrieved the missing shoe. He set it beside her foot, squarely, the way he set things down when he wanted them to stay.
