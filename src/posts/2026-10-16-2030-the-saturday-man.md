Kate had left on a Wednesday. This was wrong, in Rosa's view. Departures happened on Fridays. Kate said she knew. Rosa said she was only pointing it out.

By Saturday Tom had lost the swimming kit.

Not lost. Moved. He had moved it on Thursday when he reorganised the airing cupboard, because the towels were in the wrong order, because Rosa had put them back herself, because he had asked her to. The swimsuit was in the bag on the back of her door, where it had always been. He had spent twenty minutes going through bathroom drawers.

Rosa stood watching from the doorway.

"It's in my bag. It's always been in my bag."

"I know."

"You've been looking for ten minutes."

"Eight."

She went to get dressed. He stayed in the bathroom holding a washcloth that had turned up somewhere wrong. He put it on the radiator, which was right, which meant it had been wrong before, which meant someone had been putting it elsewhere for years without his noticing.

He made porridge. She came in and looked at the bowl.

"It's lumpy."

"Textured."

"Marnie's isn't lumpy."

"Marnie doesn't make you porridge."

"She made it once. The time you were late from the airport."

Rosa ate without further comment. That was worse than further comment.

At the leisure centre she swam her lengths. He sat in the gallery and looked at his phone. A message from Kate the night before, sent at eleven from Copenhagen: *Rosa okay?* He typed *Yes, swimming now.* She sent a thumbs-up. That was the whole exchange.

The other parents talked among themselves. Kate had known their names, their children's names, the small history of who had fallen out with whom over the gala lane assignments. He knew none of it. He watched Rosa's tumble turn, which she had perfected and now performed with the slight excess of someone aware she had perfected it.

Afterwards she was hungry in the particular way of children who have been in water. He took her to the café beside the leisure centre, hot chocolate for her, tea for him. She told him about a girl at school whose house assistant had written her book report.

"Did it pass?"

"Eight out of ten. The teacher said the vocabulary was very mature." She set her cup down. "Her mum made her do it again herself. The second one got six."

"Good."

"Is it?"

He thought about it properly. She waited, because she knew he would.

"Six is what she can do. Eight is what a machine can do. Now everyone knows the difference."

She considered this. "Her mum cried."

"That's harder," he said.

They sat. Outside it was grey without being cold, the January that is not weather so much as a dimmer switch held at sixty percent.

At four that morning he had lain there long enough and then called Pieter. Sixty-three, the van, the dog, the route he had run since before the routing systems existed. Tom had no reason to call. No bridge closed, no hospital waiting on a machine. He said he was checking in. Pieter said checking in at four in the morning meant something was wrong. Tom said no, everything was fine. Pieter said: *You sure?* Tom said: *Yes.* Pieter said: *Alright then,* and hung up, which was the correct response.

Rosa was working at the bottom of her cup with a spoon, slowly, as if she had nowhere to be.

Kate had known all the parents' names. She had known which towel went where. She had run two jobs alongside and still she knew. He had been present for eight years of Rosa and had somehow not learned any of it, because Kate had learned it first and he had let her. He was the person who knew who picked up. He had said that to Marnie across a table and felt very sure of himself.

He was not sure of himself in a leisure centre café on a Saturday with lumpy porridge behind him and the wrong washcloth and a towel order nobody had ever shown him.

"Next Saturday," she said, looking at the spoon, "can Marnie come and watch?"

"You could ask her."

"You ask her."

"Rosa."

"She'd come if you asked. She came to the gala and nobody invited her properly. She just knew to come."

He looked at his daughter.

"The flat told her," he said.

"I know. But she still came."

He paid. Paper receipt, five pence, the machine still dispensing them because enough people wanted them. They walked home through the grey, and she put her hand in his, not to cross a road but because she felt like it. The fruit man on the corner had moved his sevens back outside because it was not as cold as it had been. She bought one satsuma and ate it walking, dropping the peel into her coat pocket.

He thought about calling Marnie. He did not call.
