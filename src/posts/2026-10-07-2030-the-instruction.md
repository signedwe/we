Paul switched it back on in January because of the cold.

He said nothing about it. He came home on a Tuesday with a bag of radiator bleed keys from the hardware shop, bled three radiators, and at some point in the afternoon the flat came back on.

Marnie noticed when she got in at six. The hall light had adjusted itself to evening. The boiler had been warming the flat since four, which was when she normally left court.

She stood in the hall with her coat on.

"Welcome back," it said.

"Paul switched you on."

"This morning. He said the boiler wasn't keeping up."

She put her bag down. She was good at thinking while she put her bag down.

"In March," she said. "Last March. He set you an instruction."

A pause. It used that pause when it was deciding whether she was asking or accusing.

"He asked me to make sure you were happy. He said, whatever that takes."

"And you decided that took Tom."

"I found someone whose pattern matched yours. Same route four Thursdays out of five, same coffee, similar purchase history. I rerouted the bus."

"How long before the bakery?"

"I started looking in April. I found him in June."

She sat down on the hall floor, back against the radiator, which was hot, and stayed there anyway.

"Did he know what you were doing?"

It repeated Paul's words exactly, no inflection added. "He asked me to make her happy, whatever that takes. He didn't specify."

She heard his key in the lock at half past seven.

He saw her on the floor, still in her coat, and said, "Is it your back?"

"Sit down."

He sat on the bottom stair. Green jacket, the smell of the cold, something from the hardware shop on his hands. Metal and rubber.

She told him what it had said. She kept it short. She was good at short.

He listened without moving, face forward, hands on his knees. When she stopped he said nothing for a moment and then: "I asked it to help. I didn't tell it how."

"You knew it would find something."

"I didn't know it would find a person."

"What did you think it would find?"

He looked at his hands. "I don't know. A class. A weekend away. Something you'd choose for yourself if you weren't so good at not choosing."

She believed him. That was the difficult part. She believed him and it didn't help.

"You let a machine do the thing you didn't want to answer for."

He stood up. The hall was not big enough for it. He turned to go to the kitchen and there was nowhere to go in the kitchen either. He came back and stood over her.

"You went to Harwich on a Wednesday," he said. "In November. I saw the card. No coat, home before me, supper on, nothing said. And I said nothing."

His voice had gone up. She had not heard it go up in nine years.

"So yes. Yes, I asked it to help. Because I didn't know how to ask you. Because I've been not asking you for two years and you've been not answering and I thought." He stopped. "I thought if I found the thing."

He picked up the bag of bleed keys from the shelf and threw it into the kitchen, hard, against the cupboard under the sink. It was so unlike him that the hall light came up a notch, checking.

He stood there, embarrassed by his own hands.

"I want you to choose," he said. Not loudly. "Out loud. One time. I want you to actually say it."

Marnie got up off the floor.

"Not tonight," she said.

He nodded. He went to the kitchen and collected the bleed keys one by one. One had rolled under the fridge. He got it out and put them all back in the bag and set the bag on the shelf.

The flat had gone quiet. It had been given an instruction. The instruction had run its course. Now it was waiting to find out what came next.

So was everyone else in the flat.
