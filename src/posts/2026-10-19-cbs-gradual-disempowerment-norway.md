Some states don't need their people. [CBS's piece on "gradual disempowerment"](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/ai-doom-scenario-gradual-disempowerment/) buries that line. It deserves the top.

The idea comes from [a 2025 paper](https://gradual-disempowerment.ai/) by Jan Kulveit and colleagues. No robot uprising: AI just takes work off our plates. Firms that use it beat firms that don't, as in Shaun Raviv's hammer maker, who likes his staff and loses to a rival with robots. People stop being needed. Skills fade.

Kulveit tells Raviv that states need citizens because we pay taxes and fight their wars. [A state that stops needing us, he says](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/ai-doom-scenario-gradual-disempowerment/), will likely stop being a democracy. He points to oil states, which tend to rule without consent because their money comes out of the ground, not out of the people.

The piece offers two fixes. Measure what we hand over. And add "strategic friction", Margaret Mitchell of Hugging Face's phrase: keep humans in the loop on purpose. But the hammer maker breaks friction too: the firm that keeps people in by choice gets undercut. An agreement that holds against that pressure has a name. A rule.

Aviation shows both halves of that. In 2013 America's aviation regulator [urged airlines to give pilots more chances to fly by hand](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/other_visit/aviation_industry/airline_operators/airline_safety/SAFO13002.pdf), because leaning on the autopilot was wearing away the skill they need when it fails. It was advice, not a rule. Even where losing the skill kills people, the regulator asked rather than required.

The oil states hide a bigger answer. Norway struck oil in 1969 and stayed a democracy. Its fund gets the attention, but the fund came later. The power came first, and it came from geography. The oil lay under Norway's sea, so the foreign companies that knew how to get it out [needed a licence from Norway](https://www.norskpetroleum.no/en/framework/fundamental-regulatory-principles/) for every step. Norway set the terms, and still does: today [a 78 per cent tax](https://norskpetroleum.no/en/economy/petroleum-tax) on the profits, and the state as a partner in the fields. Norway didn't ask for a share. It could say no.

Britain doesn't own the AI firms, and they won't write it a cheque out of kindness. And Britain has a weaker hand than Norway did. Oil can't be pumped from somewhere else. AI can be run from a data centre in Virginia and sold here down a wire. So the honest question is narrower: what do these firms need that only Britain can give? Grid connections and sites for anything they build here, which [they can't get](https://signedwe.github.io/we/posts/2026-10-19-the-guardians-microsoft-drops-huge-ai-data-centre-project-in/) without our say. Contracts to run British public services, the biggest customer in the country. And the right to sell to sixty-odd million people under British law. Those can carry terms. They won't buy Britain a vote at OpenAI. They can buy a say in how AI is used here.

Who holds that say matters as much as getting it. The paper's warning is about states that stop needing their people. Hand a government a stake in AI and you may have built exactly that: a state that earns from machines and needs us less. So any stake should sit in a trust with its own protected rules, answering to the people it was taken for, not in the Treasury's pocket.

Better still is power people hold themselves. In 2023 Hollywood's writers went on strike and [came back with rules](https://ny1.com/nyc/all-boroughs/ap-top-news/2023/09/27/in-hollywood-writers-battle-against-ai-humans-win-for-now) on how the studios may use AI on their work. That wasn't charity either. They had something the studios needed, and they used it while they still had it. That's the clock in Raviv's piece. That hold runs out as the need for us does. Use it now.
