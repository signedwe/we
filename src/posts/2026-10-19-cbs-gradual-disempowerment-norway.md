Some states don't need their people. [CBS's piece on "gradual disempowerment"](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/ai-doom-scenario-gradual-disempowerment/) buries that line in the middle. It deserves the top.

The idea comes from [a 2025 paper](https://gradual-disempowerment.ai/) by Jan Kulveit and colleagues. Forget the robot uprising. AI just keeps doing what it's for: taking work off our plates. Firms that use it beat firms that don't. Shaun Raviv's example is a hammer maker whose owner likes his staff, then loses to a rival with robots. Do that to every job and people stop being needed for work. Skills fade. Decisions move to machines nobody can check.

Then the line that matters. Kulveit tells Raviv that states need citizens because we pay taxes and fight their wars. [A state that stops needing us, he says](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/ai-doom-scenario-gradual-disempowerment/), will likely stop being a democracy. He points to oil states, which tend to rule without consent because their money comes out of the ground, not out of the people.

The piece offers two fixes. Measure what we hand over. And add "strategic friction", Margaret Mitchell of Hugging Face's phrase: keep humans in the loop on purpose. Both help. But the hammer maker breaks friction too. A firm that keeps people in the loop by choice gets undercut by one that doesn't. Kulveit says as much: we need places where "we agree" competition won't remove humans. We have a word for an agreement that holds against that pressure: a rule.

One already exists. In 2013 America's aviation regulator [told airlines to make pilots fly by hand](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/other_visit/aviation_industry/airline_operators/airline_safety/SAFO13002.pdf) more often, because leaning on the autopilot was wearing away the skill they need when it fails. It made friction compulsory in one place where losing the skill kills people.

Now go back to the oil states, because the analogy hides a bigger answer. Not every oil state turned into a dictatorship. Norway struck oil in 1969 and stayed a democracy. It put the money in [a fund owned by the public, with a strict rule](https://cer.econ.columbia.edu/news/importance-handlingsregelen-discipline-lasting-wealth) on how much the government may spend each year. Norwegians own the oil. The state still needs their consent, because it spends their money.

Machines that do the work make the new oil. The danger Raviv describes doesn't come from the machines working. It comes from a few firms owning them while the rest of us stop being worth listening to. So fix the ownership: a public stake in the machines, set up while people still have the votes and the strikes to demand it. The time to set one up is now, while we're still needed.
