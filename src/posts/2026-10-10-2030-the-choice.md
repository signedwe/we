It was a Tuesday in January, cold enough that the fruit man had moved his sevens inside.

She went to the bakery at half past eight, before the rush, and sat at the window with a flat white and a cinnamon bun she ate entirely. She had called the court already and said she was sick. The coordinator said get well soon in the voice you use when someone is inconvenient.

At nine she called Tom's landline.

He picked up on the second ring.

"It's Marnie."

"I know."

"I'm at the bakery."

A silence. Not a long one.

"Fifteen minutes," he said. "I've got R. with me."

"Bring her."

R. arrived still in her school coat, wrong buttons, whale bag on one shoulder. She looked at Marnie, looked at the window, said: "Can I have the end of your bun?"

"It's all ends. I ate the middle."

She took the plate and sat, and for a minute nobody spoke because R. was eating and you didn't interrupt R. eating.

Then R. said, with her mouth full: "Are you coming to live with us?"

Tom said her name.

"It's a reasonable question."

"It is," Marnie said. "No. Not that."

R. nodded and finished the bun and looked at the pigeon that had appeared on the pavement outside.

"*Due*," she said. The pigeon ignored her.

Tom looked at Marnie. She looked back.

"I called Paul this morning," she said. "Before I called you. I told him I was going to come here. He said okay." She picked up her cup. "He didn't say it like it was okay. He said it like someone who had been ready for something and was still not ready for it."

Tom turned his cup a quarter-turn on the saucer.

"I'm not asking you for anything today. I'm not arriving on your doorstep. I'm saying the sentence I've been not saying for eleven years, because I told Paul this morning and he deserves to have heard it second, not thirty-second."

The girl at the counter dropped something metal. R. looked up and looked back at the pigeon.

"The ferry," Marnie said. "I went back fifty-one Fridays. I know you went back twice. We missed each other by a week. I've done what I'm going to do with that, and what I'm doing is telling you. That's all of it."

Tom said nothing for a moment.

"And Paul."

"Paul knows. Since October. He put the receipt back on the same creases."

He almost laughed.

"He called your number," Marnie said. "From the receipt. You said wrong number and he hung up."

Tom looked at the window.

"He told me," she said. "At the concert. He tells you things. You both do that."

"I went to see him."

"I know. He told me that too." She set her cup down. "He's better at this than either of us. I want you to know I know that."

R. had produced a small notebook from her coat pocket and was drawing the pigeon. She drew its feet first, which was the correct order.

Tom said: "So."

"So I'm going to go home and pack a bag and go to my sister's for a few days. Not because of you. Because Paul and I need to have the actual conversation and we can't have it in the flat."

"The flat," he said.

"Yes."

They sat with that for a moment, the two of them, the pigeon making a third.

"After that," she said, "I don't know. Honestly. I'm not giving you a number to put in your phone. I'm saying the sentence to your face and then I'm going."

R. held up the notebook. The pigeon was good. She'd given it a slightly disappointed expression.

"*Due*," Marnie said. "Not a seagull."

"I know. But it's got a seagull's attitude."

Marnie put her coat on. She left the exact cost of the coffee in coins on the counter because the five-pence receipt was still in her purse and some habits are stronger than sense.

At the door she stopped.

"The last line of the poem. The bird that keeps coming back to the tree. Does it stay because it wants to or because it can't help it?"

R. looked up from her drawing.

"Both," she said. "That's the whole point of it."

Outside it had started to rain, the flat grey rain of January that makes London look like itself. Marnie walked to the station without hurrying, and the rain did what rain does.
