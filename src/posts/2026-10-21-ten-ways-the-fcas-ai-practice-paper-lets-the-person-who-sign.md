By end of 2028, a named senior manager will be sanctioned by the FCA for an AI failure their own firm's paper trail shows they approved. The [good and poor practice paper](https://www.regulationtomorrow.com/2026/10/fca-good-and-poor-practice-ai-in-financial-services-good-practice-and-areas-for-improvement/) published 19 October makes that more likely, not less. It tells firms exactly what to file. It says nothing about what to understand.

Here are the ten ways it teaches you to prove you were in charge without being in charge.

**10. Document the training.**
Firms that passed gave senior managers targeted training so they could understand AI risks. The training certificate is what the FCA can see. Whether anything landed is not in the file.

**9. Assign a name before the system goes live.**
Good practice means naming who answers for the AI from the design stage onward. Name someone early enough and they were there before anyone knew what the thing would do.

**8. Run an ethics panel before launch.**
One firm [set up a panel](https://www.regulationtomorrow.com/2026/10/fca-good-and-poor-practice-ai-in-financial-services-good-practice-and-areas-for-improvement/) to review AI use cases before deployment. The panel met. The panel approved. The meeting is now the proof.

**7. Keep a live list of warning signals in the chatbot.**
This one actually works. One firm updated its chatbot with signs that a customer might need a human. The FCA cited it as good practice. It is the only item on this list that protects a real person.

**6. Set a threshold for escalation.**
Clear escalation routes, with formal triggers to a named body. A trigger is a number on a spreadsheet. Whether anyone could say why that number was right is not something the paper trail holds.

**5. Make the sign-off multidisciplinary.**
Technical owner, research owner, business owner, senior person on top. In practice: the technical team built it, the business team wanted it, the senior person read the summary slide of what both had agreed.

**4. Make sure the senior person received the information.**
Not that they understood it. Not that they pushed back. That they received it, in a documented process, so they could make an informed decision. A full inbox is not a decision.

**3. Don't overstate what the model can do.**
[The FCA found](https://healthcareandprotection.com/fca-wants-ai-services-to-add-warning-on-financial-advice-and-finds-firms-overstating-accuracy-of-models/) firms overstating model accuracy to customers. Good practice is not doing this. The paper trail cannot show what the person who approved the copy believed when they clicked send.

**2. Add sign-off after launch, but quickly.**
Poor practice is adding oversight only after deployment. The implication: add it soon after and you are clear. The system ran. Something was noticed. The paperwork arrived. Yellow card.

**1. Produce a name, a certificate, a risk register and an escalation log.**
[The FCA says](https://www.traverssmith.com/knowledge/knowledge-container/uk-fca-guidance-on-ai-in-financial-services-good-practices-and-areas-for-improvement) it will expect firms to explain who has day-to-day oversight. The test is whether you can explain it. Not whether whoever you name understood what the model was doing. A firm with all four documents satisfies the paper, even if nobody in the chain ever did.

The paper itself is careful and specific. Items seven through nine are genuinely useful. The trouble is that the one thing it cannot measure — whether a named person understood, disagreed, or chose — is the thing the Senior Managers regime was designed to require.

The FCA built a way of checking who can be pointed at. Pointed at and responsible are not the same thing. [Only 40% of consumers](https://www.moneymarketing.co.uk/news/fca-calls-for-disclaimers-on-ai-generated-financial-advice/) even know they have no recourse when a general-purpose AI gives them bad financial advice. The paper protects the firm. Nobody wrote the one that protects the customer.
