D. read the report on the train from Meadowhall. By the time she reached the school gates, she knew her son J. was in the 67th percentile for analytical reasoning, had missed three homework deadlines in the autumn term, and showed "emerging prosocial confidence". She assumed that meant he'd stopped hiding at lunch.

The meeting was at 7:15. She was early. The plastic chair in the corridor outside the maths block was the same plastic chair that had been there in 2019, when she'd sat in one for her own parents' evenings as a child, in a different school, in a different city. There is apparently one manufacturer.

Mr A. had the same report on his screen. He'd read it too. The fifteen minutes set aside to transfer information that no longer needed transferring stretched ahead of them both.

This is what nobody has written down about parents' evenings in 2031: the awkward bit used to be the gap between what the report said and what the teacher meant. Now the report says exactly what the school measured, pulled from months of logged behaviour, and the gap has moved.

Mr A. said J. was making good progress. D. nodded. The screen showed the same numbers she'd read on the train.

Then he said: "He helped a kid in 8C find his bag last week. Stayed late. The kid's new." He paused. "That's not in the system."

---

By 2031, the fifteen-minute slot still exists. Schools have tried to kill it since the nineties. It survives because nothing else gives a parent the experience of being looked at by someone who has also looked at their child. The information is free now. The look isn't.

What changed is what the teacher spends those fifteen minutes *not* saying. The system flags underperformance before a parent asks. It catches the missed homeworks, the September dip, the seating-plan switch that went wrong. A teacher who used to spend twelve of those minutes on facts now has twelve minutes of whatever they noticed that the system didn't.

Some teachers are very good at that. Some aren't, and the system's accuracy makes the difference more visible than it used to be.

The argument people are having is whether the AI summary is better or worse than what the teacher would have said. It's probably more accurate. That isn't the same thing.

D. walked home in the dark. J. was still up. She told him about the bag.

"How did they know that?" he said.

"They didn't," she said. "Mr A. did."
