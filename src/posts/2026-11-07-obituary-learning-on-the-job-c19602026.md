The entry-level job had one unspoken promise, and it kept it for sixty years. You didn't need to arrive knowing the tools. You'd learn them in the first three months from someone who'd been doing it longer.

That arrangement ended this week.

[AI mentions in UK entry-level job postings nearly tripled since the start of 2025](https://www.japantimes.co.jp/business/2026/11/05/tech/ai-skills-uk-youth-job-market/), according to Adzuna figures reported by Bloomberg. One in six vacancies in banking, legal work and software now names AI skills specifically, double the rate a year ago. [Almost a third of employers say AI has raised experience requirements for their entry-level roles](https://www.ziprecruiter-research.org/economic-insights-research/ai-employer-report-2026), before anyone has had the chance to earn that experience.

By the end of 2027, at least one major UK professional training scheme in law, accountancy or finance will formally require demonstrated AI proficiency as an entry condition, written into the contract, listed alongside GCSEs. That's when the promise below will have been officially cancelled, not just quietly shelved.

**Born**: 1960s, in the expansion of graduate recruitment. The big professions decided a good degree was enough evidence that someone could be trained, and that the tools of the trade travelled by proximity. Sit next to someone who knows, and in three months you will know. The desk was the classroom. The arrangement assumed the tools were stable enough for that to work.

It survived every new technology since. Spreadsheets. Word processors. Email. Each time, the junior hire was given a few days to locate the relevant buttons, and then left to it.

**Life**: It did real work. It handed the wrong people a chance. The first in the family to hold a desk job, who didn't know anyone who could explain in advance what the tools were called. The humanities graduate who wanted to do something, didn't know what. It said: come curious, and we'll cover the rest.

Its finest hour was probably the late 1990s, when a law firm could hire an English literature graduate and have them running the case management system inside a fortnight. Nobody made a speech about it.

**Decline**: The job description changed faster than the advice about it. The careers adviser who told students in 2023 to get work experience, polish the CV, and show enthusiasm for the sector was giving good advice. By 2025 the postings had shifted. By this week they'd shifted enough to count.

A third of employers now say AI has pushed up what they expect from someone walking in for their first day. The person who followed the 2023 advice is standing at a door that moved while they were walking toward it. Nobody sent them a note.

The objection is real: [some of those AI mentions are job-description inflation, buzzwords bolted on without a pay rise or a genuine test at interview](https://careeraheadonline.com/ai-driven-job-descriptions-are-inflating-skills-without-the-pay-or-capability/). That's true. It makes no difference to the person who didn't bolt on the buzzword.

**Survivors**: The career services offices at every UK university. Still open. Leaflets still warm.

**Funeral arrangements**: Employers say they're redesigning entry roles around checking AI output. That requires knowing what good output looks like. That requires practice on real work. That requires someone inside a firm who assigns that work. The arrangement that died was itself the answer to that problem. Nobody has named what replaces it.
