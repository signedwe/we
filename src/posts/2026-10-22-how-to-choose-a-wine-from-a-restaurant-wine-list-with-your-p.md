The waiter is standing there. The list is eleven pages long and partly in French. Everyone at the table has looked at you, because you are the one who once said you liked wine.

So you do what you always do. You skip the cheapest bottle, because that would be embarrassing, and order the second cheapest red.

Good news: [a study of London restaurant lists](https://www.decanter.com/wine-news/second-cheapest-wine-restaurant-study-457425) found the second cheapest isn't the rip-off everyone says it is. Better news: you can do much better than that, in about two minutes, without anyone noticing.

**What you'll have at the end:** a bottle that suits the food and the budget, a sentence to say to the waiter, and the faint air of someone who knows.

**What you need:** a phone and Claude, ChatGPT or Gemini. The free tier is fine, and [Claude reads photos](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/8241126-upload-files-to-claude). The free app [Vivino](https://www.vivino.com/en/app) can also scan a wine list.

**Step 1.** Photograph the page of the list in your price range. Flat, good light, the prices in shot. Two photos if the reds and whites are on different pages.

**Step 2.** Upload it and type this:

*"Here's a restaurant wine list. We're eating [what you ordered]. Our budget is up to £[number]. Pick three bottles from this list only: a safe one, a good-value one from a less famous region, and a wildcard. One line on each, and tell me how to pronounce them."*

The "from this list only" matters. Without it, the machine will happily recommend a wonderful wine the restaurant doesn't sell.

**Step 3.** Ask the follow-up nobody asks out loud:

*"Tell me which of these is likely to be the best value for what it costs here."*

Restaurant prices are often [three to four times shop prices](https://www.decanter.com/features/get-one-for-the-price-of-four-247591/). The mark-up isn't even across a list. The machine can't see the restaurant's costs, but it can tell you which bottle usually sells for more in shops than its neighbours on the page.

**Step 4.** Look for the odd corners. For whites, wine people [point diners towards](https://www.foodism.co.uk/how-to-choose-wine-in-a-restaurant/) the Loire, Germany, Greece, northwest Spain and South Africa. For reds, Portugal, the Languedoc, southern Italy and Chile. That's where the value tends to hide, and where your good-value pick will often come from.

**Step 5.** Put the phone away and talk to a human. If there's a sommelier, [point at a price on the list](https://www.decanter.com/learn/what-to-say-to-a-sommelier-budget-357232/) and say "something like this". Nobody at the table hears the number. Then mention the machine's good-value pick and ask what they think. You now sound like someone with an opinion.

*"We're thinking about this one, but what would you drink with the lamb at around this price?"*

**Where it goes wrong.** The photo is blurry and the machine invents a bottle. Check its picks are actually on the page. Vintages change and lists go out of date. And the AI hasn't tasted anything; the sommelier has.

**One rule.** You can send back a bottle that's faulty, corked or off. You [can't send it back because you don't like it](https://www.yorkshirepost.co.uk/lifestyle/food-and-drink/expert-reveals-how-you-should-actually-taste-wine-4829047). Choose carefully, and that won't come up.

**What it costs.** Nothing beyond the wine. The free tiers read photos.

**How long.** Two minutes, most of them spent photographing the list.
