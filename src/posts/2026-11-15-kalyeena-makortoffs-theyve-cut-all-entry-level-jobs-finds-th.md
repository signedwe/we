Amine drives past the beach on his way to the careers office. He has a degree from Bournemouth University. He can't find a job.

The Guardian found him this week at the edge of a finance quarter built on a former farm, a few minutes from the seafront. [JP Morgan runs 5,300 people there](https://tradersunion.com/news/institutions/show/4225273-bournemouth-finance-ai-job-risk/), pushing more than a trillion dollars through the system every day. Barclays operates there. Standard Chartered. Insurance firms Amine won't name, because he worked for one until it cut its entry-level intake and blamed AI.

The Guardian calls Bournemouth "an unlikely seaside town" for all this. That framing deserves a push.

Big Bang deregulation in the 1980s flooded the City with back-office demand. London property prices couldn't hold it. Banks started moving processing work outward, to places where humans came cheaper. Bournemouth caught a lot of it. The finance quarter followed the money, and the money followed the wage gap. Cost arbitrage built this town's economy. Nothing else did.

AI takes that logic one step further. The question that sent those jobs to Bournemouth — where do we put the cheap humans? — now gets a different answer. You remove the humans. Same motive, different destination.

[Two-thirds of the UK's 2.5 million financial and professional services workers](https://finwire.io/news/business-news/theyve-cut-all-entry-level-jobs-unlikely-seaside-town-on-frontline-of-ai-threat) already sit outside London, TheCityUK says. Bournemouth caught some of that dispersal. No second Bournemouth catches what comes next, because nothing disperses when the work stops needing humans at all.

A Barclays worker told the Guardian, anonymously, that skills feel beside the point now. Overhead reduction drives the cuts, not performance. That worker understands the motive. The Hollywood writers' strike in 2023 showed that people who sit at the point where content gets made can still win terms before the replacement locks in. Finance back-office workers don't sit at that point. Banks designed these roles to move. Standardised, transferable, relocatable. That portability made Bournemouth possible. It also makes the exits quick.

What Amine's generation loses isn't a rung. Every employer in the finance quarter cuts its graduate intake, saves money this year, and drains the pool from which all of them will want to promote in a decade. No single body watches that happen. No agreed floor stops it. Each firm acts rationally. Together they hollow out the pipeline they all depend on.

Amine tells the Guardian he's not sure where to go. JP Morgan's local Conservative councillor told the Guardian he'd heard nothing of cuts at the Littledown campus. Both things can be true. [JP Morgan itself told the Guardian](https://www.aol.co.uk/articles/ve-cut-entry-level-jobs-060011000.html) that AI helps its people do more. The cuts Amine describes happened elsewhere in the quarter, at firms nobody named. The quarter keeps processing its trillion dollars a day. Amine keeps driving past the beach.
