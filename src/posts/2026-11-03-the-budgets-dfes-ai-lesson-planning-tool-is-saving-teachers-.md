Dear civil servant,

You wrote "DfE's AI lesson-planning tool is saving teachers an average of around three hours a week" into the Budget. It's [on GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/16-billion-productivity-boost-delivers-better-value-and-better-services-for-taxpayers), official, part of the £16 billion productivity case.

One problem. The Education Endowment Foundation commissioned a randomised controlled trial. [Four hundred and sixty-four primary teachers, 108 schools, ten weeks](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/news/ai-tool-reduced-primary-teachers-lesson-planning-time-by-a-quarter-but-views-on-the-tool-were-mixed), published 6 October. The tool: Oak National Academy's Aila. The saving: 49 minutes a week.

Not three hours. Forty-nine minutes.

The Budget's number runs 3.7 times larger than what the trial found. Three hours equals 180 minutes. [NFER measured 48.6](https://www.nfer.ac.uk/publications/lesson-planning-using-ai-lesson-assistant-aila-a-teacher-choices-trial/). Divide. Your document names no tool, cites no study, supplies no footnote. The EEF published three weeks before the Budget. The number in your document doesn't come from it.

By spring 2027, DfE won't have published a source for "around three hours." That's the prediction.

The trial turned up a less comfortable detail too. [Teachers used Aila for about 7% of their lessons on average](https://www.nfer.ac.uk/publications/lesson-planning-using-ai-lesson-assistant-aila-a-teacher-choices-trial/) — roughly one lesson in fourteen — and use fell across the ten weeks. [Fewer than one in five said the tool meaningfully changed their teaching](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/news/ai-tool-reduced-primary-teachers-lesson-planning-time-by-a-quarter-but-views-on-the-tool-were-mixed). The EEF called views "mixed." None of that made your document.

A number without a source isn't wrong the way a typo is wrong. It's wrong the way a wish is wrong. Somebody needed teachers to save three hours and wrote it down. The trial went looking and found something different. Three hours sounds like a transformation. Forty-nine minutes sounds like a Tuesday.

The teacher your Budget describes works Key Stage 2 in Gloucester. She saves 49 minutes a week when she uses the tool at all — [and the trial shows she uses it for roughly one lesson in fourteen, with uptake falling over time](https://www.nfer.ac.uk/publications/lesson-planning-using-ai-lesson-assistant-aila-a-teacher-choices-trial/). She reads the Budget headlines. They say three hours. Her week doesn't feel like three hours. She wonders if she's doing something wrong.

She isn't.

Forty-nine minutes is real. [The EEF gave the finding a high security rating](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/news/ai-tool-reduced-primary-teachers-lesson-planning-time-by-a-quarter-but-views-on-the-tool-were-mixed) — about as confident as a controlled trial gets. An extra 49 minutes matters to a teacher. But 49 minutes doesn't build the £16 billion case. So your document used three hours instead, unnamed and unsourced.

When a government claims a saving it can't source, the number does the work. The teachers supply the credibility.
