Rosa opened the door before Marnie knocked.

"I heard your shoes," she said.

The flat smelled of toast and ironing. Tom came out of the back room holding the iron, and put it down by the kettle.

"She's been ready since Monday," he said.

Rosa sat down across from Marnie with a notebook. DANISH across the top of the first page, in capitals.

Tom made tea and kept his back to them.

"*Måge*," Rosa said. "I know that one. What's pigeon?"

"*Due*. Say it like something has mildly let you down."

Rosa tried it twice, getting the flatness right, and aimed it at the window where no pigeon was.

"*Due*," she said, with feeling. "Pieter."

They did animals. She wanted the rude word for dog. Marnie said she had to earn it. Rosa accepted this with the patience of someone who intended to earn it quickly.

"*Hund*," Marnie said.

Rosa got it wrong, then right.

"When I'm in Copenhagen," Rosa said, not looking up, "I can say all of these at the zoo."

Tom put the teapot down.

"What zoo?"

"The Copenhagen one. Mum says there's a good one."

"When did Mum say that?"

"When she said about Copenhagen." Rosa drew a seagull beside *måge*, mostly beak. "From January. She's got a job. I go in February for the holidays." She looked up. "Marnie's teaching me so I can talk when I get off the plane."

Tom turned to Marnie.

She picked up her tea, which was a mistake, because it gave her hands something to do and her face nothing.

"Kate asked me on Friday," she said. "After the assembly."

"Friday."

"She said she'd tell you."

"She would say that." He did not raise his voice. He went to the window and stood with his hands in his pockets, and Marnie, who had once thought his stillness was gentleness, saw it now for what it partly was: a man waiting for the thing to be over so that nobody could say he had made a scene.

"*Spurv*," Marnie said, to Rosa. "Sparrow. For when the zoo disappoints you."

Rosa wrote it, spelled it wrong, fixed it.

Then she put the pencil down, got off her chair, went to the window, and took her father's hand out of his pocket. She held it, the way you hold an adult's hand to cross a road.

"*Lort*," she said, to the street.

"You weren't supposed to know that one yet," said Marnie.

"Mum taught me. She said it was practical." Rosa did not let go of Tom's hand. "Can we do animals that aren't at the zoo?"

Tom laughed, short and real, and something in him gave way.

At the door, with Rosa back at the table drawing a sparrow, he said it quietly.

"She told you first. And you let me hear it from an eight-year-old."

"Kate said she'd tell you."

"Kate says a lot of things." He held the door open. "Next Tuesday. Come early. I want to know what else everyone's decided."
