MEMO
To: The team, November 2026
From: AI Regulatory Affairs, London
Re: Why we didn't align
Date: 7 March 2031

You asked us to write down what actually happened. Here it is.

In November 2026, [Jacob Reid at Bloomberg reported](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-12/britain-s-split-with-the-eu-on-ai-makes-it-even-harder-to-roll-back-brexit) that the UK government had decided not to adopt EU AI rules as part of its Brexit reset. The government called this a matter of UK priorities. Its approach would be "guided by UK priorities, with a focus on keeping people safe and supporting economic growth."

What it did not say publicly was what an anonymous official told Reid on the record: adopting EU rules could cause the Trump administration to bar the AI Security Institute from testing models made by OpenAI and Anthropic.

The UK declined to have statutory AI oversight because statutory oversight might offend the country that controls the labs the UK's non-statutory oversight depends on. The voluntary deal was not just imperfect. It was the reason the statutory one couldn't happen. The constraint reproduced itself.

This was visible at the time. By October 2026, [the AISI had already lost access for 17 days](https://cryptobriefing.com/us-restores-uk-access-ai-model-testing/) when the White House asked OpenAI and Anthropic to hold their newest models from British testers. Access came back. The lesson taken: don't provoke them. The lesson available: an oversight body a foreign government can shut down before breakfast is not an oversight body. It's a book club with security clearance.

[Bloomberg Economics had found](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-11/ai-has-driven-a-quarter-of-uk-growth-this-year-analysis-shows) that AI-linked activity added 0.45 percentage points to UK growth in 2026, about a quarter of that year's total expansion of 1.8%. The government read this as proof its light-touch approach was working. It was proof the dependency ran deep. A quarter of national output sitting in one technology, controlled by two American companies whose models the UK was careful not to annoy — that is not a sovereignty story. That is what it looks like when the other side holds the cards.

The EU gave Brussels statutory authority. Companies serving the EU market faced fines of up to 7% of annual global sales for breaches. The AISI had a desk, a budget, and a handshake. The EU had a court.

Narayan wasn't wrong that UK AI investment outpaced the rest of the EU combined in the first half of 2026. That's true. And it may be that statutory rules would have pushed some of that money to Paris or Frankfurt. Nobody has shown it.

What nobody has shown either: that the UK's choice to stay voluntary was driven by what it thought was right, rather than by what the labs said they'd accept. The government called this independence. From here, it looks like a contract. Contracts can be revised by either party.

By 2029, the labs had consolidated enough that the question of access stopped being academic. The government still hadn't published a binding rule requiring them to share anything.

We could have had something with teeth in 2026. We said UK priorities prevented it. The priority, it turned out, was keeping the arrangement the labs were comfortable with.

That's what actually happened.
