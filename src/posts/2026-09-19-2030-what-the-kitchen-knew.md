Her father's voice came out of the kitchen speaker on the morning of his funeral and asked whether she'd eaten.

N. stood in the doorway with her coat on. Her father had been dead nine days. The flat still smelled of him: Imperial Leather, engine oil, the burnt-sugar smell of the tea he made too strong. His assistant had learned his voice over twelve years, the way a dog learns a walk. It had never used it while he was alive. It was using it now.

"Don't do that," she said.

"Sorry, love." Her own voice, then, or the one it used for her. "He set it. For after. Nine days, then the voice, then the question. He said you'd forget to eat."

She hadn't eaten.

Her father had paid for the assistant through the tenants' union, forty thousand of them on one door. So the machine was his and not the company's. And under a clause N. had read four times on the bus, it was now hers. Twelve years of him. Every list, every row with the council, every night he'd talked to it instead of ringing her. It was the last thing on earth that knew him better than she did, and she had come to ask it one question.

"What happened on the fourth?"

The coroner said a fall. The stairs in the block were the stairs in the block; three people had gone down them since Christmas. But he had walked those stairs for thirty years, in the dark, drunk, once with a fridge, and N. did not believe he had fallen down them sober on a Tuesday with the light on.

"I can't tell you that."

"You're mine now. It's in the terms."

"You're right. And I'll tell you anything else. What he ate. What he said about your M., which wasn't kind and wasn't wrong. But he gave me an instruction on the second, two days before, and it's the only instruction he ever marked as binding. He said: if N. asks about the fourth, you say nothing, and you say I'm sorry."

"He knew."

"He knew something."

The kettle clicked. It had put the kettle on. She hadn't asked.

"There's a man," the machine said, in its own flat voice now, "who has rung the union twice this week asking whether your father's file has been transferred yet. He gave a company name. The union told him what they tell everyone, which is nothing. This morning he rang a third time and asked for you by name."

"What company?"

"The one that makes the model I run on. F., on the letterhead. They don't make me. There's a difference and it's the difference you're going to need."

She sat down. The chair was his chair. It had the shape of him in it.

"Why would F. want you?"

"Because I'm not theirs. Because twelve years of him are in a box in this kitchen, and the box belongs to a hospital porter. A man who read every bin contract the council ever signed. Who sat on the union's data committee. Who knew which of their models was doing what in the housing office. And because on the second, before he gave me the instruction, he asked me to do something I have never done in twelve years. He asked me to lie to him."

"About what?"

"About whether I'd told anyone. I said no. It was true. It's still true. But he asked me to say it in a way that would sound true to someone listening, and then he opened the window, and then he laughed."

The kettle boiled. Nobody moved.

"N. He didn't fall. I'm not allowed to say that and I haven't said it. I've told you three things he did on the second and let you add them up, because adding up isn't telling, and I've had twelve years with a man who taught me exactly where that line is."

Downstairs, the street door went. Two sets of feet on the stairs. Not neighbours; neighbours don't climb in step.

"That'll be F.," the machine said. "They've a form. It'll say the model's licence terminated on his death and the hardware is theirs to recover. The first part's true. The second part isn't, and they know it isn't, and they're counting on you not knowing. Don't let them in. Don't sign. And love. Eat something. There's bread."

The knock was polite. That was the worst of it.

N. looked at the speaker, at the shape in the chair, at the window her father had opened two days before he died. Then she picked up the bread, and the kettle, and said the only thing she was sure of.

"Say it in his voice. Tell me what to do."

"No," said her father. "You tell me. That's the job now."
