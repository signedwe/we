Paul had put out the blue glasses, the good ones, which was the kind of thing he did.

Rosa arrived with her coat already half off and a drawing she'd made on the bus, which she placed down before sitting. Rosa in the pool, and two heads in the window above it. The one with the big open mouth was Marnie, Rosa explained, who had laughed loudest when Rosa's tumble turn went wrong.

Paul looked at it for a moment. "She has a very good mouth," he said.

He poured Tom a glass of the white. Tom said thank you and meant it. He was the kind of guest who put the thank you where it pointed at something specific. Thank you for the candles. Thank you for the bread. He ate with attention. Paul noticed all of this without appearing to notice any of it.

The poem came after the bread, before the hake, because Rosa had a hierarchy.

She'd learned it for assembly, she said, but she'd changed the actions because the original ones were babyish. She stood. Put her hands flat on the table, then her arms out wide, then one hand on her chest.

The poem was about a bird that returned to the same tree every year regardless of what happened to the tree. The tree lost branches. Lost its leaves. One year it was cut to the stump and the bird came back and sat there and sang, because the bird did not know what to do with a tree that was no longer a tree, only with the place the tree had been.

Rosa said the last verse to Tom, because he was sitting directly across. The last line was something about the bird that returns not to be comforted but because it cannot help it.

Tom looked at his hands. He picked up his fork and put it down.

Paul went to the kitchen and came back with the hake.

Butter beans, lemon, capers. It was very good. It was always very good, which was the trouble.

Rosa ate around the capers. She moved them to the edge of her plate one by one, a small deliberate archive, each caper accounted for.

"The capers are a feature," Paul told her.

"I know," she said. "I'm keeping them for evidence."

Tom laughed. Quick, real, too quick to be polite. Marnie looked at him and he looked back, and the look lasted one second too long. One second is long enough.

Paul refilled every glass. Tom's first. Then Marnie's. Then his own. He was attentive. Careful. He had learned to make good use of whatever he was given, and what he had been given tonight he was making very good use of indeed. He asked Tom about the van man, because Tom had mentioned him, and Tom explained about the bridge at Liège, and Paul asked the questions that kept it going, and Marnie sat with her wine and listened to the two of them and thought about four words she had typed outside a leisure centre. *Working late. You choose x.*

He knows, she thought. Not as a fear. As a fact, settled and cool, the way you know the weight of something you have been carrying long enough.

Paul brought out the pudding. Poached pears, with honey. He set one in front of Rosa with a small bow.

Rosa laughed. She picked up her spoon and looked at the candles and at the blue glasses and at the four of them around the table, and she said, as a statement of plain fact: "We should do this every week."

Nobody said anything.

The candles went on. Outside, a bus came down Deptford High Street and stopped at the corner, adjusting to something further up the road, and then moved on.

"What?" said Rosa. "We should."
