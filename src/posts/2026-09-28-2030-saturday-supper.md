Paul had put out the blue glasses, the good ones.

Rosa arrived with her coat half off and a drawing she'd made on the bus: Rosa in the pool, two heads in the window above it. The one with the big open mouth was Marnie, Rosa explained, who had laughed loudest when Rosa's tumble turn went wrong.

Paul studied it. "She has a very good mouth," he said, and stuck it to the fridge, over the shopping list, with the magnet he used for bills.

He poured Tom a glass of the white. Tom thanked him for the candles, for the bread, for the wine, a guest who put his thanks where they pointed at something. Paul noticed all of it without appearing to notice any of it.

The poem came after the bread and before the hake, because Rosa had a hierarchy. She stood. Hands flat on the table for the bark, arms wide for the branches, one hand on her chest for the singing. The bird came back to its tree every year, whatever happened to the tree, and the year it was cut to a stump the bird came back anyway and sang, because it could not help it.

She said the last line properly. She said it to Tom, who was sitting across from her. Tom looked at his hands.

Paul went to the kitchen and came back with the hake. Butter beans, lemon, capers. It was very good. It was always very good, which was the trouble.

Rosa moved her capers to the edge of the plate one by one.

"The capers are a feature," Paul told her.

"I know," she said. "I'm keeping them for evidence."

Tom laughed, too quick to be polite. Marnie looked at him and he looked back, and the look lasted one second too long. One second is long enough.

Paul refilled every glass. Tom's first, then Marnie's, then his own. He asked Tom about the van man and the bridge at Liège and asked the questions that kept him talking, and he was a little too eager, laughing too early at the bridge story, filling Tom's glass when it was still half full.

*He knows*, she thought. Not as a fear. As a fact, settled and cool.

Then Paul set down the bottle too hard and said, louder than he meant to: "I said Esbjerg the other day, about Marnie's crossing. Wrong. It was the Hook. She's told me a dozen times." He tried to smile at Tom and it didn't come off. "Same boat as you, then."

Nobody moved. The bottle had tipped Rosa's water over, and Paul went red and mopped it with his napkin and said sorry to Rosa three times, and she said it was only water, and he said he knew, he knew it was only water.

Paul brought out the pudding. Poached pears, with honey. He set one in front of Rosa with a small bow.

Rosa picked up her spoon and looked round at the candles and the blue glasses and the four of them, and said, as a statement of plain fact: "We should do this every week."

Nobody said anything.

"What?" said Rosa. "We should."

"We should," said Paul, and raised his glass to her, and drank, and was the only one who did.
