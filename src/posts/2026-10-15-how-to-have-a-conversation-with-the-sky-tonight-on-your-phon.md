Jamie pointed her phone at the gap between two rooftops and found out the bright one was Jupiter. Ten seconds. Then she opened YouTube and got a twenty-minute lecture she didn't ask for.

There's a better version.

**What you'll have at the end:** a real back-and-forth about what's above your head tonight, told in the version nobody teaches.

Why AI beats YouTube: the video gives you a lecture. The assistant answers the question you actually asked. When you say "I can see Orion's belt from my window in Leeds, tell me the Babylonian version, not the Greek one" — it gives you the Babylonian version. The video doesn't know you're in Leeds.

**Step one: find out what you're looking at. Five minutes.**

[Stellarium Mobile](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/stellarium-mobile-star-map/id1458716890) is free on iOS and Android. Point it at the sky. It names everything: stars, planets, constellations, the ISS if it's passing. [Sky Map](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.google.android.stardroid&hl=en_US) does the same on Android and is open source. A clear window works fine.

Write down three things you can see.

**Step two: open Claude or ChatGPT. Voice mode if it's cold.**

[Claude voice mode](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/11101966-use-voice-mode) is free on iOS and Android. ChatGPT voice mode is free too. You can lie in the dark and just talk.

Tell it what Stellarium showed you. Then ask for the version nobody teaches:

*"I can see Orion's belt right now. Tell me the story the Babylonians told about it. Not the Greek one. Make it three minutes long and dramatic."*

From there: ask for the Inuit version. Ask who the Maasai thought those three belt stars were. Ask it to tell the same story for a six-year-old. Ask what the Chinese called them. It adapts every time. A video doesn't.

**The thing nobody mentions.** [Orion alone has at least twenty distinct named cultural stories](https://encyclopedia.pub/entry/56320): Babylonian, Sumerian, Egyptian, Lakota Sioux, Aboriginal Australian, Inuit, Maasai, /Xam, Crow Nation, Chinese, Hindu, Wasco, Hebrew and more. [A search for "Orion mythology" returns mainly the Greek version](https://nightskytourist.com/orion/), sometimes Egyptian, occasionally Maori. Three of twenty. The other seventeen are sitting there waiting for a question.

My sum: twenty traditions identified across the sources below. Roughly three surface from a standard first-page search. The assistant gives you all twenty, in whatever order you want, in the dark, out loud.

**What it costs:** nothing. Both apps free. Voice mode on both assistants free.

**How long:** fifteen minutes to start. Longer, because you'll keep asking.

**Where it goes wrong:** Stellarium can drift if your phone compass is near metal. Step away from radiators and recalibrate. The AI narrates all twenty traditions with the same confidence. That confidence is not equally earned. For the Babylonian and Greek versions it draws on thousands of sources. For smaller Indigenous traditions it may have far less to go on. If a detail sounds extraordinary, ask it where the story comes from. This is a door, not a destination.

*Try this: "I can see [whatever Stellarium shows you]. Tell me three stories about it. One from the Southern Hemisphere, one from the Arctic, one from the Middle East. Short and strange."*

Jamie did this for forty minutes. She learnt nothing about Jupiter. She learnt that [the Inuit saw Orion's belt as three seal hunters](http://www.stellar-journeys.org/starmyths.htm), and she's still thinking about it.
