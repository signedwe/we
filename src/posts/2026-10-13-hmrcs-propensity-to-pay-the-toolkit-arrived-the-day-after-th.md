MEMO

TO: DSIT
FROM: First-tier Tribunal, Tax Chamber
RE: Your risk toolkit. September 2026.
DATE: March 2031

---

HMRC scored your business before anyone told you the score existed.

By the end of 2028, a case came before this chamber on that fact. We are writing to say what we found.

On 8 September 2026, DSIT [published a toolkit](https://www.aigl.blog/ai-risk-management-toolkit-guidance/) telling public bodies to be transparent about AI, to let people understand how decisions about them were made, and to build in a way to challenge those decisions.

On 9 September, HMRC published a short record describing a model called Business Propensity to Pay. [It scores about four million UK businesses every month](https://www.publictechnology.net/2026/09/15/economics-and-finance/hmrc-deploys-ai-to-categorise-businesses-by-propensity-to-pay-tax-debts/) on how likely they are to pay a tax debt. It decides whether the letter you get from the debt team is calm or aggressive. It has been running since before the toolkit existed.

The record lists no accuracy figure. No false-positive rate. No outside check. No way to dispute your score.

Four million businesses, twelve months: forty-eight million individual scores a year, none of them visible to the firm being scored.

A builder in Preston paid every quarter for six years. The model put her in the low-engagement group anyway. She got the hard letter. She asked why. HMRC said it could not share model outputs. Nothing in the toolkit said what she could do next, because the toolkit was written for the people deploying the model, not the people inside it.

That is the only sentence in this memo that matters: the toolkit was written for the people deploying the model, not the people inside it.

[The toolkit](https://www.aigl.blog/ai-risk-management-toolkit-guidance/) has five appetite levels, averse through hungry. They belong to the organisation running the model. The businesses being scored hold no level. They are the ground the appetite is expressed on.

Publishing a record that names a model is not the same as opening it. DSIT's document said: monitor systems across their whole life. HMRC's document said: here is a system. Neither said: here is what you do when it gets you wrong.

We note this for the record. The door was labelled. Nobody cut a hole in it.
