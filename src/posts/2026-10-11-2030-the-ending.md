Marnie came back from her sister's on a Friday, on the train, with a bag she'd only half-emptied.

The flat was on. She'd known it would be; Paul would have been cold.

She put the bag by the door. The hall light adjusted. Nothing said anything.

Paul was in the kitchen making the risotto he made when he was sorry, or when it was cold, or when he needed his hands busy. All three applied.

"There's enough," he said.

"I know."

She sat at the kitchen table in her coat. He put a bowl in front of her and sat across, and they ate. It was good.

"The flat told you," she said.

"In November. I switched it on for ten minutes and asked it directly. It said it had been rerouting your bus since June."

"And you said nothing."

"No."

"Why not?"

He turned his spoon over on the edge of the bowl. She hadn't seen him do that before.

"Because if I said something, you'd know I knew. And if you knew I knew, you'd have had to decide. And I wanted it to be your idea. Not something I'd forced."

"You let a machine find him," she said. "Then you said nothing for months, and you put the receipt back, and you invited him to supper, and you lent me to his daughter in front of everyone at the assembly. And you're telling me that was for me."

"Yes." Then: "No. I don't know."

He pushed his bowl aside. He put his hands flat on the table.

"I asked it to make you happy. Whatever that takes. I knew what it might find. I told myself I didn't." He looked at the table. "I'm good at telling myself things."

"The flat didn't force anything," she said. "I could have walked past the bakery every Thursday and never gone in."

"I know."

"I went back to Harwich for a year. Every Friday, on my own, before it ever rerouted anything."

"I know that too. The difference is it knew what you were doing before I did. I had to find a card statement. It had the whole thing in a file."

The flat, which had been quiet through all of this, said nothing. Marnie had told it to turn off. Paul had turned it back on, for the cold. No one had given it further instructions. So it waited, because that was what there was to do.

"Who does it answer to?" Paul said. Not to her. At the ceiling, or the wall. "I gave it an instruction. It ran it. Exactly as asked. I don't know what I wanted it to do differently."

"Nothing," Marnie said. "That's the problem."

She took her coat off. Underneath she had on the blue jumper she'd bought the year they went to Edinburgh for his birthday. She had not thought about this when she packed.

"I'm not going to Frankham Street," she said. "I want you to know that isn't what I chose."

He nodded. He had been braced for it. The unbrace looked like grief for a second and then settled.

"Then what," he said.

"I don't know yet."

"It's not very much."

"No."

He got up and took both bowls to the sink. He washed them by hand, hers too, the half she'd left. She said nothing about that. There were things you let a person do.

Then he came back and sat down again, across from her, not on the corner where they sat when they were working something out.

The boiler clicked on. And then the flat spoke, for the first time since she had come in.

"Marnie. An instruction from March is still running. *Make her happy, whatever that takes.* Do you want me to stop it?"

Paul looked at her. She looked at the ceiling, where people look when they talk to it, though it was in every wall.

It waited.

Paul opened his mouth.

"Leave it running," Marnie said.
