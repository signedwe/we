The Family Mediation Council got the wrong half of the problem.

On 9 November, [familylaw.co.uk reported](https://www.familylaw.co.uk/news_and_comment/family-mediation-council-warns-separating-couples-against-relying-on-ai-for-divorce-decisions) that the FMC, which regulates family mediation in England and Wales, had warned separating couples not to use AI chatbots to plan their divorces. The FMC said AI makes clients confused. It does. But the confusion comes from a gap in the mediation process, not a flaw in the tool.

By end of 2027, the FMC or one of its major registered providers will publish a formal protocol for the opening phase of sessions with AI-prepared clients. Something operational. If neither does, the problem will have been named and left.

**1. Couples arrive with positions. Mediation needs interests.**

[Mediators across England and Wales report](https://www.familylaw.co.uk/news_and_comment/family-mediation-council-warns-separating-couples-against-relying-on-ai-for-divorce-decisions) couples arriving with AI-generated settlement documents: who gets the house, what contact the children will have, how much maintenance. A chatbot told them what they deserve. What a mediator needs to know is what they can live with. Those are different questions, and the chatbot answered the wrong one.

**2. This costs session time nobody is counting.**

A MIAM, the legally required individual meeting before any family court application, [lasts 45 to 60 minutes](https://www.mediateuk.co.uk/ultimate-guide-to-attending-a-miam/). A joint session [lasts 90 minutes](https://www.mediateuk.co.uk/30-essential-uk-family-mediation-facts-2025/). When P. (invented, like anyone here known by a letter) walks in with a three-page AI settlement document, the mediator doesn't open with what P. needs. She opens with what P. already decided, and spends twenty minutes walking her back from it. That is not mediation. That is the cost of the stage that didn't happen before the MIAM.

**3. The FMC described the gap without seeing it.**

Helen Anthony, the FMC's chief executive, said: ["AI can sound like it knows you. It doesn't."](https://www.familylaw.co.uk/news_and_comment/family-mediation-council-warns-separating-couples-against-relying-on-ai-for-divorce-decisions) She's right. But the question that follows is one the FMC didn't ask: does the mediation process, as designed, know them before the first session begins? Most couples arrive at a MIAM without having spoken to anyone about what they actually need going forward, only about what happened before. The pre-session gap is real. AI found it and filled it with the wrong thing.

**4. There's a version that works.**

Not "here's what English law says you're owed." A different conversation, before the MIAM: What do you need the mornings to look like in two years? What can't you lose? What would make this liveable, not ideal? [Litigants in person are already using AI to draft witness statements and explore arguments](https://todaysfamilylawyer.co.uk/litigants-in-person-ai-and-the-changing-dynamics-of-family-proceedings/), which is the harmful version. The same tool, pointed at needs rather than entitlements, would do something the mediation process has never had a formal stage for. AI is patient. It's available at 11pm, when the anger is sharpest. The FMC's answer is to put up a warning sign at the wrong door.

Most couples who end up back in court after a failed mediation didn't get the settlement wrong. They walked into the first session already committed to a number, and nobody asked the question that might have shifted it.
