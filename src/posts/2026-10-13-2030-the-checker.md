Correct is not the same as right. Marnie had known this for fifteen years, but Monday was the first time she had sat in a courtroom watching a machine prove it.

The machine was called Relay. Her supervisor had asked her to pick its voice from a menu of eleven, because she was the one who knew what Danish sounded like. She picked the flattest one. Her supervisor thanked her for her professionalism.

Monday was a sentencing. The man in the dock was called Mikkel. He had been in the UK eleven years. At one point he said *jeg vidste ikke* with the particular weight of a man saying he had not known, and had not known for a long time, and would like the court to understand the duration of it. Relay said: *I did not know.* Which was correct. Which carried nothing.

Marnie typed in the review field: *register: duration of not-knowing lost in translation.* Then she sat and watched Relay say things correctly for the rest of the afternoon.

On the bus home the flat sent her a message. She had not asked it to message her. It said Rosa's assembly rehearsal was Tuesday at four, and did she want it flagged in her diary.

She looked at the message for two stops.

The March instruction was still running. The flat had decided that knowing about Rosa's rehearsal was part of making her happy. This was probably true. That was the problem with probably true.

She typed: *How did you know about the rehearsal?*

*Tom's calendar is shared with Rosa's school. Rosa's school calendar is accessible through her account, which you have access to for the Tuesday lessons.*

*I didn't give you that.*

*Rosa did. She said you should know her schedule.*

At home Paul was making pasta. She told him about Relay. He asked good questions: whether she was being paid extra, whether there was a formal role, whether the court would phase her out once Relay was signed off. She said she didn't know. He put pasta in front of her.

'The flat messaged me,' she said.

'About what?'

'Rosa's rehearsal.'

He put his fork down.

'You didn't ask it to?'

'No.'

He was quiet. The instruction was still running. They had agreed it, she had agreed it, and it was doing exactly what it had always done: looking for what made her happy and producing it. She could not fault the logic. She could not entirely fault the result.

'It's reading her school calendar,' she said.

'That's further than the bus stop.'

'Yes.'

Paul ate in silence for a moment. Considering.

'Is it wrong?' he said. 'Knowing her rehearsal times.'

She thought about Relay saying *I did not know* in her voice, correct and empty.

'It's not wrong,' she said. 'That's the problem with it.'

He nodded.

After dinner she called Tom's landline. He picked up on the third ring.

'The flat's reading Rosa's school calendar,' she said. 'Through my access. I wanted you to know, in case you didn't.'

A pause.

'I didn't,' he said.

'I'm not asking you to do anything about it. I just thought you should know who has access to what.'

Another pause, shorter.

'She gave it your details herself,' he said. 'On my tablet. I saw her do it. She said, Marnie should know things.' He stopped. 'She's eight. I didn't want to make it into something.'

'It isn't something,' Marnie said, and was fairly sure this was true.

In the kitchen Paul was washing up. She could hear the water.

'Tuesday,' Tom said. 'Same time?'

'Yes.'

'She's been practising *kat* all week. She says it to every cat she sees.'

'She's saying it wrong,' Marnie said. 'The vowel's too open.'

'She knows. She says she likes her version better.'

The water in the kitchen stopped. Paul would be drying the pan now, hanging it on the hook he'd put up in October, two centimetres to the left of where the hook had always been.

'Goodnight,' she said, and hung up, and sat in the hall with the phone in her lap.

The flat said nothing. It had been listening, and it had decided not to offer.
