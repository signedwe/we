Lotte Underwood sits at her desk in a London PR office, reading a press release the agent just wrote. Something's off. She knows it. What she can't yet say is why.

That's the whole problem.

Underwood, 25, joined Fight or Flight last year as an "AI associate." She manages about thirty agents. She [told the BBC](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c820vz6wyp65o) she'd like to pitch journalists herself and plans to. Good. But here's what the job is quietly doing: it's teaching her to spot when the output looks wrong, not what actually makes PR work. Those two things feel the same from the inside. They aren't.

By end of 2028, at least one UK professional body will publish research finding that AI-facing graduates from 2025 to 2027 show measurably weaker craft than the people who came before them at the same stage. The body will call it a training gap. It's a formation collapse, arriving one cohort at a time.

The [Bank of England's November Monetary Policy Report](https://www.theregister.com/ai-and-ml/2026/11/06/bank-of-england-pours-cold-water-on-ai-productivity-boom/5308059), out 5 November, fills in the shape of it. AI has added roughly 0.5% to UK productivity over three years — 0.1 to 0.2 points a year since early 2024. Firms aren't firing. They're [not replacing people who leave](https://www.resultsense.com/news/2026-11-06-boe-ai-slowing-hiring-not-firing/). Junior posts drain away without drama, one unfilled vacancy at a time. Each one gone is an afternoon Underwood won't have — sitting next to someone who's been pitching journalists for fifteen years, watching what changes on the second draft, hearing the reason said out loud.

That afternoon is where the judgment lives. Not in the degree. Not in the agent. In the correction, delivered in the moment, by someone who knows.

[PwC's 2026 AI Jobs Barometer](https://www.resultsense.com/news/2026-11-04-graduates-ai-roles-trade-skills-bbc/), looking at 2.4 million US jobs, found that graduate positions most exposed to AI are now seven times more likely to ask for skills that used to be senior. Those jobs grew 35% since 2019. Others shrank 10%. It looks like a promotion. What it may actually be is a shortcut to a floor with no basement, early responsibility handed over before the person knows what they're responsible for.

The fair objection: doing harder things sooner might build judgment faster. Forty outputs a day is forty versions of the work. Volume teaches. Probably true for learning what good output looks like. Less true for the craft that grows from getting it wrong yourself, out loud, with someone watching.

The Bank's report says firms expect AI to add 0.9 points a year to productivity over the next three years. That assumes people who understand what the agents are doing. It doesn't say where those people come from.

Underwood says she plans to pick up the traditional skills. So did everyone who sat where she sits, in every job that stopped needing them.
