The safeguard at the heart of Britain's new automated decision law is a word nobody has defined.

By the end of 2027, a tribunal will rule on a rejected candidate's claim and find that a human did review it. A real person, clicking a real screen. And still find against the employer, because the law required the review to be *meaningful* and nobody, not Parliament, not the Secretary of State, not the ICO, has said what that means. That ruling will be the first time a court has had to construct the definition, because Parliament declined to write it down.

[Since 5 February 2026](https://bratby.law/automated-decision-making-duaa-uk-regime/), Article 22C of the UK GDPR requires that when a hiring tool, a benefit calculator, or a loan scorer makes a significant decision about a person, they get a right to human intervention. The statute uses the phrase "meaningful human involvement." Article 22D gives the Secretary of State power to define it by regulation. [No such regulations have been made.](https://bratby.law/automated-decision-making-duaa-uk-regime/)

That is 229 days since the law changed. From 5 February to 22 September 2026, the day the AI Regulation and Safety Bill reaches [committee stage in the Lords](https://cubbbix.com/blog/ai-regulation-september-2026-global-update). The definition still doesn't exist.

A recruiter opens a dashboard. The software has scored four hundred candidates. She sees rankings and flags. She doesn't see the model's reasoning. The vendor doesn't expose it. She approves or rejects. The candidate receives a letter saying a human reviewed their application. That sentence is true. Whether it satisfies "meaningful" is a question the law leaves entirely open.

This is what the new regime actually did. The old Article 22 said: decisions with significant effects on people are prohibited unless you can justify an exception. [The new Article 22C says: permitted, provided you apply safeguards.](https://www.scaffold.digital/news/uk-ai-regulation-in-2026-whats-in-force-whats-coming-and-what-your-business-should-do) Parliament moved the pressure from the justification to the safeguard. Then left the safeguard undefined. What used to be a gate is now a checklist with a blank line for the most important item.

[Legal commentators](https://bratby.law/automated-decision-making-duaa-uk-regime/) describe "the most consequential question under the new regime" as whether a human review process qualifies as meaningful, and note that no definition exists in the Act itself. [The ICO's draft guidance](https://www.kennedyslaw.com/en/thought-leadership/article/recent-uk-legal-and-regulatory-developments-on-ai-and-automated-decision-making/) is the closest thing to a steer. Draft guidance isn't law. It hasn't been tested anywhere.

There is a case against this. Leaving "meaningful" undefined isn't necessarily careless. Courts build definitions through cases, and an over-prescriptive statutory definition might freeze a word that needs to flex as the technology changes. That is a real argument. It is also an argument for getting cases heard quickly, which requires a candidate to bring one, which requires her to know the review was hollow, which requires access to information the current law doesn't guarantee her.

The person this costs is the candidate who was rejected. She holds a right to meaningful human involvement in a decision that shaped her life. The statute forgot to say what meaningful looks like.

The letter said a human reviewed it. Somewhere, a person pressed a button.
