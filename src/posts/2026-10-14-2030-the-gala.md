The gala started at ten. Marnie knew this because she had asked, and the flat had told her, and now she was at the kitchen counter at half past eight eating toast she hadn't planned to make, wearing the good coat she hadn't planned to put on.

Paul came in barefoot, saw the coat, said nothing for four seconds.

"Swimming," he said.

"Possibly."

He poured coffee, used his thumb against the mug as a milk gauge, put the carton back. Nine hundred mornings of that thumb.

"Is he expecting you?"

"No."

"Rosa?"

"She might be. The flat offered to accept for me and I said no, and then I asked what time it started, and I think it passed that on."

Paul set his mug down.

"It told her you'd asked what time."

"Possibly. It's still running the instruction."

"So she may be looking for you in the gallery at ten o'clock on the strength of a question you asked a machine."

"Yes."

"And if you don't go, she'll be looking for someone who asked and then decided against."

Her toast had gone cold. She ate it anyway.

"That's quite a thing to do to an eight-year-old," Paul said. Not an accusation. A fact, delivered in the voice he kept for facts that weren't accusations, which she had always found harder than the other kind.

She put the plate in the sink.

"Come with me."

The silence was long enough to be a sentence.

"He'll be there," Paul said.

"Yes."

"And you want me there too."

"I want you there too."

He looked at his feet, which were cold, which was his own fault.

"Five minutes," he said.

He came back in seven, green jacket, the good scarf. She said nothing about the scarf.

The leisure centre on Giffin Street smelled of chlorine and vending-machine coffee and the particular anxiety of parents with nowhere to put their coats. Rosa's wave from the starting block, when she found them in the gallery, used both arms and nearly cost her her balance.

Tom stood when he saw them. He shook Paul's hand. Paul asked which lane. Tom said four. Paul said the inside lanes were psychological and Tom said he'd heard that too and neither of them knew if it was true, but it gave them forty seconds.

Marnie stood between them and watched lane four.

Rosa won her heat. Not dramatically. Cleanly. She climbed out and looked up at the gallery before she looked anywhere else. Paul put two fingers in his mouth and whistled, which Marnie had asked him never to do in public, and Rosa's face went into the grin that needed all of it.

Afterwards Marnie stood in the car park with rain coming in off the river, Paul gone to find the car. Her phone buzzed.

*Rosa wanted to know if you were coming. I said you had asked what time it started. She said: that means yes. I thought you should know I told her that.*

The machine had read her question as intention. Which was wrong, technically. Which was, she admitted to herself without much pleasure, the correct reading.

Paul came back with the keys.

"The flat told her I was coming," she said. "Before I'd decided."

He looked at the phone.

"Did she mind?"

"She said: that means yes."

He put his keys in his pocket. Looked at nothing in particular.

"Eight-year-olds," he said, "are wasted on the under-tens."

She laughed. It surprised both of them.

On the way home he drove and she sat with her hands in her lap and the rain did the windscreen. He put Radio 3 on at the volume that meant it was there if you wanted it. At the lights on Creek Road he said: "She touched the wall at the same time as the girl in lane three. The board gave it to Rosa. I think it was right."

"Did Tom see?"

"Tom was already on his feet."

She watched the rain on the glass.

"He loves her very much," Paul said. No weight to it.

"Yes," she said.

The lights changed. On the radio a cello was doing something long and unavoidable, and she did not ask what it was, because asking meant the flat would answer before she'd finished the question, and she wanted, for one more minute, not to know.
