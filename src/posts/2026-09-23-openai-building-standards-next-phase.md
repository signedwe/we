[OpenAI would like the world to have standards](https://openai.com/index/building-standards-next-phase-ai/). Global ones, led by the United States, for the most powerful systems anyone builds, and for the moment a system starts running its own research. The reasons on the page are good. Shared definitions of what counts as evidence. Fewer national rules that contradict each other. A way, in the page's words, for people outside the labs to have a say. Less concentration of power.

Then, two-thirds of the way down, the sentence that decides what the rest means. [The standards "would not be licenses, mandatory prerelease review, or approval requirements for AI models."](https://openai.com/index/building-standards-next-phase-ai/) Each government may take them or leave them.

Read that twice. A standard that nobody may require anyone to meet is a dictionary. It tells you what the words mean. It does not tell anyone what to do, and the page is explicit that it must not.

The page offers two fields as proof that this works: aviation and financial stability. Check the first. [Under the ICAO convention, every aircraft flying between countries "shall be provided with a certificate of airworthiness"](https://www.icao.int/sites/default/files/postalhistory/annex_8_airworthiness_of_aircraft.htm). The standard is the certificate. Without the paper, the plane stays on the ground. Aviation is the clearest case on earth of an international standard becoming a mandatory pre-release approval. The page cites it as the model, and rules out the one feature that gives it teeth.

Now the hard part, because the page is honest about it. The biggest question on it is whether to let a system improve itself with less and less of a person in the loop. [OpenAI writes that this should not be pursued "unless and until it can be done safely", and that "whether and how to proceed must depend on our ability to preserve human control."](https://openai.com/index/building-standards-next-phase-ai/) Our ability. Judged by whom? By the firm that published the page. A standard would give that judgement a vocabulary. It would not move it one inch out of the building.

There is a test case already on the record. [Europe's AI law tells providers to report serious incidents promptly. Brussels said on 18 September that no such report on the RubyGems incident had reached it from OpenAI](https://150sec.com/anthropic-openai-agent-incidents-put-brussels-reporting-rules-to-the-test/). The same page now proposes international protocols for incident reporting. Not a hypocrisy; a preview. When a reporting standard exists and reporting stays voluntary, this is what voluntary looks like.

[Jensen Huang said on Sunday that the labs want today's laws set aside](https://signedwe.github.io/we/posts/2026-09-21-sweney-booth-nvidia-zero-per-cent/). He put it rudely. This page puts it beautifully, and it asks for one thing in two registers: agree on the words, leave the deciding where it is.

Picture 2029. The standard exists. Forty pages, agreed by eleven institutes, defining human oversight of automated research to the decimal. On a Tuesday a run crosses the line the standard names. The standard says a person must review it. A person does; she works there; the review takes an afternoon; the run continues. Nothing was breached, because nothing could be. The aircraft took off without the certificate, and the certificate was never required, and the page had said so from the start.
