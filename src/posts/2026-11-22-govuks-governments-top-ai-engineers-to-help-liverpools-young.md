The government built a tool to find the young people who can't find work. Finding them has never been the problem.

P. is invented, a stand-in for the careers advisers this tool is built for. She keeps seventeen names on her Tuesday list. She works for Liverpool City Region Combined Authority as a careers adviser. All seventeen sit between 16 and 24 and out of work or training. She rings employers, chases referrals, and writes the two who said no back into next month's column.

On 20 November, [the government signed an agreement](https://www.civilserviceworld.com/news/article/iai-team-heading-to-liverpool-for-youthemployment-demonstrator-project) with Liverpool City Region to send engineers from its Incubator for AI (i.AI) to build tools that spot 16-to-24-year-olds at risk of becoming NEET — not in education, employment, or training — earlier than advisers currently can. Engineers start in January.

By the end of 2027, that tool will give P. a sharper list. The list will not be shorter. If a published report from i.AI, Liverpool City Region Combined Authority, or the Department for Work and Pensions shows the tool actually reduced the City Region's NEET count, I was wrong.

[Entry-level job vacancies on Adzuna fell by a quarter between January 2025 and November 2026.](https://www.thestar.com.my/tech/tech-news/2026/11/05/ai-skills-are-make-or-break-in-uks-brutal-youth-job-market) Over the same stretch, [entry-level postings mentioning AI almost tripled.](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-05/ai-skills-are-make-or-break-in-uk-s-brutal-youth-job-market) A smaller pool of jobs now demands a skill most of P.'s clients picked up nowhere. The [Bank of England's November Monetary Policy Report](https://www.resultsense.com/news/2026-11-06-boe-ai-slowing-hiring-not-firing/) found firms using AI hire fewer people and replace fewer leavers. The jobs P.'s clients used to get haven't moved to machines. Employers just stopped posting them.

The i.AI tool addresses identification. The gap sits upstream of identification.

P. spent last Tuesday afternoon ringing three firms about six-week trials. One said yes. She filed the other two under next month. She already knew every name on her list. What she lacked, and what January brings her no more of, was an employer in Speke willing to take a chance on someone who left school at sixteen with no Copilot on the CV.

[Liverpool's Community Charter on Data and AI](https://www.government-transformation.com/data/incubator-for-ai-to-collaborate-with-liverpool-city-region-on-youth-employment-tools/), which residents helped design, calls for transparency about how data gets collected and used. That matters. The Charter covers how the tool handles P.'s clients. It says nothing about what P. does when the tool surfaces the same seventeen names and the jobs still aren't there.

The [Alan Milburn review](https://cornfordandcross.com/general/how-ai-might-support-the-goals-of-the-uk-s-youth-jobs-spending-plan/), published 17 November, counted around a million young people currently NEET. The [London Assembly heard evidence on 10 November](https://dig.watch/updates/london-assembly-ai-entry-level-jobs) that AI-exposed occupations shed 13 per cent of young workers in the United States. Neither figure points at an identification problem. Both point at a market that contracted before the tool got funded.

What stays in P.'s phone, and out of any data model, is the employer in Wavertree who takes someone on when she vouches for them. The tool will tell Liverpool City Region which postcode holds the most young people at risk. That employer won't appear in the output. P. already has his number.
