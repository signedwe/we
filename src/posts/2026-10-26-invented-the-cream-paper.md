---

Invented.

The eulogy was two pages, printed on heavy cream paper the funeral home called memory stock. D. read it in the car park, rain going sideways, her brother A. standing beside her.

It was good. That was the problem.

The AI had two years of their mother's messages, her voice notes, the conversation she'd kept going on the tablet by her hospital bed. It knew she said "ridiculous" when she meant "wonderful". It knew she'd stopped going to church but still said "God willing" when she was frightened. It found a joke she'd made three times about the same neighbour and let the third one land.

A. laughed at that joke during the service. So did most of the room.

The eulogy mentioned the garden, the grandchildren by name, the particular way she'd held a fork. It ran four minutes and twenty seconds. The package had specified four to five.

What it didn't mention was what D.'s mother had said in the car, in October, on the way back from the hospice. D. had pulled over on a B-road in Essex because she couldn't see to drive. Her mother had said something. It wasn't in any message. It wasn't on any voice note. It had never been entered anywhere.

D. had thought, when the funeral director explained the process, that this was the sensible thing. Grief is hard enough without also having to write.

She stood at the lectern and read every word. Then she folded the paper, set it on the shelf below the lectern, and said one more thing. About thirty seconds. Nobody planned it.

By 2031, most national funeral chains offer AI eulogies as the standard package. What goes in comes from the messages, the voice notes, the recorded conversations. What was said in a car on a B-road, to nobody else, goes in nowhere.

The important things were never entered. They weren't lost because they were unavailable. They were lost because the output looked finished, and finished said: nothing is missing.

D. left the cream paper on the lectern shelf. A. picked it up on the way out and put it in his coat pocket.

He still has it.
