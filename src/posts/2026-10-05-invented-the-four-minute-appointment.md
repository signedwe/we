Invented. November 2031, a state secondary school in South London.

The screen knows more about A. than Mr O. does. So does A.

Both have read the dashboard. A. has been living it. Mr O. has watched it from across three rows of geography desks, every third Thursday, for two years. Her parents read it on their phones at five o'clock, before they left the house.

By the end of 2028, at least one multi-academy trust in England will have replaced parents' evening grade-reporting slots with asynchronous dashboard access, reserving teacher time for pastoral conversations only. The press release will call it restoring teacher professionalism. If no trust has done this by then, this prediction was wrong.

Mr O.'s appointment is four minutes. It used to be five.

He teaches geography. He badly misses the Isle of Man unit they cut in 2027. He is on appointment fourteen of twenty-two tonight, and his coffee has been cold on the windowsill since quarter past six. Across England, roughly half a million hours of secondary school teacher time go to parents' evenings each year. The system has taken a third of that. What it left is sitting here, under a laminated sign taped to the back of a plastic chair: GEOGRAPHY: MR O. — TABLE 7.

The screen gave A.'s parents her reading age, the predicted grade boundaries, the emotional-regulation flag from the fortnight she spent unsettled before the new timetable landed. Forty seconds. More accurate than Mr O. on grades; he admits this easily. He had read once that teacher assessments ran higher for children whose parents arrived with eye contact and first names. He believed it. He makes a point of not knowing parents' first names.

What the screen doesn't have: A.'s grandmother is ill. A. hasn't told her parents. She's been carrying it since October, and it's in her work, if you know how to look. Mr O. knows how to look because she does this thing with essay endings where she decides partway through whether the ending is worth it, and lately it never is.

Her parents sit down. They already know the grade. What they want is for someone to say the thing the grade doesn't say.

In four minutes, Mr O. will say it.

The screen is better at assessment. Mr O. is better at witness. The profession spent four years learning to name this distinction and it is now the entire job description. The hard version: the bias travels with it.

He has eight more appointments. The last is at 8:40.

A. is in the corridor. She's been there for three minutes.

The door opens.

Mr O. doesn't look at the screen.
