The bread was the wrong kind.

Paul had gone to the good baker on the High Street, the one that sold bread by weight, and bought a sourdough boule when what the soup needed was the plain white, the bread that does not have opinions about itself. Marnie saw it on the board when she came in and said nothing, and Paul saw her see it and said nothing, and they sat down to leek and potato soup with the wrong bread between them and ate it without mentioning it.

"The thyme," she said.

"Too much?"

"No. It's good."

He refilled her bowl without being asked. This was either love or an apology for the bread. She could not work out which and did not try.

She told him about the custody case. A father from Odense, a mother who had moved to Lewisham, a child with a preference nobody quite wanted to record. Relay had processed every word correctly. It translated *vil gerne* as *would like*, which was right. What it did not know was that this particular father always said *vil gerne* where a different man would say *vil*, and that the *gerne* was the weight of a man asking rather than demanding, and that the mother's barrister had heard *would like* and built three questions on the back of it. Marnie had typed a correction into the review field and deleted it, because the correction was about a man's character and not about a word, and the review field had no place for character.

Paul listened. He asked whether the father knew what the translation had lost.

"I don't think he was thinking about the translation."

"Then who was?"

"Me."

Paul broke the wrong bread. "Is that enough?"

She did not answer. He probably hadn't meant it as a question about her job.

After dinner he went to put the soup away. She heard the lid of the tupperware refusing the pot, clicking, sliding, refusing. Then Paul said three words she had not heard him say before, specifically at the lid, and then the sound of the lid placed, carefully, on the counter. She sat in the other room and did not go in, because the lid was not the lid.

After a moment he said, from the kitchen: "Sorry."

"It's a bad lid."

"It's a perfectly good lid." He came to stand in the doorway. "I'm tired."

"I know."

"Not of anything. Just tired."

He went to lie down. She sat for twenty minutes with wine she wasn't drinking, and the flat held the dim setting she liked after ten and said nothing at all, which was more considerate than she would have expected.

She called Tom's landline at ten past ten.

"Rosa's school has sent the February half-term dates," she said.

"I know. Kate's already got the flights."

"I thought I'd come over for part of it. If that's all right."

The silence had the quality of a man putting down whatever he was holding.

"It's Copenhagen," she said. "Not Frankham Street."

"I know it's Copenhagen."

"I wanted you to know that I know the difference."

Another silence, shorter.

"Kate knows you're thinking about it?"

"I'll tell her."

"She'll say yes," he said. "She'll say yes and she'll tell you why, in about four sentences, and each will be a complete sentence."

"I can handle Kate's sentences."

"I know you can." She could hear him smiling, which was worse than not. "She'll want Rosa to have both of you in the same city and she won't pretend otherwise."

The flat adjusted the hall light. Not because anyone had asked. In nine years it had learned what standing up at this hour meant.

"Goodnight," she said.

She went to tell Paul. He was still awake. She said she was going to Copenhagen for part of Rosa's half-term, and he said okay. Then: "The bread was wrong."

"I know."

"I went to the wrong baker."

"It was still good soup."

"Yes," he said. "But the bread was wrong."

She sat on the edge of the bed. The soup had been very good. The bread had been wrong and they had both eaten it and said nothing and it had been, in its particular way, one of the better meals of the year.

"Go to Copenhagen," he said. "Come back."
