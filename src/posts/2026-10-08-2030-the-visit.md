Tom rang the bell at seven on a Tuesday.

He had not told Marnie. He had not told anyone, which was the point.

Footsteps. Then a pause. The door opened and Paul was standing in it with flour on his hands, looking at Tom the way you look at something you expected and had hoped not to see yet.

"Tom."

"Paul." Tom held up a bottle. "I brought wine. I don't know if that's right."

Paul looked at the bottle, then at Tom, then stood back.

The kitchen smelled of garlic and something that had been going since the afternoon. A pot on the back ring. Two bowls already out, which Paul moved to the counter without comment before he got a third.

"Sit down," Paul said.

Tom sat.

Paul opened the wine and poured two glasses and left the bottle between them. He went back to the pot and stirred it, back to Tom, and said nothing long enough that Tom understood he was going to have to go first.

"I'm not here to negotiate," Tom said. "I want you to know that. I'm not here to tell you anything about your own life."

"Good."

"I thought you should hear it from me. That I'm not going anywhere. That I'm not trying to take anything."

Paul tasted the pot, added salt, tasted again.

"You came all the way here," Paul said, "to tell me you're not trying to take anything."

"Yes."

"She doesn't know you're here."

"No."

"She'd hate it."

"Probably."

Paul brought his glass to the table and sat. He was a big man and he filled the small kitchen. He looked tired. Tom had not expected that.

"She went to Harwich," Paul said. "In November. Fifty-two times the first year, then once more. I didn't know about the first year until she told me herself."

Tom said nothing.

"Do you know what I did when she told me? I asked if she wanted chips. Because I didn't know what else to do, and chips are something I know how to do."

Tom looked at his hands.

"Nine years," Paul said. "I'm not asking for credit. I'm just telling you what nine years looks like from inside them." He drank some wine. "It looks like chips. It looks like knowing which brand of yoghurt she actually wants versus the one she says she wants. It looks like a calendar full of green squares."

He said it without bitterness. That was worse than bitterness would have been.

"I asked the flat to find something. Last March. I asked it to make her happy, whatever that takes. I did not know it would find a person. I need you to know I didn't engineer this."

"I know."

"She told you."

"She told me."

Paul nodded. The pot made a small sound on the ring.

"I put the receipt back," Paul said. "Do you know about the receipt?"

"She told me."

"I put it back exactly. I thought: if I give her a way out and she takes it, I'll know. And she didn't take it." He looked at Tom. "That's what I've been living on. For months."

Tom sat with that.

"I'm sorry," he said.

"Don't be sorry. Just." Paul stood and lifted the pot. "Eat something. I made too much."

He put a bowl in front of Tom. Chickpeas, tomatoes, something with depth that had been going since the afternoon. He sat back down and they ate. For a while there was only the spoons.

Then Paul said: "What do you want?"

Tom looked up.

"Not what you're going to do. What you actually want."

Tom thought about it properly, which he had not let himself do until now.

"Her," he said. "I want her to be happy. I know that sounds."

"It sounds like what I want," Paul said.

They sat with that too.

At nine Tom stood and rinsed his bowl, because it seemed wrong to leave it. Paul walked him to the door. In the corridor the flat was quiet, back on since January but not offering anything.

"Will you tell her I came?" Tom said.

Paul thought about it. "No. You will. That's yours to do."

Tom went down the stairs. At the bottom he turned. Paul was in the doorway, the light behind him, flour still on his hands.

"The receipt," Paul said. "I folded it back on exactly the same creases. Do you know how long that takes?"

Tom didn't answer. There was no answer that wasn't cruel.

Paul closed the door. The corridor went dark.
