MEMO

TO: Minister for AI, October 2026
FROM: Cabinet Office, AI Sovereignty Review Unit, March 2031
RE: What the Council for Science and Technology meant to say

By end of 2028 you'll know whether any of this worked. If at least one major AI provider has publicly confirmed an exchange with the UK government — data or planning access traded for something the public sector actually wanted, lower costs, a fine-tuned model, a seat on a safety board — then the cards were real. If not, they stayed on the map.

We've had five years to work out whether the word meant anything.

The [letter sent in July 2026, published that October](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ai-sovereignty-preserving-uk-agency-in-an-ai-enabled-world), defined AI sovereignty as "sufficient agency across the AI value chain to deliver the UK's chosen outcomes." Agency is what a well-informed customer has. Sovereignty is what you have when you can say no and the other party has to listen.

The four recommendations were: map the gaps, name one coordinator, fund pilots in public services, keep buying from the big labs while building something domestic. All four happened. The map was produced. The coordinator was named, then reshuffled, then absorbed into the DSIT AI directorate. The pilots ran. The Alan Turing Institute's domestic model went into soft launch in 2029. It handles about 3% of public-sector AI work.

The big labs handle the rest. Their terms changed in 2028. Britain negotiated, and got better terms, because the map showed what the UK had to offer: grid connections, planning fast-tracks, NHS data, court transcripts, 68 million people with high AI adoption. Those cards are real. We played them.

But cards aren't deeds.

[Norway](https://www.economicshelp.org/blog/www.economicshelp.org/blog/214768/economics/norway-uk-oil/) didn't get sovereignty over North Sea oil by mapping how dependent it was on Shell and then naming a coordinator. It got there by [owning 70% of the fields](https://www.economicshelp.org/blog/214768/economics/norway-uk-oil/) before the drilling started. Britain drilled with Shell and hoped to negotiate later. It went fine until it didn't. The UK got roughly [£67bn from North Sea privatisation](https://www.economicshelp.org/blog/214768/economics/norway-uk-oil/). Norway built [the world's largest sovereign wealth fund](https://worldbyflow.com/p/norways-oil-fund-the-sovereign-wealth-reference-model-be681f1a), now worth more than $2 trillion. That gap took forty years to become obvious. This one will take less.

The CST letter said, without quite saying it: Britain doesn't own the compute, the model weights, or the training pipelines, and has no domestic lab at the frontier. All four were true in October 2026. All four remain true in March 2031. The letter called this AI sovereignty, pending a plan. The plan arrived. The situation didn't move.

By 2029, "sovereign AI" in the UK had come to mean: a British company runs the servers, the data stays onshore, and someone in Whitehall has read the API terms.

The [CST was right to warn the minister](https://www.unite.ai/uk-council-for-science-and-technology-publishes-ai-sovereignty-advice/). What it didn't do was name what Britain owns that the big labs actually need, and attach terms to it. Not a coordinator. Terms. Grid connections. Fast-track planning. NHS cohorts. Court transcripts. Student records. The letter named these things. It did not say: we'll withhold them until you give us something structural in return.

The North Sea oil is still under the North Sea.

*This memo is invented. The future it describes is not.*
