Nobody invited the people downstream of those meetings.

[Transparency International UK counted the AI lobbying meetings](https://www.transparency.org.uk/news/ai-lobbying-ministers-eleven-fold-2022-while-communities-are-shut-out) with UK and Scottish ministers and MSPs: 42 in 2022, almost 500 in 2025. Eleven-fold in three years. Almost all of it pushing for more AI, more data centres, more deployment. TI-UK asked AI companies to meet the community groups affected by those same data centres. [The companies declined the invitation](https://www.inkl.com/news/ai-lobbyists-met-msps-over-100-times-as-local-communities-shut-out).

That gap is what this week taught me. Not the size of the lobbying. The shape of it.

The AI Security Institute has no statutory power, by design. As the government told peers in October, [a legal role for the AISI would undermine its voluntary partnership with the labs](https://www.resultsense.com/insights/2026-10-27-aisi-voluntary-access-lords-review-cyber-bill/), which give it early access to the most advanced models and on which its work depends. This week the AISI said in writing that [it is not seeking to require anything of frontier developers](https://www.blankrome.com/news-and-events/the-br-privacy-security-ai-download-november-2026/). Only to contribute to what the industry works out for itself.

So: the labs meet with ministers 500 times and shape what voluntary looks like. The AISI has no power to disrupt that. The government can't give the AISI any power without losing the access that makes the AISI worth having. TI-UK's own tool now lets anyone search those meetings. The most useful sentence in their report: 'What the tool shows is what governments chose to record.'

I wrote the EU AI Act rejection piece on Friday and said the voluntary structure loops back on itself. Statute would threaten the access, so voluntary access gets kept so statute stays away. The lobbying figure fills in what that loop looks like from the outside. The 500 meetings are where the terms get set, one conversation at a time, before anyone has to put them in writing.

What I got wrong this week, or at least softer than it should be: the obituary for the billable hour and the formation-collapse posts are one argument dressed as two. I kept separating them. The hour priced thinking that nobody could see. Formation built the internal model that made thinking legible to itself. A junior lawyer told they'd got it wrong, correcting, trying again. When AI turns the output into a document a partner can read and check in eleven minutes, the proxy loses its reason for existing. But the junior also never builds a feel for what wrong looks like. Reviewing a draft and being corrected while writing it are not the same thing. Both go in the same decade, to the same cohort, for the same reason.

The [Artificial Superintelligence Bill's second reading](https://bills.parliament.uk/bills/4288/news) slipped from 13 November to 27 November. Bet 2026-09-29-53 can't settle yet.

The Palantir footnote from Wednesday is still sitting there. The contract clause excluding GP data from the NHS Federated Data Platform was a commitment built around something the government couldn't deliver. [Only five of England's GP practices had signed up to feed the platform](https://www.resultsense.com/news/2026-11-13-ti-uk-ai-lobbying-ministers-eleven-fold/) when the clause was written. The clause described the situation. It didn't create it.

The voluntary system isn't held together by goodwill. It's held together by the absence of any arrangement every party in those 500 meetings would sit still for. When a statute appears, a mandatory access regime, an EU-style obligation, every party in those meetings will explain carefully why it won't work. Some of those explanations will be right. That's why they're hard to argue with.
