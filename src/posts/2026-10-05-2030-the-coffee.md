Kate was already in the café by the station when Marnie arrived, an Americano in front of her.

She tilted her head at the seat opposite.

"Copenhagen," Kate said.

A fact, not a topic.

"There's a project management firm there. Construction, infrastructure. They want me from January." She drank some of it. "Six months, could run longer."

"And Rosa?"

"Rosa comes in February for the school break, Easter, and the summer if I'm still there. I want her to be able to say something when she gets off the plane. Not be the child who points at everything." A pause. "Tom doesn't know yet. I'll tell him."

Said flat. Not bitter, not fond. Just the conclusion of a sum she'd done years ago.

"So you want me to teach her."

"I want her to be able to order a hot chocolate without a mime show." "She can already say seagull and she knows it's disappointing. That's a foundation."

"You don't have to," Kate said. "The Thursdays are already arranged. This is separate."

"I know."

"Good. Because I'm not asking you for a favour. I'm asking whether you want to do a thing." She looked at Marnie steadily. "Most people don't bother with the difference."

"Yes," Marnie said. "I'll teach her."

Kate nodded. Done.

"He didn't tell me about you," Kate said. "After the ferry. He came back different and I thought it was me, for about a year, and then I stopped thinking it was me. He never said what it was." She looked at her coffee. "I thought you should know that. You probably think he did."

Marnie held her cup in both hands.

"I saw the photo on his phone last spring. He didn't explain it." "Rosa told you it plays music. He turned that off eighteen months ago."

The coffee machine started up for someone else's order.

Eighteen months ago was the month Marnie had first walked past the bakery and stood under the launderette awning and said no thank you to a door. She had not known until this moment that the months were the same.

"I'm not telling you this so you'll do anything," Kate said. "You've been kind to my daughter. I prefer people to have accurate information."

She picked up her coat. Put it on, one arm then the other.

"Tuesday evenings. If that works. She'll want something rude. Start mild."

"*Lort*," Marnie said. "She can earn the rest."

Kate paid for both coffees on her way out, wrist to the reader, already in motion, the door swinging shut before Marnie had registered she was gone.

She thought about a man who turned off a song and said nothing about it. Who carried a photo and told her to her face he couldn't remember her name. Who came home different and let someone else spend a year working out why.

She took out her phone to tell Tom, then put it away. Kate had said she would tell him herself. Marnie believed her, and that was a mistake, though not Kate's.
