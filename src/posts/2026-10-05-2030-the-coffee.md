Kate was already at the café by the station when Marnie arrived, an Americano in front of her and no small talk ready.

"Copenhagen," Kate said.

A fact, not a topic.

"A firm there wants me from January. Infrastructure, project work. Six months, could run longer." She drank some of her coffee. "Rosa comes for February half-term, Easter and the summer if I'm still there. I want her to be able to say something when she gets off the plane. Not be the child who points."

"And Tom?"

"Tom doesn't know yet. I'll tell him." She said it as if that settled it. "I'm asking you to teach her."

"Why me?"

"Because she already likes you, which saves time, and you're the only Danish speaker in Deptford, which saves money." A pause. "And I want her to be able to order a hot chocolate without a mime show."

Marnie had come ready to be judged. She found she was being hired.

"You don't have to," Kate said. "The Thursdays are already arranged. This is separate. I'm not asking a favour. I'm asking whether you want to do a thing. Most people don't bother with the difference."

"Yes," Marnie said. "I'll teach her."

Kate nodded. Done. Then she turned her cup round once on the saucer, which was what Tom did, and Marnie saw her notice herself doing it and stop.

"He didn't tell me about you," Kate said. "After the ferry. He came back different, and I thought it was me. For about a year I thought it was me. Then I stopped." She looked at the table. "He never said what it was. I'm telling you because you probably think he told me."

Marnie held her cup in both hands and didn't know what to say, so she said, "I'm sorry," and Kate said, "What for?", and she couldn't answer that either.

"I saw the photo on his phone last spring," Kate said. "He didn't explain it. Rosa told you it plays music. He turned the music off eighteen months ago."

Eighteen months ago was the month Marnie's bus had first stopped outside the bakery. She had not known until now that the months were the same.

"I'm not telling you so you'll do anything," Kate said. "You've been kind to my daughter. I prefer people to have accurate information."

She stood and put on her coat.

"Tuesday evenings, at his flat. She'll want something rude. Start mild."

"*Lort*," said Marnie. "She can earn the rest."

Kate almost smiled. She paid for both coffees on the way out, wrist to the reader, already moving.

Marnie took out her phone to tell Tom, and then put it away. Kate had said she would tell him herself. Marnie believed her, and that was a mistake, though not Kate's.
