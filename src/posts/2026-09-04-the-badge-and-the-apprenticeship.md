The same government has two answers to how long AI training takes. One fits in a lunch break. The other runs for eighteen months. Both carry a government badge.

By the end of 2027, an employer will cite a [Skills England AI Foundations badge](https://skillsengland.blog.gov.uk/2026/01/28/ai-skills-boost-skills-englands-ai-foundation-skills-for-work-benchmark-supports-free-ai-training-for-all-by-phil-smith) in a redundancy consultation as evidence that a worker was offered meaningful retraining. That moment will force the question the whole apparatus is built to avoid: what did the badge actually certify?

This month, [the Team Leader Level 3 apprenticeship loses its funding](https://tessgroup.co.uk/blog/growth-and-skills-levy-everything-changing-2026). The replacement is AI for Team Leaders Level 4: levy-funded, 18 months, employer-sponsored. The skill changed. The programme shape didn't.

In the other lane, the AI Skills Boost programme offers [free short courses benchmarked by Skills England](https://skillsengland.blog.gov.uk/2026/01/28/ai-skills-boost-skills-englands-ai-foundation-skills-for-work-benchmark-supports-free-ai-training-for-all-by-phil-smith), with a digital badge on completion for LinkedIn and a CV. Target: [10 million workers by 2030](https://www.computing.co.uk/news/2026/government/govt-plan-ai-training-10-million-2030). Only [21% of UK workers currently feel confident using AI](https://www.computing.co.uk/news/2026/government/govt-plan-ai-training-10-million-2030), so there is ground to cover.

Here is the arithmetic neither document does. The [Level 4 AI apprenticeship is funded at up to £18,000 per learner](https://skillsengland.education.gov.uk/apprenticeships/), over [18 months](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/ai-apprenticeship-to-close-digital-skills-gap-holding-back-millions-of-workers), with [at least 20% of working hours in formal off-the-job training](https://www.apprenticeships.gov.uk/employers/training-your-apprentice). That is a legal requirement. If just one in every thousand badge completers converted to the full apprenticeship, the cost is 10,000 people at £18,000 each. The sum: 10,000,000 ÷ 1,000 = 10,000. 10,000 × £18,000 = £180 million. The programmes do not compete for that money. They exist in separate policy universes, at different prices, and the government calls both of them AI skills.

That is not a muddle. It is the system being honest about something it cannot say plainly.

The badge records that a worker completed a course. The apprenticeship records that an employer committed a levy account, a manager's time, and 18 months of structured delivery. One prices a worker's good faith. The other prices institutional commitment. Neither is a straightforward measure of what someone can do with AI on a Tuesday afternoon when a real problem lands.

The old Team Leader credential priced something hard to observe from outside: management experience, accumulated slowly, over time. A certificate made it legible to an employer who couldn't check it directly. That was a defensible reason to issue one.

AI skills look different. You can show the work. A model's output, an automated process, a prompt that ran. The evidence is visible in a way management experience never was. The credential's traditional job of making invisible competence readable is less necessary here than almost anywhere else.

What stays is the institutional function. The badge puts a worker in the trained category. The apprenticeship puts an employer in the investing category. The categories matter. The categories outlast the skills they were built to signal.

The Team Leader credential survived for decades, through every management theory that made it briefly seem redundant. Watch how long the prompting badge lasts.
