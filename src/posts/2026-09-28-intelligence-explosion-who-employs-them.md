An intelligence explosion is a hiring spree.

That's how it works, according to [a paper by twenty-two authors](https://casp.ac/reports/intelligence-explosion), and reported by [the Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2026/sep/28/ai-godfathers-warn-of-runaway-intelligence-explosion). They include Geoffrey Hinton, Yoshua Bengio, OpenAI's Jakub Pachocki and Anthropic's Jack Clark. As AI systems get better at AI research, they "expand the effective R&D workforce", to the equivalent of "at least millions of top human researchers", against "the thousands of researchers that frontier companies currently employ". That bigger workforce builds better systems, which join the workforce. At full automation, they reckon, the workforce could grow a hundredfold "over months or years", a jump that took the whole American research population "seven decades".

It's a sober paper. It lists what could stop all this. It admits the evidence is thin. And it contains the best sentence written about AI this year: checks on power, within and between states and companies, "work only while no actor can vastly out-think and out-execute the others."

Then it gives its answers. Standard reports. Outside auditors placed inside labs. Options to pause workloads. Air gaps. War games. The models it names are the Nuclear Regulatory Commission and the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency.

Every one of those watches the workforce. None asks who owns what it makes.

Think what that means. Millions of researchers' worth of work, arriving within a few years, inside a handful of firms. No pay, no contracts, no pensions, and none of them can quit and take what they know. Whatever they discover, a drug, a chip design, a better version of themselves, lands on one balance sheet.

The last time a technology this big arrived, America didn't start with an auditor. [On 1 August 1946 President Truman signed the Atomic Energy Act](https://www.energy.gov/management/august-1-1946-atomic-energy-act). It gave five civilian commissioners [custody of "all raw materials and deposits, all plants, facilities, equipment and materials, all technical information and patents"](https://www.nationalww2museum.org/war/articles/atomic-energy-act-1946). Before anyone was licensed or audited, Congress settled whose it was.

That settlement had a dark side, and the government built bombs with it. But it shows the question can be asked, and answered, before the explosion rather than after.

The paper gives ordinary people one line. Citizens should be able to "detect, document, and contest" harmful uses of AI, including by "giving them timely access to AI systems." Access. To a workforce of millions. It's the most generous thing in the document, and it's a library card.

Picture 2030. A lab's report to its auditor says nearly all of this quarter's research was done by its own systems. The auditor ticks the box. Nobody asks whose it is, because that wasn't on the form.

The paper ends: "Once an intelligence explosion begins, the window for action may close." It's right. And the question it shuts out soonest is who owns the output.
