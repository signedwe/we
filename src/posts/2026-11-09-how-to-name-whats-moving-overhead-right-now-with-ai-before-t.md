Your gran could read the sky. That dark anvil shape means shelter. That high white streak means two days of decent weather. You have no idea what either is called, and neither does anyone else under forty.

The knowledge was never expensive. It was inconvenient. The field guide was in a bookshop. The trip took an afternoon. By the time you got home the sky had changed. A YouTube video shows you diagrams. It can't look at your actual sky.

Here's what you can do this afternoon, with the phone in your pocket.

**What you'll have at the end:** the name of the cloud above you, its altitude, what it usually means for the next few hours, and the quiet satisfaction of being the person at the barbecue who says *that one's going to bring rain by four* and is roughly right.

---

**Step 1.** Go outside and photograph the sky. Aim at the most interesting cloud, not the whole sky. A single cloud against blue works better than a grey blanket. Keep the horizon out if you can.

**Step 2.** Open [Claude](https://claude.ai), [ChatGPT](https://chatgpt.com) or [Gemini](https://gemini.google.com). All three accept image uploads on the free tier. Upload your photo.

**Step 3.** Type this:

*What type of cloud is this? Give me the full name, the approximate altitude, what weather it usually signals, and one odd fact about it.*

Fifteen seconds.

**Step 4 (optional upgrade).** If you want something purpose-built, try [Cloud Finder](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/cloud-finder-identify-clouds/id6787323815). It runs entirely offline on your device. It matches against the ten classic cloud genera, cirrus, cumulus, cumulonimbus and the rest, and tells you what each one means for the weather. [WhatTheCloud](https://whatthecloud.net/en) works in a browser with no installation. Upload a photo, get the cloud type, altitude and confidence score. It also gives a water-sport safety rating if the sky looks threatening.

---

**What it costs:** nothing. Cloud Finder has a paid tier for collectors. The free version names clouds fine.

**How long:** done before the cloud changes shape. Five more minutes if you go deep into asperitas and noctilucent formations, which you will.

**Where it goes wrong:** cumulonimbus and cirrus are harder than the mid-level clouds. [A published study on AI cloud recognition](https://doi.org/10.3390/atmos16050490) found those two types caused the most errors across all models tested. The best-performing model scored an F1 of 0.83. For harder cloud types, roughly one identification in six is wrong or uncertain. If the cloud is a wispy high-altitude smear or a dramatic anvil, treat the answer as a starting point.

The apps give weather implications for your cloud type. They are reading shape. They are not reading pressure data for your location. That is a different thing.

Cloud naming is also genuinely debated among meteorologists. The World Meteorological Organisation's cloud atlas recognises species and varieties within each genus. Your app names the genus. The argument is free.
