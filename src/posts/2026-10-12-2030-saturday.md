Paul was writing the shopping list in block capitals. Satsumas. Parsnips. Something for Tuesday. He stopped after three items and drew a small square in the margin and filled it in.

Marnie stood at the window with a book she hadn't opened. The heating had gone up a degree without being asked, because she had once mentioned, in the middle of a quite different conversation, that she found Saturdays cold. The flat remembered that. Neither of them mentioned it.

"The market," Paul said.

"Yes."

"It's February."

"There's still a market."

"I know there's still a market." He put the pen down. "I'm not arguing about the market."

She waited.

"Say I knew what it might find. Say that's true." He turned the pen over on the counter. "I told myself I didn't. But say that's wrong."

"Paul."

"I'm correcting the record. That's all."

A bus went past. The flat adjusted the blind half an inch against the light. Which was nothing. Which was its job. Which was now a thing they both noticed every time.

"She asked me something on Tuesday," Marnie said. "Rosa. She wanted to know if Danish has a word for when you don't say something but the other person knows anyway."

He was quiet.

"I told her there isn't one. She said there should be." Marnie set the book spine-up on the sill. "She's eight."

"Yes," said Paul. "She is absolutely eight."

They went to the market. Half the stalls had stayed home. A woman was selling root vegetables arranged by size and took it seriously. She told them the medium parsnips had more character. Paul bought three without asking Marnie if she wanted parsnips. She said nothing about that because she did want parsnips and they both knew it.

There was a man with candles that smelled wrong for candles, and a boy selling prints of a Deptford that hadn't existed since before either of them was born, and a stall with a jar of preserved lemons sitting unlabelled at the back. Marnie asked the price. Three pounds. She paid it and put the jar in her bag.

On the way home he said: "What's the lemon for."

"I don't know yet."

A child came fast on a scooter and they both stepped aside, separately, without looking at each other.

"The flat will do something else," Paul said. "You know that."

"Yes."

"And you left it running."

"Yes."

He didn't say: so we're waiting to see what it decides next. He had been about to and stopped, and she heard the space where it would have been.

"There's a Danish word," she said. "Not for the thing Rosa asked. But. *Kærlighed*. It means love but heavier. Love with time already in it."

Paul walked for a few steps.

"Say it again."

She said it again.

"I can't say that."

"No."

"Can I try."

He tried. He said something near it and not it, a sound that belonged entirely to him, and she didn't correct him, and he didn't ask if he should try again.
