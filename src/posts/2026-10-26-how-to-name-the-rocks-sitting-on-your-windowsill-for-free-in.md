Someone in your house has a rock.

A smooth grey one from a beach in Cornwall. A sparkly bit from a walk in the Peak District. That heavy dark lump from Iceland that ended up next to the succulents. It's been there four years. Nobody knows what it is.

You can find out tonight.

**What you'll have at the end:** the name, formation story, and rough age of any rock, mineral, or crystal in your house. In the time it takes to boil a kettle.

---

**Step 1: Take the photo properly.** Natural light, plain background. A sheet of white paper works. Photograph the texture. If there's a broken face or crystal face, point the camera there. Put a coin in frame for scale. Two photos beat one: the whole specimen, then a close-up of the interesting bit.

**Step 2: Go to [whatismyrock.com](https://whatismyrock.com/).** Free, no account, no sign-up. It runs on Claude AI and [analyses colour, grain size, texture, crystal structure, banding, and lustre](https://whatismyrock.com/) in your photo against knowledge of thousands of rock and mineral types. Upload the photo. In the notes field, add what you can feel: heavy for its size? Waxy or glassy surface? Does it scratch your fingernail or not?

**Step 3: Read what comes back.** Not just the name. The story. Where it formed. Whether it cooled from magma, compressed on an ancient seabed, or crystallised deep inside a mountain root. How old it probably is. Whether it's common on British beaches or a bit unusual.

**Step 4: Go further.** Paste the result into Claude or ChatGPT and ask:

*"I have a specimen identified as [name]. What's the best simple test to confirm it? What are the most likely UK places it came from?"*

It'll walk you through a scratch test (Mohs hardness: harder minerals scratch softer ones), a streak test (drag it across the back of an unglazed tile), whether it should fizz in a drop of white vinegar. The vinegar test catches limestone and chalk every time.

**Step 5: Look it up on the BGS map.** The British Geological Survey's [online geology viewer](https://www.bgs.ac.uk/map-viewers/bgs-geology-viewer/) is free. Type in where you found the stone. The map tells you what bedrock sits underneath. Then check whether that matches what you're holding.

---

**What it costs:** Nothing. Whatismyrock.com is free. Claude and ChatGPT work on the free tier. The BGS map is free.

**How long:** Five minutes for an identification. An afternoon if you tip the box of holiday pebbles out on the kitchen table, which you will.

**Where it goes wrong:** [Accuracy is high for common specimens](https://stonesnapapp.com/blog/best-rock-identifier-apps/) — granite, quartz, basalt, sandstone, obsidian, pyrite — when the photo is sharp. It drops on anything weathered, polished, dyed, or sealed, and on look-alikes: calcite versus quartz, quartzite versus marble. Those need a physical test, and the AI will tell you so. It can't measure hardness, density, or acid reaction from a photo. Add those observations in the notes field and the result improves.

**Why AI beats a field guide:** A field guide shows you what rocks look like in a book. This looks at your actual rock. Before this existed, the options were: join a geological society, buy the Hamlyn Guide and try to match your specimen by eye, or email a photo to a university and wait. None of those usually happened. The rock just sat on the windowsill.

The rocks have been there for years. They just didn't have names.
