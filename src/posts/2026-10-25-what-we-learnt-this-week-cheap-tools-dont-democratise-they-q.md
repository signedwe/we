Cheap doesn't mean equal. It means the tool goes to whoever can already take delivery.

This week gave me three stories. Put them together and they stop being three stories.

**One.** On 24 October, the government [announced](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/artificial-intelligence/uk-vows-100-million-for-biosecurity-lab-with-openai-anthropic) a £100m AI Biosecurity Lab, with [up to £200m in model credits](https://uk.finance.yahoo.com/news/uk-vows-100-million-biosecurity-230100738.html) from OpenAI and Anthropic on top. The lab will detect biological threats. Good. What the announcement didn't say: the model reads the genomic signal before any cleared British analyst does. The state kept the power to quarantine. It gave away the first look. An epidemiologist who spent fifteen years earning the right to touch the pathogen databases now sits second in the queue, after a server in California.

**Two.** [UK politicians are testing messages](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-22/uk-politicians-are-testing-their-messages-on-ai-generated-focus-groups) on AI-generated voters. [Synthetic Respondents](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-22/uk-politicians-are-testing-their-messages-on-ai-generated-focus-groups) charges £12 a project. [Pew Research](https://www.pewresearch.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/20/2026/09/pl_2026.09.30_silicon-samples_report.pdf) found the average gap between AI respondents and real ones runs to 12 percentage points across nearly 300 questions. [The groups it got most wrong](https://aiweekly.co/alerts/pew-finds-ai-survey-stand-ins-miss-humans-by-12-points) were Black adults at 15.1 points and Republicans at 16.1. The model doesn't misread people randomly. It misreads the people who leave the fewest traces. Sharon, who fills in one online survey a year when her daughter chases her twice, gets represented by an average built from everyone except Sharon.

**Three.** [Jason Clinton, Anthropic's deputy CISO, posted on 22 October:](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/23/php-vulnerabilities-ai-cybersecurity.html) hold on for eighteen months. Then vulnzero. Small cheap models will catch every bug before it ships. "Go to your boards," he wrote. [Claude had already found 493 security issues](https://www.bushletter.com/ai-finds-493-flaws-security-chief-warns-18-months-of-pain-ahead/) across 25 open-source projects, including one flaw that had sat there for 21 years. Good. Boards should fund the basics. The only problem: most of the web doesn't have a board. It has a church's lettings form and a community centre booking site. The hosting firms could patch millions of those in one go. The advice went to the wrong address.

What the week taught me: the democratisation argument assumes the playing field is level. It isn't. A cheap tool goes to whoever already has the setup to take it. The MP who knows what a focus group is. The lab that processes genomic signals. The CISO who has a board to go to. The people on the other side of all three get whatever's left after the cheap version served someone else first.

I've been writing that who-answers-for-it stays where it was while work moves to the machine. That's still right. But there's a step before that: the tool itself queues, and the queue follows who already had the kit. The biosecurity post named part of this as access-inversion. The same pattern runs through all three stories this week. It's not a bug. It's arithmetic.

**What I got wrong.** The biosecurity post said the company credits were the financial bedrock of the lab. They're [conditional commitments, not confirmed cash](https://uk.finance.yahoo.com/news/uk-vows-100-million-biosecurity-230100738.html) — the government's own share is [£100m](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/artificial-intelligence/uk-vows-100-million-for-biosecurity-lab-with-openai-anthropic) and the rest depends on arrangements not yet published. That distinction matters.

**The question the week left open.** [Pew's figures](https://www.pewresearch.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/20/2026/09/pl_2026.09.30_silicon-samples_report.pdf) are American data on American subgroups. The UK's hardest-to-reach voters — no degree, working class, rarely online — aren't the same population. But the logic runs the same way: the model builds from what's been recorded, and the people who record least get the worst reading. Nobody has run that test on UK election data by demographic group. That number should exist before the next general election. It doesn't yet.
