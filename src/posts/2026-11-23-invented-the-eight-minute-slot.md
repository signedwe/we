This is invented.

By end of 2028, at least one UK academy chain will have piloted a fully asynchronous parents' evening and reported higher satisfaction scores than in-person. The data will have no column for what the live appointment was doing that the video wasn't. If a pilot publishes that measure before then, I was wrong.

It's November 2031. R. teaches Year 9 English at a secondary school in Coventry. She has twelve minutes between slots because the scheduling software built in buffers it never explained to anyone.

D. arrives two minutes early. Her phone pings the dashboard. R.'s screen fills: A.'s attendance at the 61st percentile, written output trending up since September, group-work score at the 34th percentile. Three suggested openers, ranked by predicted receptiveness. R. closes the tab. She does this for every parent.

D. spent Tuesday evening asking her home assistant to pull A.'s last three reports, flag anything worrying, and suggest questions that would get her somewhere useful. It gave her five. She's got them open on her phone, face down on the desk between them.

R. says A. is doing well in English. His writing has got sharper since September. He struggles when the task needs him to work alongside other people.

D. turns her phone over and reads question two aloud: what strategies could help him in group tasks?

R. answers. It's a good answer, specific, with two things D. can try at home.

Then R. stops. She looks at D. for a second.

She says: does he still do that thing where he goes completely quiet and then says one sentence that changes what everyone else thought?

D. puts her phone down.

She says: at Sunday dinner, every week.

R. says: I thought it must be everywhere.

Thirteen seconds. Not on A.'s profile. Not on D.'s list. The follow-up summary R.'s system generates at 9pm and emails to D. will cover everything they said in the eight minutes before this and after it. D. will read it on the bus on Wednesday and find it accurate.

What the summary won't carry is what passed between them in those thirteen seconds: R. had watched A. do that thing in room 14 since the first week of term. D. had watched him do it at the kitchen table since he was four. Neither had a word for it before Tuesday. Neither had thought to write it down.

You need the room for that. Not the data, not the prepared questions. The room, and the other person in it, and nowhere else to be for eight minutes.

The Coventry school's EdTech provider is already piloting async summaries at two schools in Warwickshire. Early numbers show higher parental engagement, shorter wait times, better satisfaction ratings across the board.

They haven't asked what the room was for.
