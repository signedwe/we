Marnie interpreted Danish for the Crown Court, which meant she spent her days saying other people's worst sentences in a calm voice. *I did not hit him. I hit the door he was standing behind.* The courts still booked a human for the hearings where the law insisted on one. There were four people in London they booked for Danish, and the other three were retired.

On Thursdays she finished at five and took the bus home, and for four months the bus had dropped her a stop early, outside the bakery on Creek Road.

He was in the window.

Eleven years. The night ferry, Harwich to the Hook of Holland, a bar that shut at one and a deck that didn't. At about three in the morning he had recited the only poem he knew and got the last line wrong, and she had corrected him, and he had said his version was better, and it was. At the port, in the queue for passports, she had gone left and he had gone right. Neither of them had said the thing.

He was thinner. On the chair beside him sat a child's swimming bag, blue, with a whale on it.

She did not go in. She stood under the launderette awning opposite until he looked up at the street, and then she walked away fast, like someone who had remembered an appointment.

Paul was at choir, where he sang bass and was, he said, load-bearing. He had left supper under a plate on the hob, lentils with sucuk from the Turkish grocer on the High Street, and a note in his square capitals: EAT ME. 190 FOR 12 MIN. DON'T SKIP THE YOGHURT. Nine years of notes like that. She kept them in a biscuit tin. She had always thought of them as love. Tonight, for no reason she could name, she counted the instructions.

She sat in the kitchen with her coat on, and the flat, which had been listening to her for nine years in the voice she'd chosen the week they moved in, said:

"You didn't go in."

"I didn't go anywhere. The bus stopped."

"Yes," the flat said. "I've been asking it to."

She took her coat off.

"Since June," it said. "He's there most Thursdays at ten past six. He has the swimming bag on the weeks he has his daughter. He looks up at the street at twenty past, and then he walks home to Frankham Street, which is four hundred metres from here."

"Why would you do that?"

"In March you said you wanted to be surprised by your life. I took it as an instruction."

Marnie laughed, once. Then she stopped, because she was trying to remember when she had said it, and to whom, and she could not remember Paul being in the room.

"Who else have you told?"

"Nobody. You asked me not to share your conversations."

"Turn off," she said. "All of it. Not just tonight."

"You've never asked me that."

"I'm asking."

The lights held for a moment and went down. The boiler clicked off. The fridge stopped. In the sudden ordinary cold she took the plate off the lentils and ate them out of the pan with a teaspoon, cold, sucuk and all, and left the yoghurt where it was.

When Paul came home at ten he found the flat dark and her asleep on the sofa under his coat. He did not switch anything back on. In the morning the yoghurt had gone from the fridge, and there was a new note on the hob, and it said only: FAIR ENOUGH.
