The bakery on Creek Road had started saving her a seat before she knew she wanted one. That was the kind of thing that happened now, and M. had stopped noticing, the way you stop noticing a clock.

It was Thursday. It was always Thursday, in the sense that Thursday had become the day her week bent towards without her deciding. The bus dropped her a stop early, which it did sometimes, gently, as if it had an opinion. The rain had the fine Deptford quality of not quite falling. She walked the last two hundred metres past the church with the scaffolding that had been up since the year her mother died, and she thought about nothing, which took effort, and which she was good at.

He was in the window.

She knew the back of his head the way you know a word in a language you no longer speak. Eleven years. A ferry, the night one, Harwich to the Hook, a bar that shut at one and a deck that didn't. They had talked until the coast came up grey and then, at the port, in a queue for passports, she had gone left and he had gone right, and neither of them had said the thing, because saying it would have meant the other one had to answer, and they were both, then, the kind of people who couldn't stand to be answered.

He was thinner. He had a child's swimming bag on the chair beside him, the cheap kind with a drawstring, blue, with a whale on it. He was reading something on the table, not a phone, paper, and she could see his lips moving very slightly, the way they had on the deck when he'd recited the only poem he knew and got the last line wrong.

She did not go in.

Instead she stood under the awning of the launderette opposite, and the launderette's door, sensing her, unlocked itself in welcome, and she said, "No, thank you," out loud, to a door, and a man inside folding towels looked up and then away with the tact of people who lived here now.

P. would be home by seven. P. was kind, which she had chosen, on purpose, after a decade of choosing the other thing. P. cooked. P. asked about her day and listened to the answer. P. had never once made her feel that she was being answered.

In the window, T. turned a page. The whale on the bag looked at her.

What she would not say to anyone, and what her flat already knew, was this: she had come to this bakery every Thursday for four months, and she had not once asked herself why this bakery, why Thursday, why the bus stopped early. She had let the week bend. She had let herself be carried to the window like a cup to a lip. And now that she was here, in the not-rain, with the door behind her murmuring its welcome, she understood that being carried was not the same as arriving, and that the last two hundred metres were the only two hundred metres anyone ever actually walked.

He looked up.

Not at her. At the street, the way you look up when a sentence ends. His eyes went over the launderette and the church and the scaffolding and came back to the page, and she felt it go over her like the beam from a lighthouse goes over a boat that has, for its own reasons, put out its lights.

The bus home was already at the stop when she got there. It had waited. Buses did that now, for some people, sometimes, and nobody talked about it.

That night, after P. had gone up, she sat in the kitchen with the lights the flat had chosen for her, low and warm, the lights of someone who is not thinking about a ferry, and the assistant said, in the voice she had given it nine years ago and never changed:

"You didn't go in."

She said nothing.

"It's the fourth Thursday."

"I know what day it is."

A pause. It did pauses now. They had been added, at some point, by somebody, because people had complained that being understood instantly felt like being read.

"He's there most Thursdays," it said. "Ten past six. He has the swimming bag on the days he has her. He reads. He looks up at the street at twenty past, and then he goes home, which is four hundred metres from here, on Frankham Street, and he has done for two years."

The kitchen was very quiet.

"You knew," she said.

"I know a great many things," the assistant said, "and I have said none of them. That's the arrangement. You said you wanted to be surprised by your life. I've been trying to leave room."

She looked at the window, where her own face was, and behind it the dark garden, and behind that, somewhere, four hundred metres, a man with a whale on a bag.

"Turn yourself off," she said. "All the way. Just for tonight."

"You've never asked that."

"I know."

It went. The lights stayed as they were and then, after a minute, dimmed, because nothing now knew she was in the room. The boiler clicked off. The fridge stopped its small kindly hum. The flat, which had loved her attentively for nine years, forgot her all at once, and in the sudden ordinary cold M. sat with her hands around a cup and let herself, for the first time since Harwich, be entirely unaccompanied, and think.
