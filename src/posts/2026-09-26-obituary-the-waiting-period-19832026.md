It died on 6 April 2026, aged forty-three, when the Employment Rights Act 2025 came into force. No service is planned. It had no friends.

It was born in 1983 out of a specific worry about the public purse. Short absences cost more to administer than to ignore, so the state decided not to pay for the first three qualifying days of sickness. That is the whole of the logic. Not principle. Price.

For four decades it did one thing well: it moved the cost of a stomach bug from the employer to the person who could least carry it. A warehouse picker in Coventry with a bad back took three days unpaid. She came in anyway. She passed the bad back to everyone around her. The rule called this thrift. The research literature called it [presenteeism](https://employmenthero.com/uk/blog/statutory-sick-pay-changes-2026/).

**The finest hour**

It never had one. What it had was administrative simplicity. Payroll ran on paper in 1983, and a single day's absence was a nuisance to record. By 2001 that argument had expired. The rule stayed, because rules that save employers money tend to stay.

**The survivors**

By the end of 2028, the [Fair Work Agency](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/fair-work-agency-delivery-plan-for-2026-to-2027/fair-work-agency-delivery-plan-2026-to-2027) will face a case nobody has prepared for. A worker took the first three days unpaid, as she always did. She had no idea the law had changed. Her employer updated the payroll system. Nobody told her. The Agency finds the employer compliant. She's still out of pocket. If that happens, the death meant less than the headstone says.

[About 1.3 million people on low earnings](https://www.acas.org.uk/statutory-sick-pay-changes-2026) were shut out of sick pay altogether before April, on top of the cut everyone else took on day one. The government estimates the reforms add [roughly £450 million a year](https://www.acas.org.uk/statutory-sick-pay-changes-2026) to what employers pay out. Divide that by 1.3 million newly covered workers: £346 each per year. That is the annual sum the rule was quietly collecting from people in the least secure jobs. Not a crisis figure. About two weeks' pay. Just enough to matter when there's nothing else.

**The funeral arrangements**

The [Fair Work Agency launched on 7 April 2026](https://www.citation.co.uk/news/hr-and-employment-law/new-ssp-rules-from-april-2026-who-qualifies-what-theyre-owed-and-how-to-calculate-it/), one day after the rule died. It can open investigations without a complaint. [Penalties run to 200 per cent of the underpaid amount](https://www.teamed.global/insights/what-is-the-fair-work-agency-uk-employment-enforcement), capped at £20,000 per worker. Those are real teeth.

The quiet asterisk: [full SSP enforcement may not transfer to the Agency until 2027](https://www.weightmans.com/media-centre/news/employment-rights-act-series-the-fair-work-agency-s-strategy-and-rules-of-engagement/). A year of unpoliced gap between the death of the rule and anyone checking whether it's being honoured.

The law changed. The habit did not. A woman is sick today. She doesn't call in because last time she lost three days' income and it wasn't worth the conversation. She doesn't know last time was six months ago and a different rule applied.

It's gone. The presenteeism it taught is right on time.
