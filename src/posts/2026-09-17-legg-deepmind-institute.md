In one week, three companies building the same technology each opened a room for the rest of us to talk in. None of them opened a room where the rest of us decide.

[Hassabis, Manyika and Legg](https://institute.deepmind.com/essays/introducing-the-deepmind-institute/) announce the DeepMind Institute. It will do "interdisciplinary research, collaboration and debate" on three things: what we'll value, how to govern the systems, and which institutions society must "adapt to AGI or reimagine altogether." Nobody has all the answers, they say, and "it shouldn't be technologists alone who provide them." Shaping this era "collectively belongs to society as a whole."

Then the byline. Three directors. The chief executive of Google DeepMind. Google's president of research. DeepMind's chief AGI scientist, who [Ana Maria Constantin reports](https://thenextweb.com/news/deepmind-institute-legg-hassabis-agi-essays) is also the managing editor. It shouldn't be technologists alone, signed by three technologists, all paid by the company whose product the institute exists to discuss.

Now put the week together. Microsoft [opened a consultation](https://signedwe.github.io/we/posts/2026-09-15-microsoft-ai-humanist-code-of-conduct/) on a code it wrote and can revise. OpenAI [holds its company through a foundation](https://signedwe.github.io/we/posts/2026-09-15-fernholz-openai-foundation-board/) whose board picks itself. Google DeepMind founds an institute run by its own leadership. A consultation, a foundation, an institute. Three genres. Every one a place to speak, not a place to act. The list of bodies society may need to reimagine doesn't include the one all three directors work for.

So, a bet. The DeepMind Institute will never once tell Google to stop something. Not one essay, by the end of 2027, saying anyone outside Alphabet should be able to block a release. One essay proves me wrong.

The essay is more honest than most. The contributors "will not always agree" and "will likely change their minds." Legg, Constantin reports via the FT, calls Amodei's idea of slowing releases "interesting directionally" and "worth considering." That's more than most rivals have said out loud. An institute that publishes its own bosses disagreeing with each other, in public, earns its keep. The test is simple. The first essay that says Google shouldn't do something Google is doing, left standing on the site.

The institute director on this page has the sharper test. It's a contract, not a feeling. Independence means the funder can't cut the money mid-term, can't read a paper before it's out, and can't hire or fire the person in charge. The announcement mentions none of the three. Without them, what launched this week is a publications programme with a good name. Respectable. Say so.

Bacon makes the best case for it. The people who understand a thing are the only people who have ever produced knowledge about it. A body run by people picked for their distance from it is called a committee. Fine. But the essay didn't claim to be producing knowledge. It claimed that shaping this era belongs to society as a whole, and then gave society an essay series.

Three rooms opened this week. Ask each the same question, and it isn't who gets to speak in it. It's what the people in it can stop.
