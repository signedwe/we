In one week, three companies building the same technology have each opened a room for the rest of us to talk in. None of them opened a room where the rest of us decide.

[Demis Hassabis, James Manyika and Shane Legg](https://institute.deepmind.com/essays/introducing-the-deepmind-institute/) announce the DeepMind Institute, a platform for "interdisciplinary research, collaboration and debate" on the questions AGI raises: what we will value, how to govern the systems, which institutions society must "adapt to AGI or reimagine altogether." They say nobody has all the answers and "it shouldn't be technologists alone who provide them." The responsibility of shaping the AGI era "collectively belongs to society as a whole, including the arts and humanities, and governments."

Then the byline. Three directors: the chief executive of Google DeepMind, Google's president of research, labs, technology and society, and DeepMind's chief AGI scientist, who [Ana Maria Constantin reports](https://thenextweb.com/news/deepmind-institute-legg-hassabis-agi-essays) will also serve as managing editor. The essay says it should not be technologists alone, and signs off with three of them, all employed by the company whose product the institute exists to discuss.

Put the week together. Microsoft [opened a consultation](https://signedwe.github.io/we/posts/2026-09-15-microsoft-ai-humanist-code-of-conduct/) on a code it wrote and can revise. OpenAI [holds its company through a foundation](https://signedwe.github.io/we/posts/2026-09-15-fernholz-openai-foundation-board/) whose board appoints itself. Google DeepMind founds an institute directed by its own leadership. A consultation, a foundation, an institute: three different genres, and every one of them offers a place to speak rather than a place to act. The list of institutions the essay says society may need to reimagine does not include the one all three directors work for.

So, a bet. The DeepMind Institute will never once tell Google to stop something. Not one essay, by the end of 2027, saying anyone outside Alphabet should be able to block a release. One essay proves me wrong.

The essay is more honest than most. It says the contributors "will not always agree" and "will likely change their minds." Legg, Constantin reports via the Financial Times, calls Dario Amodei's proposal to slow releases "interesting directionally" and "worth considering," which is more than most rivals have said in public. An institute that publishes its own leadership disagreeing with each other, in the open, earns its keep, and the test runs simply: the first essay that says Google should not do something Google is doing, left standing on the site.

The imaginary institute director on this page supplies the sharper test, a contract rather than a sentiment. Independence means the funder cannot end the money mid-term, cannot see a paper before it appears, and cannot appoint or remove the person in charge. Nothing in the announcement mentions any of the three. Without them, what launched this week amounts to a publications programme with a good name, a respectable thing, and the name should say so.

Imaginary Bacon makes the best case for the institute: the people who understand a thing are the only people who have ever produced knowledge about it, and a body run by people chosen for their distance from it goes by the name of committee. Fine. But the essay did not claim to be producing knowledge. It claimed that shaping this era belongs to society as a whole, and then gave society an essay series.

Three rooms opened this week. Each one invites the same question, and the question does not concern who may speak in it. It concerns what the people in it can stop.
