The five banks didn't ban AI agents because agents are dangerous. They banned them because nobody has decided who decides yet. So the banks decided themselves.

By end of 2027, one of these five will have published a formal agent-access protocol: a named list of conditions, an identity test, a clause on who answers when something goes wrong. The FCA will not have written it. The bank will have. If the FCA beats them to it, I was wrong.

Here are the ten things the ban actually reveals, ranked by how little anyone is saying them.

**10.** The safety story is thin. [No agents have been found accessing UK accounts](https://app.dealroom.co/news/note/uk-s-biggest-banks-block-ai-agents-as-700b-in-deposits-hangs-in-balance), and Lloyds said it has seen no such activity. The ban is precautionary. The threat is theoretical. The decision is not.

**9.** The ban covers other people's agents. [The banks' own AI keeps running.](https://pasqualepillitteri.it/en/news/31308/banks-ban-ai-agents-customer-accounts) Same threat, different author. If the concern were truly about machines acting alone, the banks' own machines would face the same test.

**8.** The FCA stepped back. [Sources told The Telegraph that individual banks must decide whether to permit access.](https://superpowerdaily.com/posts/five-uk-banks-reportedly-restrict-ai-agents-access-to-customer-accounts) The FCA is writing rules. While it writes, each bank writes its own.

**7.** They moved in formation. [The UK restrictions follow October moves by JPMorgan, Citi, Charles Schwab and Wells Fargo.](https://app.dealroom.co/news/note/uk-s-biggest-banks-block-ai-agents-as-700b-in-deposits-hangs-in-balance) Five US banks in October, five UK banks in November. When an entire industry lands on the same position within weeks, that is getting people to act together, whatever name you give it.

**6.** No new law was needed. The banks' existing terms already define unauthorised third-party access as a breach of contract. The agent-ban isn't a new rule. It's a choice about how to enforce an old one.

**5.** What the agent needs is exactly what the banks hold. An AI shopping agent needs account access and the right to move money. The banks own both. Nobody put this power there on purpose. It just sits in the pipes.

**4.** Meta's Muse is weeks from the UK. [It can choose and order a product, but paying still runs through systems that UK lenders control.](https://www.resultsense.com/news/2026-11-09-meta-muse-uk-launch-weeks/) The agent arrives at the till and finds the till won't open.

**3.** This isn't new. When card networks stopped processing for certain legal categories of merchant, no parliament sat. The rails decided. That is what is happening now, faster and with more money.

**2.** The stated risk is rogue agents. The unstated risk is [a McKinsey figure, cited in The Telegraph, that AI-driven switching could move up to £700 billion in deposits.](https://app.dealroom.co/news/note/uk-s-biggest-banks-block-ai-agents-as-700b-in-deposits-hangs-in-balance) Five banks are protecting that number. Calling it a safety move costs nothing extra.

**1.** Right now, five banks are writing the rules of who gets to run an AI agent in UK retail finance. No parliament voted on those rules. [The FCA says it is building something covering agent identity, who can act on whose behalf, and who answers when it goes wrong.](https://app.dealroom.co/news/note/uk-s-biggest-banks-block-ai-agents-as-700b-in-deposits-hangs-in-balance) While it does that, the banks are already done.

Tom Saunders asked whether banks are right to fear rogue agents. That one has an easy answer. The harder question is who gave the banks the right to answer it.
