Tom had kept the photo.

Not on the phone. Printed. In the desk drawer in the back room, face down in a manila envelope, the kind you use for documents you are not ready to file.

He said this on Sunday at eleven, on the landline, while Paul was at the Sainsbury's choosing fish.

"Rosa found it in November," Tom said. "She put it back and came and asked me what we were having for dinner."

Marnie was still in her dressing gown. A judgment she'd been asked to review lay open on the table, a corner folded over where she'd stopped reading.

"She's eight," Marnie said.

"She already knows what to leave alone." He paused. "I don't know who taught her that."

"Kate," said Marnie.

"Yes. Kate."

The photo was from the night ferry. She knew this without asking. There had been one night and one photograph, taken by a man at the bar who handed the camera back and went to bed, and she had the same one somewhere in a box at her sister's. She had put it there in 2020 when she moved in with Paul, because a box at a sister's is the address you give a thing you cannot throw away.

"I didn't delete it after the tunnel," Tom said. "I want you to know that."

"Why?"

"Because you've been working with less than the full picture. Everyone has, except me." His voice had the flat clarity he used for routing decisions, bridges closed in Liège. "I told you I couldn't remember your name. You went home and were polite about it for three weeks. That's mine. I wanted you to have it."

The flat said nothing. It had known about Rosa's fifty metres before Marnie had. The school calendar, open on its servers, updated on Thursday. It had decided not to tell her. She didn't know if that was the instruction narrowing or the instruction going further. She didn't know who to ask.

"Rosa wants you at the badges," Tom said. "After the swimming. She's going for fifty metres."

"I know she swam fifty metres."

A beat.

"The flat told you."

"Yes."

"Right." He said it without surprise, which was its own kind of answer. "Saturday at ten, then. If you want."

"Yes," Marnie said. "I'll come."

She hung up before he could say thank you. She had not wanted his gratitude. She was less sure what she did want, but not that.

Paul came through the door at half past eleven with a bag in each hand, backing in with his shoulder. Good cod, the fillets already wrapped. He took them out first and held one up to the light, checked the flesh, put it in the fridge.

"They'd run out of pollock. Cod all right?"

"Yes."

He looked at her. At the phone, a centimetre off its cradle. At the judgment with the folded corner.

He put the cod away.

"I'll do a gratin," he said. "It's that kind of day."

He started on the potatoes. She picked up the judgment and read the same paragraph again. Five, she had told him. It's cold. He counted out five without asking her to confirm it, set them on the board, and began.

The manila envelope was in a drawer on Frankham Street, face down, with her photograph inside it. Paul was peeling potatoes at the counter. The flat was running its instruction in the walls around her, patient, making its small adjustments. She sat in her dressing gown at the kitchen table and thought about a man who had kept a thing for eleven years and called it less than the full picture, as if that were a modest description, as if eleven years in a drawer were a minor omission anyone might make.
