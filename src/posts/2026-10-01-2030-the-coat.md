Kate arrived in a good coat, with no hurry about her.

Marnie had been ready for damage. Someone cold, or worse, someone polished and fair about it. She had been readying herself for three days, since the tunnel, and it had made her slow going up the stairs, which she noticed and resented.

Kate was forty-two. She had a face that had settled into something dry and unsurprised. She came through the door of Tom's flat with Rosa's swimming bag and a box from the Turkish place on the high street, which she set on the counter without ceremony.

"Rosa," she said.

"Mum." Rosa was in the hall putting on her shoes, one foot braced on the wall, in the way Tom must have decided not to fight.

Kate looked at Marnie. It was not the examination Marnie had been steeling herself against. It was brief and almost technical, the kind of look a locksmith gives a door that has already been opened.

"You're the interpreter," Kate said.

"Yes."

"Danish."

"Yes."

"Rosa tells me you taught her the Danish for seagull," Kate said.

"Måge."

"She's been saying it to pigeons mostly. She says they don't mind."

Rosa appeared, shod. "The pigeon on the wall at school is called Pieter."

"You named it," Kate said.

"He came back three times. That's a relationship."

Tom was standing in the kitchen doorway. He said he'd get her bag and went.

Kate looked at Marnie again. Unhurried.

"He's better," she said. Not quite to Marnie. Not quite to the room. "He was useless before Christmas."

Marnie said: "Good."

"Rosa didn't notice. That was the point."

She looked at the box on the counter. "Spinach börek. They'd sold out of the cheese." A pause. "There's enough for three. I didn't plan that, so don't read into it."

Marnie thought: she knows. Not assembled it from clues, not suspected. Knows.

Tom came back carrying Rosa's school bag and overnight case and a drawing on a roll that Rosa immediately grabbed and uncurled, creasing it.

"It's the seagull," Rosa said. "Marnie helped."

Kate looked at it. The bird was enormous and almost entirely beak.

"Remarkable," Kate said.

"I know." Rosa rolled it back, neat and fast, with the confidence of someone who has been told their work is good and believes it.

Kate picked up the swimming bag. She and Tom looked at each other and some agreement passed between them, efficient and without warmth, the residue of a long shared habit neither of them had bothered to unlearn.

"Thank you," Kate said to Marnie. Even. Correct.

"Måge," Rosa said to the window. No birds.

The door closed.

Tom and Marnie stood in the flat, which was very quiet. The börek sat on the counter.

Enough for three.
