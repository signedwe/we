Kate arrived in a good coat, with no hurry about her.

Marnie had come to Frankham Street on the Tuesday to return a library book Rosa had left at supper, and had been asked in, and had said yes, and was now standing in Tom's kitchen when the child's mother let herself in with her own key.

She had been ready for damage. Someone cold, or worse, someone polished and fair about it. What came through the door was a woman of forty-two with a dry, unsurprised face, Rosa's swimming bag over her shoulder and a box from the Turkish bakery on the High Street, which she set down on the counter.

"Rosa," she said.

"Mum." Rosa was in the hall with one foot braced on the wall, doing up a shoe.

Kate looked at Marnie. It was brief and almost technical.

"You're the interpreter."

"Yes."

"Danish."

"Yes."

"Rosa says you taught her the Danish for seagull."

"*Måge*."

"She says it to pigeons. She says they don't mind."

Rosa appeared. "The pigeon on the wall at school is called Pieter."

"You named it," Kate said.

"He came back three times. That's a relationship."

Tom said he'd get Rosa's bag and went. He was gone a long time for a bag.

Kate didn't fill the silence. Marnie, who filled silences for a living, found she couldn't either.

"He's better," Kate said at last. Not to Marnie exactly. "He was useless before Christmas."

"Good," Marnie said, and heard how stupid it was.

"Rosa didn't notice. That was the point."

She looked at the box on the counter. "Spinach börek. They'd sold out of the cheese." A pause. "There's enough for three. I didn't plan that, so don't read into it."

Marnie read the woman as cold, and was glad of it, because cold was easier. It would be a week before she understood she had been watching someone being careful.

Tom came back with the school bag and the overnight case and a drawing rolled into a tube, which Rosa seized and unrolled on the counter. A seagull, enormous, almost entirely beak.

"Remarkable," Kate said.

"I know," said Rosa.

Kate and Tom looked at each other over the drawing, and some agreement passed between them, efficient and without warmth.

At the door Kate stopped, turned, and came back into the kitchen. She took a knife from the block, cut the börek into four, put one piece on a plate, and held the plate out to Marnie.

"Four," she said. "In case. Eat that one now, while I'm here, so I know you did."

Marnie ate it standing up, with Kate watching, and Rosa watching, and Tom looking at the floor. It was still warm. Kate nodded once, as if a form had been signed, and took her daughter home.
