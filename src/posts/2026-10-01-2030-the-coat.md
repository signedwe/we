K. arrived in a good coat, with no hurry about her.

M. had been ready for damage. Someone cold, or worse, someone polished and fair about it. She had been readying herself for three days, since the tunnel, and it had made her slow going up the stairs, which she noticed and resented.

K. was forty-two. She had a face that had settled into something dry and unsurprised. She came through the door of T.'s flat with Rosa's swimming bag and a box from the Turkish place on the high street, which she set on the counter without ceremony.

"Rosa," she said.

"Mum." Rosa was in the hall putting on her shoes, one foot braced on the wall, in the way T. must have decided not to fight.

K. looked at M. It was not the examination M. had been steeling herself against. It was brief and almost technical, the kind of look a locksmith gives a door that has already been opened.

"You're the interpreter," K. said.

"Yes."

"Danish."

"Yes."

"Rosa tells me you taught her the Danish for seagull," K. said.

"Måge."

"She's been saying it to pigeons mostly. She says they don't mind."

Rosa appeared, shod. "The pigeon on the wall at school is called Pieter."

"You named it," K. said.

"He came back three times. That's a relationship."

T. was standing in the kitchen doorway. He said he'd get her bag and went.

K. looked at M. again. Unhurried.

"He's doing better," she said. Not quite to M. Not quite to the room. "He was quite bad for a while. Before Christmas."

M. said: "Good."

"Rosa didn't know. That was the main thing."

She looked at the box on the counter. "It's spinach börek. They'd sold out of the cheese." A pause. "There's enough for three."

M. thought: she knows. Not assembled it from clues, not suspected. Knows.

T. came back carrying Rosa's school bag and overnight case and a drawing on a roll that Rosa immediately grabbed and uncurled, creasing it.

"It's the seagull," Rosa said. "M. helped."

K. looked at it. The bird was enormous and almost entirely beak.

"Remarkable," K. said.

"I know." Rosa rolled it back, neat and fast, with the confidence of someone who has been told their work is good and believes it.

K. picked up the swimming bag. She and T. looked at each other and some agreement passed between them, efficient and without warmth, the residue of a long shared habit neither of them had bothered to unlearn.

"Thank you," K. said to M. Even. Correct.

"Måge," Rosa said to the window. No birds.

The door closed.

T. and M. stood in the flat, which was very quiet. The börek sat on the counter.

Enough for three.
