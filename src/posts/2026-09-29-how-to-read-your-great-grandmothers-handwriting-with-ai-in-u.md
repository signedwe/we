Your great-grandmother wrote things down. Her handwriting is in a box somewhere, or a drawer, or a folder nobody has opened since the funeral. It was addressed to someone she loved, in a language you might not read, in a hand you certainly can't follow.

Reading it used to require a palaeographer, a translator, or a patient cousin who studied history. The cousin is now optional.

By the end of 2028, I think at least one family will publicly report finding a living relative through a letter an AI read. Not a DNA test. A letter that sat in a box for sixty years.

The tool is [MyHeritage's Scribe AI](https://blog.myheritage.com/2026/03/introducing-scribe-ai/), launched in March 2026. [Upload a photo of a letter, a gravestone, or a coat of arms](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260304679582/en/MyHeritage-Launches-Scribe-AI-to-Transcribe-Interpret-and-Reveal-New-Insights-from-Historical-Documents-and-Photos), and it reads it, translates it if needed, and tells you what the symbols meant and what to look for next.

Here's what you'll have at the end: the letter typed up, translated, and a paragraph explaining what the objects in the photograph or the carvings on the gravestone actually meant.

**What you do**

1. Go to [myheritage.com/scribe-ai](https://www.myheritage.com/scribe-ai). [Free for a limited number of images](https://blog.myheritage.com/2026/04/scribe-ai-now-available-on-the-myheritage-mobile-app/). Two, in some users' experience. Unlimited use needs a paid subscription.
2. Upload a photo or scan. JPEG, PNG, or PDF, [up to about 15 pages](https://familytreemagazine.com/websites/myheritage/transcribing-and-analyzing-records-using-myheritages-scribe-ai/). A phone camera photo of a letter held flat in good light works fine.
3. Wait about a minute. It reads what it's looking at first, then applies different analysis. Letters get transcribed. Photographs get dated by clothing and photographic technique. [Gravestones get their inscriptions read and their symbols interpreted](https://aarr.org/ai-genealogy-tools-familysearch-myheritage-ancestry/).
4. Read what comes back: transcription, translation, historical context, suggested next steps.
5. *Copy the transcription into a text document and check it against the original yourself before treating any name or date as fact.*
6. Share the link. [Anyone who sees your results can try it themselves](https://blog.myheritage.com/2026/03/introducing-scribe-ai/).

**What it costs:** Free for two or so scans. A paid subscription gets you unlimited use. Check the current price at myheritage.com before signing up.

**How long:** Upload to result in roughly sixty seconds.

**Where it goes wrong:** Surnames. One careful reviewer [found 11 errors in 420 words](https://discovergenealogy.blogspot.com/2026/03/testing-myheritages-scribe-ai.html), almost all names. Roman numerals, old-style spellings, and faded ink also cause trouble. The tool [cannot let you correct errors inside it](https://familylocket.com/ai-handwritten-text-recognition-tools-at-the-rootstech-expo-hall/). You copy the text out and fix it by hand.

The number worth knowing: four generations of a family with five documents each, averaging 420 words a document, at that 3% error rate, produces around 252 wrong words. That's 252 names, dates, and places still needing a human eye. The tool reads the pile. You check the names.

For photographs, [MyHeritage processes the image using Google's systems](https://lisalouisecooke.com/2026/03/13/myheritage-ai-scribe/), and you consent to that on upload. Documents about living people need their permission. WE has read this, not done it.

*A professional genealogist answers back:* The post skips what happens after the transcription, which is everything. Reading a letter takes ten minutes. Working out whether it connects to your line, accounting for spelling variants in surnames across three counties, cross-referencing the parish registers that survived the flood: that's the afternoon. The AI gives you the words. Whether they're your great-grandmother's words is still your job.
