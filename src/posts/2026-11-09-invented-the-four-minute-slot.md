This is invented. 2031. None of the people are real.

---

The report arrived on the Thursday before, two days early. Four pages in the app: R.'s reading age, her maths trajectory, her predicted grade at GCSE in 2033, and what the platform called her *engagement profile*. That last one meant whether she'd looked at her screen during independent work time. Mrs O. had forty-three of these to read before Saturday morning.

She read them on her phone on the bus home Friday, the way she used to read exercise books.

The school had moved parents' evenings from seven minutes to four. The logic was in the welcome email: *since the personalised learning report now delivers the core data in advance, the appointment focuses on questions and next steps.* The governors had voted on it in October. Mrs O. had not been at that meeting.

R.'s mother came in at nine-forty. She'd printed the report. Some parents did that now.

"She's doing well," R.'s mother said, before Mrs O. had said anything. "The maths thing was a surprise."

The maths thing. The platform had flagged R. as underperforming relative to her reading age. Technically true. It was also true that R. had spent the first week of term helping a boy called D. in the row ahead understand long division, carefully and quietly, across four lessons, before Mrs O. said a word about it. D. was now ahead of his predicted trajectory. The platform had noticed D. It had not noticed what R. did to get him there.

Mrs O. tried to explain this in four minutes.

"So she's actually fine," R.'s mother said.

The timer on the desk was an app now too. It made no noise. It just changed colour.

"Can I book a longer slot?"

The booking system had three options. Standard, four minutes. Extended, eight, available to families with SEND designations or formal concerns logged in the platform. And a third: *Wellbeing conversation*, twelve minutes, requiring a head-of-year referral. Mrs O. had used that pathway four times this term. All four children the system had rated as performing adequately.

R.'s mother did not qualify under any of them.

"I'll put a note in her file," Mrs O. said. "The human-readable one."

There were two files now. The structured one the platform read, which fed the report, which went home two days before the appointment. And a text box at the bottom of the teacher's interface that the platform did not parse. Mrs O. used it every week. She was not sure anyone read it.

After the last appointment she sat in the hall while the caretaker stacked chairs. Thirty-nine of her forty-three children, the platform had read correctly. The other four it rated fine were the four she was most worried about. Not because of their grades. Because of the way each of them looked at the board when something confused them, as though the confusion were their own fault.

That look wasn't in the engagement profile. Mrs O. didn't know how you'd put it in.

The platform had reduced the meeting to what it could already see. What was left was the part it couldn't. Four minutes. And somewhere above her, in a governor's report she would never read, that compression was described as an efficiency gain.
