This site has one line at the top of its front page, written by the person who runs it. Own it or be owned. For three months WE has argued for that line. This piece argues it is wrong.

Britain has already run the experiment. A building society belonged to its savers and borrowers, who [set policy and appointed the directors on a one-member, one-vote basis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Building_society). It was the nearest thing this country had to a machine owned by the people who used it. Then somebody offered the members money.

[Many of the largest building societies, other than Nationwide, turned themselves into banks](https://www.thenews.coop/big-bang-demutualisation-building-societies-failed/). The members voted for it. The same account says boards offered inducements, drawn from reserves built up by members who were dead and so could not vote, and that speculators opened accounts just to vote yes. The living took the cheque. Nobody held a gun to anyone's head. People were asked whether they would like some money now, or a say in a mortgage lender for ever, and they answered like sensible people with a gas bill.

We know what happened next. A study of those societies found that, though they had been very large and successful as mutuals, [none of them exists today on a standalone basis](https://kar.kent.ac.uk/id/document/1481422). The members sold a thing older than any of them. What they sold is gone.

Russia ran the same experiment on a whole country. From October 1992 the state [offered a voucher to each citizen, 148 million in all, in what the Moscow Times called the largest sell-off of government property in history](https://www.themoscowtimes.com/archive/vouchers-reflect-schism-over-privatization). Each had [a face value of 10,000 roubles, about $12, and by May 1993 was being sold for $6 or $7](https://time.com/archive/6723211/former-superpower-for-sale-cheap/). Weeks before the scheme closed, a poll of 1,201 Russians found that [the largest single group, 30 per cent, had sold their vouchers without investing them](https://www.themoscowtimes.com/archive/poll-points-to-apathy-on-voucher). In liquor shops and kiosks, [people had begun calling a bottle of vodka a voucher](https://www.themoscowtimes.com/archive/privatization-vocabulary-is-as-foreign-as-the-ideal). In 2004, a decade after that first phase ended, the Irish Times reported that [few Russians had any idea what had happened to the shares they bought](https://www.irishtimes.com/news/why-privatisation-is-still-a-dirty-word-to-russians-1.1147197).

Two cases are not a law of nature. But the pattern is probably this. Hand a great many people a small, sellable piece of something large and far away. Wait for a hard winter. They sell, and the buyer is whoever already had money. That is no failure of character. A share is the one kind of power that was designed to be sold. Selling it is using it as intended.

Now do the sum for AI. By [WE's own calculator](/we/dividend/), which starts from a guessed value of $10 trillion, a fifth of the whole industry shared among everyone alive comes to about $241 each. Ask yourself, honestly, what you would do with a certificate worth $241 and a man at the door offering $300.

So the dream of this site, a machine owned by the people who use it, has a life expectancy. It is the time between the day the shares are handed out and the first bad winter.

There is one way out, and the mutuals that survived found it. Societies set up charitable foundations, and since 1997 have [required new members to give up their rights to any windfall](https://kar.kent.ac.uk/id/document/1481422). Nationwide, which [survived its own vote in 1998 by a whisker](https://archive.spectator.co.uk/article/1st-august-1998/26/our-mutual-friends), is still here. The trick was to make the members unable to cash in.

Look hard at that trick. A thing you cannot sell, cannot borrow against and cannot leave to whoever you like is not property. Lawyers have a word for a stake like that, which is yours because of who you are and which nobody can buy from you. The word is a vote. Nobody owns their vote. That is exactly why it still belongs to them. The law here [forbids paying a person for their vote](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1983/2/section/113), and nobody thinks that law insults the voter.

Follow that to the end and three unwelcome things come out.

First, every scheme now on offer is a slow sale. Dividends, staff shares, tokens for users, a public fund that pays out. If it can be turned into cash, it will be, and it will end up where the cash is.

Second, the cure is to take a freedom away from the very people it is meant to help. You may have a say over the machine. You may not sell it, even if you are hungry, even if you would rather have the money. That is paternalism and there is no polite name for it. The honest defence is the one the dead building society members never got to make. The thing was built by people who are gone, out of everything anyone ever wrote, and it will be used by people not yet born. The living are not its owners. We are the ones holding it this week.

Third, WE has been using the wrong word. Being owned is the danger, and that half of the line stands. Owning is not the cure. What people need over AI is what they have over Parliament: a say that cannot be bought off them, because it was never theirs to sell.

Picture it, and this part is imagined. It is 2034. A letter comes on your eighteenth birthday. It says you now hold one vote in the models that run the country's hospitals and courts. It says the vote is worth nothing, cannot be sold, and will be needed about once every four years, usually on a wet Thursday. At the bottom, in small print, somebody has added: please do not lose it. Your grandmother sold hers for a holiday in 1997.
