"The Esbjerg boat, wasn't it," said Paul, sitting down, answering for her. "Before we met. She went to Denmark to learn to swear properly."

He set the orange juice in front of her.

The Esbjerg boat had stopped running years before her crossing. She had taken the Hook. Paul almost never had anything wrong, and he had this wrong, and she held the cold bottle and let his answer stand. In the half-second she didn't correct it, it became the crossing she'd taken.

Later she would wonder whether he had got it wrong at all.

"I did the Hook," Tom said to Paul, easily. "Night boat. Years ago."

"Oh, lovely. Was it rough?"

"No. It didn't rain." Tom said it to Paul and didn't look at her. "There was a cabin I didn't use. I was up on deck all night."

"Why didn't you use the cabin?"

"I was talking to someone."

Paul nodded, delighted, sipping his hot chocolate through the good side of his mouth. He loved other people's stories the way some men love football, loyally, with no need to win. He asked about the bar, about the dark, whether it was true about the phosphorescence. Tom answered every question. He was a good witness. He said nothing false and nothing complete.

Marnie drank her juice. It tasted, as it always had, of a tin.

Then Paul did something she had not seen coming, which in nine years was a short list.

"Come to supper," he said to Tom. "Saturday. Bring your daughter, if she eats fish. I do a hake with butter beans that Marnie has been politely eating for eight years. Our flat's forgotten how to have people round."

Tom looked at Marnie. Marnie looked at the orange on the label.

"We'd love to," Tom said.

"Good." Paul went to pay, tapped his wrist to the reader, and checked the amount before he moved on, as he always did.

Tom put his coat on. At the door he turned back.

"Next Thursday?"

It was addressed to the table. Either of them could have picked it up.

"Probably," she said.

Outside it was properly cold, the first cold of the year. Paul put his hand out without looking and she took it without thinking. He didn't ask about Tom. He asked whether they still had the big dish, the oval one, for the fish.

"Under the sink."

"Good." A few steps on, in the voice he used for the weather: "He looks at you like you owe him a book."

She stopped walking. Paul did not. He let go of her hand, walked on three or four paces, and turned round under the streetlight with his hands in his pockets, waiting, perfectly pleasant, for her to catch up.

She caught up.
