T.'s question was still in the air when P. answered it.

"The Hook of Holland route, wasn't it."

Not a question. He set a flat white and a small orange juice on the table, juice for M. because he still knew without asking, and drew a chair across with the ease of a man who had never once made anyone count the cost of a small kindness.

M. picked up the glass. She had not taken the Hook of Holland crossing. She had taken Harwich to Esbjerg. The longer one. Open sea, overnight, the one where you could stand at the stern at two in the morning and watch the wake close behind you until there was no trace of it. She held the glass and let P.'s answer sit there, and in that half-second it became the crossing she had taken, and she understood that this was how it would go.

"I went Esbjerg," T. said. "Night crossing."

P. leaned forward. He asked about the ship, the cabin, the length of it, whether the sea was rough in March. T. answered steadily, with the patience of someone glad to be asked.

The cabins were very small, he said. There was a porthole.

He said *porthole* the way you say a word that belongs to one specific night and nowhere else, and M. looked at the juice bottle label, a small drawn fruit in a style that had not changed in thirty years, and thought: he has not forgotten as much as he claimed.

"We had the boat nearly to ourselves," T. said. "Off-season. March."

P. said that was the best way to do it. He said there was something about crossing water with almost no one else aboard. He began to say it reminded him of long train rides through the night, then stopped himself and said no, actually, that wasn't right, you could still see the land on a train, it wasn't the same thing at all.

M. watched T. register this. The small correction P. had made to his own sentence. T.'s expression was the one she had seen three Thursdays ago, when she had first understood they were talking about something they were also not talking about.

At the counter behind T., the display showed the week's specials in a pale typeface that adjusted its brightness to the room. It had been doing this for eighteen months. Nobody looked at it anymore. That was how it worked best.

"We should get going," P. said. To M., not to T., though the *we* settled over the table like a hand laid flat.

He went to the counter. He tapped his inner wrist once against the reader. A small private audit he ran on every transaction, index finger checking the confirmation before he moved on. The cashier said thank you with the lift in it that meant the amount was correct.

M. stood and put on her coat. T. looked up from his cup.

"Next Thursday?"

The question occupied a careful middle distance. Addressed to the table, collectible by either of them, deniable as an appointment if it needed to be. She had used that grammar herself. She knew its weight to the gram.

"Probably," she said.

Outside, the cold came fast. P. put his hand out and she took it without thinking, the reflex of two people who had been cold together many times before, and they walked toward the High Street. He did not ask about T. He built instead the space he built around things he had looked at and chosen not to name. Load-bearing, carefully edged, just wide enough for both of them. They walked inside it.

It held.

For now, it held.
