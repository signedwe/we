By the end of 2027, the Bank of England's Financial Policy Committee will publish a stress scenario in which an AI earnings miss hits sovereign debt markets. They've already named the risk. The step from named risk to numbered scenario is when they start running figures against it.

Here's why that bet costs something.

The BBC [reported Bailey's interview on 1 October](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-01/boe-governor-warns-ai-boom-could-trigger-market-shocks-bbc). The story everyone ran was the bubble warning. Bailey said ["everybody is currently priced to be a winner"](https://thenextweb.com/news/andrew-bailey-ai-market-shocks-bank-of-england) and reached for Netscape. Good line. Not the interesting document.

The interesting document is the [FPC's September record](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/financial-policy-committee-record/2026/fpc-summary-and-record-september-2026), published the same day. Two facts in it that the bubble coverage skipped. First: AI hyperscalers now account for 47% of sterling corporate bond issuance this year. Nearly half the sterling bond market. Second: some of that financing involves what the committee calls "circular arrangements" — money flowing back into the sector it's funding. The capital is partly checking its own homework.

Now the arithmetic. [JP Morgan analysts, cited in the FPC record](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/financial-policy-committee-record/2026/fpc-summary-and-record-september-2026), put AI-related debt capex at $4.1 trillion between 2026 and 2030. Call it $820bn a year. But $450bn had already landed by early September — eight and a half months in. Annualised, that's $635bn for 2026. The first year is running at 77% of the five-year average. Either years two through five go quiet, or the estimate is already wrong.

**Bailey's essay says:** test the models. Find the failure points before they matter. [He wrote this in the Bank's own Insight series](https://bankofenglandinsights.substack.com/p/frontier-ai-and-the-question-of-governance) and told the BBC regulation isn't where he'd start.

**The FPC record says:** the failure point isn't in the model. It's in the moment the people who lent $450bn decide the revenue won't arrive.

Testing catches bad model behaviour. It doesn't catch a bond market that reprices because investors blinked. Those are two different shocks and they'd land at the same time.

There's a version where this is fine. The AI Security Institute watches the models. The FPC watches the debt. Different jobs. The problem is that a model incident — something that erodes confidence in near-term AI revenue — collapses both sides at once. The FPC already says so: a reassessment of productivity-gain expectations ["could affect not only AI-related asset valuations but also sovereign debt markets."](https://ca.investing.com/news/stock-market-news/boe-warns-middle-east-conflict-ai-debt-raise-risks-to-financial-stability-93CH-4859593)

Bailey used Netscape to warn investors. He meant: not everyone survives the boom. He didn't mean: what happens to sterling bond markets when the Netscape of 2026 holds 47% of the market on the way down.

That's the version of the story the BBC interview didn't tell.
