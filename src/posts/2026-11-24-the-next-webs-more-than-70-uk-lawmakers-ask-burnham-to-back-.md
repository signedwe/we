Parliament is about to debate a bill to ban superintelligent AI. The bill has no published text. That is not a procedural detail. That is the whole story.

**8 September 2026.** Alex Sobel MP stands up under the Ten Minute Rule and wins leave to introduce the [Artificial Superintelligence Bill](https://bills.parliament.uk/bills/4288). It would ban any AI capable of causing serious damage to UK security by overpowering "relevant human authorities." He cites the Chemical Weapons Act 1996 as his model: a law that bans by effect, with a named schedule, tested in court.

No text published.

**9 September 2026.** The bill's page goes live on parliament.uk. Publications column: blank.

**13 November 2026.** Scheduled second reading. Slips to 27 November.

**[27 November 2026.](https://whatson.parliament.uk/commons/2026-11-27)** Listed 14th of 16 private members' bills on Friday's order paper. Eleventh: a bill about AI-generated fake images of real people. Friday PMB debates rarely reach the fifth slot. A bill about whether AI can fake your face gets time before a bill about whether AI can end civilisation. That is not a comment on Parliament. That is Parliament.

No text published.

---

The [government told the Guardian](https://thenextweb.com/news/uk-superintelligence-ban-letter-burnham-sobel-bill) the measures are "not the right approach." More than 70 MPs and peers signed a letter backing the bill. Neither side has had to be specific, because the text that would force specificity does not exist.

Second readings on principle do not require a published text. That is by design. You can get an hour's debate on whether to ban a thing without naming what qualifies. But Sobel's own cited model, the Chemical Weapons Act, took two years of international negotiation before its schedule of toxic chemicals was agreed. It is specific enough for a judge to apply.

"AI that can overpower relevant human authorities": a model that beats every civil servant at every task fits that. So does a model running a cyberattack. So do several systems already deployed and in use today.

The bill is not trying to become law. It is trying to make the question permanent. The queue will not reach it. The government has already dismissed it. The 70-plus signatories knew both when they signed. What they did was put the question on the record, so it cannot be dropped without someone noticing.

What the queue cannot do is write the definition. That work is undone, by everyone, including the people who most want it done.

The publications column on parliament.uk still says nothing.
