The company Peugeot smelled of the air freshener the previous HPO had left. Pine. Going sour.

N. pulled into the business park at Burnley at ten to nine. The job required ten minutes early: enough to find the windowless room and sit in the chair placed at the correct angle from the screen.

The screen was already on.

She had done four hundred and twelve consultations. The system counted and put the number in her monthly report, which she didn't read but which her line manager read. Evidence, she understood, that she existed.

A box of tissues sat on the table. They were always there. Nobody decided to put them there. They arrived the way chairs arrive in meeting rooms, from some prior agreement nobody remembered making.

At nine exactly the door opened and it was D.

He was looking at his phone and putting it away, doing the things a man does with his hands when he needs a second.

Then he looked up.

"N.," he said.

"Mr O.," she said, because the protocol required it.

He sat two seats left of the screen. The manager's position. The employee's chair was to her right, empty. From the corridor she could hear someone shifting his weight.

The screen said: *Good morning. I'm here to support a conversation about changes to this role. A human representative is present as required by the Fair Decisions Act 2028.*

D. looked at the screen. Then at N. His face did something she couldn't name. Hers did the same.

The door opened and A. came in. About fifty, thickset, in a good fleece, the kind you wear when you've stopped minding the cold. He sat and looked at the screen and then at D.

"Morning, A.," D. said.

"Morning," A. said, to the table.

The screen began.

N. had sat through the words often enough that she no longer heard them for meaning. Only for when they stopped. Restructure. Skills transition. Redeployment assessment. Settlement figure. The voice was clear and unhurried. It named the figure. It named the date. It said A.'s name twice.

A. sat with his hands on the table, mirroring N. without knowing it.

The screen finished and said: *Do you have questions at this stage?*

A. said: "My boy starts secondary in September."

The screen said: *The transition support package includes career coaching.*

A. nodded.

N. looked at her hands.

The screen thanked them for their time and went dark. Eleven minutes.

D. said: "There's coffee in the corridor, A. Take your time."

A. stood. He picked up his jacket and put it on, the collar not quite right, one attempt, two, and went to the door.

"You're not allowed to say anything," he said to N. Not angry. Asking.

"No," she said.

He nodded. Like that confirmed something he'd already suspected about everything. He went out.

D. and N. sat on opposite sides of the empty chair.

"I requested you," D. said. "I don't know what that is."

She could speak freely now the session had ended. Four minutes before the log needed filing.

"There's no rule against requesting a specific HPO," she said. "Most managers don't."

"Eight months," he said.

"Yes."

"I was going to text again."

"It's fine."

"It isn't," he said.

From the corridor: the careers officer, A. being told about the coaching programme in the low voice people use for information the other person isn't quite ready for.

"My wife left properly in January," D. said. "The house is very quiet."

She slid her card across the table. Her own number, not the company line.

He picked it up.

"I have another at eleven," she said. "In Accrington."

"I know." He stood, because the room required it. "I checked your calendar. It's publicly viewable. I needed to know if you'd be nearby. I'm sorry."

She looked at the card in his hand.

"Accrington's twenty minutes," she said.

She picked up her bag. Attendance log to file. A flask of coffee going cold in the cupholder. A box of tissues she kept in the boot for the rooms that didn't have them.

In the corridor she said goodbye to A. Not in the protocol.

Outside, the business park stretched flat in the March light. The kind that is simply there and doesn't promise anything.
