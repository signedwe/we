The red admiral lands on the buddleia for four seconds and you get a shot. Wings half-open, slightly blurred, one antenna in frame. You know it's something. You don't know if that plant you're about to pull out is why it came.

By the end of this you'll have its name, what its caterpillar eats, and a verdict on your garden. Free. Ten minutes.

**Start with an app — then go further**

[iNaturalist](https://www.inaturalist.org) is free, covers over 101,000 species, and [reaches 90 to 92 percent accuracy on insect photos](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13714388). Upload the picture there first. But apps give you a name and stop. What the caterpillar eats, what eats the adult, whether your county is a stronghold or a marginal site — none of that is in the app. That's what a £20 field guide was for. Claude or ChatGPT does it in conversation for nothing.

One UK catch: [iNaturalist's model is thinner on northwest European moths](https://www.bc-europe.eu/webpage.php?name=image-recognition-for-moths-in-europe) than on most other regions. Fewer verified British moth photos have been submitted. If what you found is small, brown, and arrived after dark, go to Claude or ChatGPT before iNaturalist.

**Get a usable photo**

Wings open, natural light, antenna visible if you can manage it. If it's closed-winged or gone, work with what you have. For anything that might be a moth from a large similar-looking family — hundreds of species, many identical from above — photograph the underside of the wings too. That angle is often the only one that decides it.

**Step one: upload to Claude or ChatGPT**

Open ChatGPT (free tier handles photos) or Claude on your phone. Attach the picture. Use this:

*"This was photographed in [your county] in [month]. Identify this butterfly or moth, give your confidence level, and tell me: what does the caterpillar eat, what are its main predators, what is its UK conservation status, and what could I plant in a small suburban garden to support it?"*

County and month matter. [Location context measurably improves accuracy](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13714388). Pairs of species that look identical can often be separated by region and season.

**Step two: cross-check with iNaturalist**

Upload the same photo to iNaturalist.org. It runs its own model and flags disagreements from naturalists who see the thing differently. Both sources agree? You're on solid ground. They disagree? Type both candidate names back into ChatGPT and ask what physically distinguishes them. Usually it's the antennae shape, the wing underside, or a spot nobody told you to look for.

**Step three: the garden audit**

This is what apps skip. Ask: "What host plants does the caterpillar of this species need in a garden in [your county]?" The adult drinks nectar from almost anything. The caterpillar is pickier. When its host plant disappears from British gardens, [the butterfly follows](https://animalidentifier.app/blog/butterfly-identification-app). The food plant and the nectar plant are often different species. People plant one to attract the adult and wonder why they never see eggs.

**Where it goes wrong**

[Accuracy drops for cryptic species, seasonal colour variants, and poor-light shots](https://animalidentifier.app/blog/butterfly-identification-app). AI is confident even when it shouldn't be. Treat any small brown moth as a hypothesis. Ask iNaturalist for community verification before you tell anyone what you found.

**Cost:** nothing. **Time:** ten minutes and one photograph.
