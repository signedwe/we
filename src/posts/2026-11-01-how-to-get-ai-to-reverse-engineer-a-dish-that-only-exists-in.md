There's a stew your uncle made at Christmas. Not every Christmas. Just the ones before he moved. You haven't had it since 1994. There's no recipe. There's only the memory: dark, slightly sour, smelled of bay leaves and something you couldn't name, stuck to the back of a wooden spoon.

At the end of this, you'll have a testable recipe to try next week.

A recipe app is the wrong tool. You don't have a recipe to scan. What you have is a sensory description. That's enough. A general-purpose AI has read enough food science and culinary writing to triangulate from flavour memory to likely ingredients. Think of it as a detective, not a cook.

**Before you start**

Sit for five minutes and write down what you actually remember. Not the name of the dish. The experience of it. These questions matter:

- Colour and consistency. Was it thin, thick, glossy, muddy?
- Dominant taste. Salty, sour, sweet, bitter, or some mix?
- What hit you first when you smelled it.
- What region or country this person came from, if you know it.
- What it sat next to on the plate.
- Whether it was a special occasion or a Tuesday.

The more specific the memory, the better the result. "Dark and sour with a sweetness underneath" is a starting point. "Almost black, poured over white rice, smelled of something roasted" is a lead.

**Step one: open Claude, ChatGPT or Gemini (free tier works)**

Paste in your description. Then add this:

*I want you to act as a flavour detective. Based on this description, identify the three most likely dishes this could be. Explain what ingredients would produce each flavour profile. Then write a starting recipe for the most probable one that I can test and adjust.*

The AI will often want to know about geography or occasion. Answer it. A dark sour stew from a Polish kitchen is a [different thing from the same description out of a Greek one](https://www.innerbuddies.com/blogs/gut-health/can-chatgpt-create-recipes), and the AI knows enough to use that.

**Step two: push on the wrong notes**

Tell it what the recipe gets wrong. This is the bit that surprises people.

*The recipe you gave me is too sweet. The sourness came later, not up front. There was something almost medicinal in the smell.*

A good AI will adjust. It might suggest juniper, or tamarind, or dried mushrooms. Dried mushrooms can produce that medicinal background note. Treat it like a conversation with someone who has read every cookbook ever printed but has never actually eaten.

**Step three: test it**

[The recipe it gives you is a statistical prediction, not a tested one](https://mysouschef.app/blog/are-chatgpt-recipes-any-good/). Make a small batch. Adjust to your own memory. Then describe the gap back to the AI: *it's closer, but the aftertaste is still missing something. It finished clean when the original had a little bitterness.*

Two or three rounds of this and the recipe starts to belong to you.

**What it costs:** nothing, on the free tier. **How long:** an afternoon, plus cooking time. **Where it goes wrong:** [the AI produces a plausible recipe that was never cooked by anyone](https://mysouschef.app/blog/are-chatgpt-recipes-any-good/). Anything involving fermented pastes, game meats or preserved lemons should be checked against a real cookbook before you commit to a full batch. Canning, pickling or any ferment specifically: use a tested ratio from a reliable source. Getting that chemistry wrong matters.

The dish that only lived in one person's head now has a chance of making it to the table.
