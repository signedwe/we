This is invented.

The mediator, R., has been doing this for nineteen years. She knows when a person is speaking and when a person is reciting.

The room is a converted terrace in Didcot, two chairs either side of a low table, a window onto a car park. D. sits on the left with a printed document, twelve pages, tabbed. His ex-wife, L., sits on the right with her phone face-down and a printed document, nine pages, also tabbed. They have not looked at each other since they came in.

R. opens the way she always does. She asks each of them to describe, in their own words, what a good outcome would look like.

D. goes first. He speaks for four minutes. He uses phrases like *the child's developmental needs across both residential settings* and *consistency of routine as a protective factor*. He does not stumble. He does not pause to find a word. At one point he says *the literature suggests* and then stops himself and says *I read that somewhere*.

L. goes next. She uses different phrases that mean the same things. She also does not stumble. She also says *research indicates* at one point and also stops herself.

R. has seen this before, twice this month. Both arrived having spoken to something that listened and gave them the strongest version of their position. It handed them the register of a professional. It pre-answered the objections. The document on D.'s side has a section headed *Objections and Responses*. So does L.'s. The objections are each other's arguments. The responses are identical in structure.

What R. is looking at is not a conversation. It's two positions that already met somewhere else, and already failed to agree somewhere else, and are now being read aloud in a room in Didcot by two people who look frightened.

She puts her pen down.

She asks D. what his daughter does on Sunday mornings. Not in the document sense. Just: what does she do.

He says she watches a show about horses on a tablet. Then she gets into bed with him and they watch it together and she falls back to sleep. He says this in his own voice, which is nothing like the document's voice. L. is looking at him. She did not know about the horses.

This is the part R. cannot explain to anyone who asks whether AI is coming for mediation. The machine prepared both of them for a fight. Neither of them came here to fight. They came because they cannot talk to each other. The preparation made them more correct and less able to reach each other than they were before they walked in.

By 2031, every family mediator in England has sat in this room. The professional training now covers it: how to get from the prepared position back to the person underneath, and how long that takes. The answer is about forty minutes. The initial meeting used to take forty minutes total.

D. is still talking about Sunday mornings. L.'s document sits on the table, face up, unread by anyone but her.

R. thinks: the machines got everything right and missed the whole thing.
