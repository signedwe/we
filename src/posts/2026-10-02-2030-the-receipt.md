*The people here are made up. The world they live in is not quite ours yet.*

The purse had fallen behind the radiator.

P. found it when he was moving the shelf unit two centimetres to the left, which he had been meaning to do since March, because it sat at an angle that could be corrected and he corrected things that could be corrected. He picked the purse up. It was warm from the radiator. He set it on the counter and it came open and the receipt was there in the card section, folded in half, tucked behind her travel card.

He read it. A phone number in blue pen, someone's handwriting, slanted left. Below that, in the same hand: *T.*

He folded it along its original crease. He put it back.

Then he put the kettle on, because the kettle was what you put on.

The shelf unit was still at its angle. He looked at it and left it.

M. came home at half past six with a bag from the Turkish shop: the good olives, the sesame bread she liked, a piece of the good feta he had mentioned once, months ago, in passing. It cost four pounds eighty for a small block. He knew the price because he did the shopping.

She put it all on the counter.

"I thought we could eat in," she said.

"Good," he said.

He looked at the feta. She had bought it because she had done something. She did not know that he knew. The block sat on its paper, four pounds eighty, which was now the price of whatever she had done.

He sliced it anyway.

They ate on the sofa with the plates on their knees. He had made the salad with the walnuts and the bitter leaves, which took longer than she realised and which he had never told her. She ate with her feet tucked under her. The lamp was on.

"Rosa's doing a thing at school next month," M. said. "Some kind of assembly. T. mentioned it."

"We should go," P. said.

A pause.

"That would be nice," she said. "She'd like that."

"I'll put it in."

He did it while she was still sitting there. He opened the shared calendar they had kept since 2024, which had their dentist appointments and her court days and the three separate occasions the boiler man had come and not fixed it. He added Rosa's assembly. He gave it a colour. Green, which was what they used for things they were looking forward to.

M. watched him.

"Done," he said.

She looked at her own screen. The green square sat in the third week of November. She looked at it for a moment without saying anything.

"Thank you," she said.

He collected the plates.

In the kitchen he ran the water and washed them by hand, one at a time, which she found slow. Twelve centimetres away, in her purse on the chair by the door, the receipt sat folded along its crease.

He dried both plates and stacked them.

The shelf unit was still at its angle. He would do it at the weekend, he decided. Properly, with a spirit level.
