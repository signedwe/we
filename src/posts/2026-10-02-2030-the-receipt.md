The purse had fallen behind the radiator.

Paul found it when he moved the shelf unit two centimetres to the left, which he had been meaning to do since March, because it sat at an angle that could be corrected and he corrected things that could be corrected. He set the purse on the counter. It fell open, and the receipt was there in the card pocket, folded in quarters, behind her bank card.

He did not unfold it straight away. He stood and considered whether he was the kind of man who would. Then he unfolded it.

A phone number in pencil, someone else's handwriting, slanting left. Below it: *Tom*.

He folded it back along its creases and put it where it had been, behind the bank card, exactly. He was good at exactly.

He put the kettle on, because the kettle was what you put on.

He had known since the bakery. He had known, if he was honest, since a night in March when she came home from somewhere and said she wanted to be surprised by her life, and he had understood that he was not a surprise and never would be. He had decided then what he would do, which was nothing that could ever be held against him. A man who is only kind cannot be left without the leaving looking cruel. He knew this about himself. He did not much like it. He did it anyway.

Marnie came home at half past six with a bag from the Turkish shop: olives, the sesame bread she liked, a block of the good feta he had once mentioned in passing.

"I thought we could eat in," she said.

"Good," he said.

She had bought the feta because she had done something. He could have told her the date.

He sliced it.

They ate on the sofa with the plates on their knees. She talked about a case. He listened properly, the way he always did, and asked the next question, the way he always did.

"Rosa's got an assembly next month," she said. "Tom mentioned it."

"We should go."

A pause.

"That would be nice," she said.

"I'll put it in."

He did it while she watched. The calendar offered, as it always did, to remember things for him. He typed it in himself. Rosa's assembly. He gave it a colour: green, which was what they used for things they were looking forward to.

"Done," he said.

She looked at the green square in the third week of November. He watched her understand that she could not now not go, and that he had arranged it so kindly that she could never say he had.

"Thank you," she said.

He took the plates to the kitchen and washed them by hand. Across the room her purse sat on the chair by the door with the receipt in it.

He dried both plates, put them away, and then went back to the hall, picked up the shelf unit, and moved it the two centimetres back to where it had been, crooked, as it had always been, so that nothing in the flat would show that he had found anything at all.
