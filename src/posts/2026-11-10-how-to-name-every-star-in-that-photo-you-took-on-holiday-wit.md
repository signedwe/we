You have a photo on your phone of a sky so clear it made you stop walking. You took it on a hillside in Greece, or in the garden during a power cut, or leaning out of a tent. You cannot name a single thing in it.

Tonight you can.

The stargazing apps never mention this problem: they need the live sky. [Stellarium](https://www.skywhen.com/blog/best-stargazing-apps-2026), [SkySafari](https://androidlounge.net/16-best-android-apps-for-stargazing/), Star Walk. You hold your phone up and they label what's overhead right now. Great outside. No use at all for a photo from last August.

For a photo you've already taken, you need something that reads the image itself.

**What you'll have at the end:** the names of the constellations in your photo, the stories behind them, and probably one fact that stops you mid-sentence.

**Step 1: Find the photo.** Stars need to be distinct points, not streaks. A dark, clear night, a steady hand or a surface to rest on. Most modern phone cameras manage this. If the stars look smeared, the camera moved.

**Step 2: Upload it.** Two routes.

The dedicated tool: [frescai.com/star-identifier](https://frescai.com/star-identifier) takes the image directly. It [reads the arrangement, relative brightness, and spacing of the stars](https://frescai.com/star-identifier) to match against known constellations. It gives you ranked possibilities rather than a single answer. That's honest. A wide-angle shot often catches parts of several constellations at once.

The general assistant route: open Claude or ChatGPT, attach the photo, and type:

*I took this on [date] from [rough location, e.g. southern Spain, latitude about 37°N]. What constellations and named stars are visible? Which direction was I probably pointing?*

Date and location matter. Without them, the assistant is guessing at which part of the sky was overhead.

**Step 3: Go further.** Once you have names, ask:

*What can I actually see in Orion beyond the belt? What's the fuzzy patch below it?*

The fuzzy patch below Orion's belt is the Orion Nebula. A star-forming cloud, [visible to the naked eye](https://imageidentifier.ai/what-constellation-is-this) if you know to look. Most people photograph it without knowing it's there.

**Where it goes wrong:** Dense, dark photos with hundreds of faint stars are harder than a clear shot of three bright ones. The [frescai tool](https://frescai.com/star-identifier) says so explicitly when it's uncertain. For the general assistants, give them location and date, then check whatever name they give you against [Stellarium's free web version](https://www.skywhen.com/blog/best-stargazing-apps-2026), which runs in any browser.

[SkySafari 8](https://androidlounge.net/16-best-android-apps-for-stargazing/), which reached Android in March 2026, added plate-solving. It can identify what's in a photo and lock it onto a star chart. It costs a few pounds and is built for people with telescopes. The free routes above handle most holiday snaps.

**What it costs:** Free for both main options. No login required at frescai. Claude and ChatGPT accept image uploads on their free tiers.

**How long:** Five minutes. Maybe ten if you get talking to Orion.

Four photos on your camera roll have a sky you've never read. That's four conversations you haven't had yet.
