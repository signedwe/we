The voters AI focus groups get wrong most badly aren't random. They're specific.

Sharon is 54. She lives in Rochdale. She cleans a primary school on weekday mornings. No degree. She fills in roughly one online survey a year, when her daughter emails a link and chases her twice. She barely uses the internet beyond WhatsApp.

This morning a politician at Westminster is testing a speech about energy bills on an AI focus group. [Synthetic Respondents, a London startup, charges £12 a project](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-22/uk-politicians-are-testing-their-messages-on-ai-generated-focus-groups). A thousandth of traditional costs, it says. Sharon is among the voters being simulated.

[Pew Research Center published a study last month](https://www.pewresearch.org/data-labs/2026/09/30/can-ai-stand-in-for-human-survey-takers-not-really/). Average error between AI respondents and real ones: 12 percentage points across three waves. The groups the AI got most wrong were people without college degrees, people who rarely go online, and Black adults. The people who put least into survey databases are the ones the model reads least well.

Sharon, specifically.

Jacob Reid's Bloomberg piece calls this democratisation. Backbenchers can now afford research previously out of their budget. That's true. It's half the story. The politician gets cheaper research. Sharon gets a copy of herself built from people who are not her.

[Cornell researcher Angelina Wang found](https://www.motherjones.com/politics/2026/03/polling-artificial-intelligence-democracy-market-research-ai-surveys/) that AI models don't just misread groups like Sharon's. They read those groups the way outsiders do. The synthetic Sharon is assembled from everyone except Sharon.

There's a second problem. Traditional focus groups sometimes surface the unexpected: the voter who breaks the pattern, the worry nobody had a name for yet. An AI persona can't find what wasn't in its training data. It's the average of past opinions, made fast. Novel political anger arrives as a surprise at every election. It's a surprise because it hadn't been recorded yet. An AI focus group can't find it because it looks for things people have already said.

Cheap tools that mostly work push political messaging toward voters who are easy to read. Not as a plan. Just as arithmetic. The citizens hardest to reach by phone or post end up with the least tested messaging aimed at them.

A parliament that checks its ideas only against the voters it can already model stops being surprised by the ones it can't.

Sharon finishes her shift at half twelve. The leaflet comes Thursday. The speech never found her.
