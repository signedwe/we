~~The entry test for becoming a commercial pilot in the UK costs £70,000 to £120,000.~~ Training to fly commercially in the UK costs roughly £100,000 to £150,000 by the integrated route, and £50,000 to £70,000 modular. The struck range straddled the two and put the floor where the modular ceiling is. Calling it an entry test was wrong twice over: a test costs nothing like this, and the money is the training, not the exam. That's just the licence. It doesn't include living costs for the eighteen months you're training, or the type-rating you'll likely need on top. Before you even start professional training, [the private pilot licence runs £14,000 to £19,000](https://flyer.co.uk/feature/learn-to-fly-2026-what-does-it-cost-to-get-a-ppla-part-1-the-dream-vs-the-reality/). ~~And standard UK student loans don't cover any of it.~~ Student finance covers the academic part of a degree-linked programme and never the flight training. Buy the training on its own from a flight school and no loan touches it.

The fair objection: flying a commercial aircraft is genuinely dangerous. The training hours exist because lives depend on them. Safety isn't a fiction here. Some airlines do run cadet schemes. [EasyJet has one](https://becomeapilot.easyjet.com/). [Ryanair has a part-sponsored programme](https://careers.ryanair.com/pilots/become-a-pilot/). These are real. They've helped some people in. But they're extremely competitive, they don't cover the full cost for everyone, and you still need to get through the door first.

Here's what sits next to that. The industry has spent years complaining it doesn't have enough pilots. ~~The estimated global shortfall ran to between 649,000 and 674,000 new pilots needed by 2043, according to 2024 industry reports~~ [Boeing's 2024 outlook forecasts demand for 674,000 new pilots between 2024 and 2043](https://boeing.mediaroom.com/2024-07-22-Boeing-Projects-Global-Need-for-Nearly-2-4-Million-New-Commercial-Pilots,-Technicians,-Cabin-Crew). That is demand, not a shortfall: the number the industry expects to hire and train, retirements included. It is not a gap between supply and need, and the struck range was two consecutive editions of the same Boeing report glued into one figure. BALPA, the UK pilots' union, is more sceptical about those numbers. But even they say [demand for newly qualified pilots is increasing](https://www.ftnonline.co.uk/2023/10/30/balpas-scepticism-about-the-global-pilot-shortage-and-upfront-training-fees/). Meanwhile ~~only around 6% of pilots worldwide are women~~ around four to six per cent of the world's airline pilots are women. Across all licence categories the figure is roughly double, so the denominator matters.

So we have a profession that says it needs more people, that the pipeline is too thin, that the demographic mix is too narrow. The entry cost is six figures. There's no public loan scheme. Nobody designed it this way. Nobody's seriously redesigning it now.

Aviation isn't like medicine or law, where the state decided the public needed these people and subsidised the cost accordingly. There's no principle that says a would-be pilot should self-fund at that scale. It's just how it turned out. The cost became the filter. The filter stayed.

The industry gets to complain about the shortage and charge the entry fee at the same time. That's a neat trick if you can pull it off.
