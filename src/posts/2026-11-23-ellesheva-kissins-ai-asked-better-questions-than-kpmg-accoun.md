The building has four floors. Only one of them matters.

On 18 November, the [Financial Times reported](https://fourweekmba.com/ai-kpmgs-first-ai-audit-used-100-agents-on-a-ftse-250-firm/) that KPMG ran more than 100 AI agents through every phase of a parallel audit of an unnamed FTSE 250 company. Two partners and one director ran the whole thing. The agents gathered data, wrote up findings, produced draft conclusions. In several places, Cath Burnet, KPMG's UK head of audit, told the FT, "the AI was able to go so much deeper" than the regular team.

Floor one: the technology. More than 100 agents, four months to build, every phase of a full audit. [Firms had used AI on specific tasks before](https://www.resultsense.com/news/2026-11-18-kpmg-ai-agents-ftse-250-audit-pilot/), never end to end.

Floor two: the regulator. The Financial Reporting Council [published guidance two days earlier](https://fourweekmba.com/ai-uk-agentic-audit-rules-came-233-days-before-kpmg-pilot-news/), on 16 November, warning that sharing AI-derived insights with clients can threaten an auditor's independence. The agents read far more of a company's data than any sample would. What they surfaced beyond the audit brief is now a compliance problem. The FRC's answer: the human auditor always signs the opinion.

Floor three: the fees. By end of 2027, one of KPMG's major clients will cite Project Exponential in a fee talk and win. KPMG already knows this. It [pressed its own auditor, Grant Thornton, to cut its fee by roughly a seventh](https://www.irishtimes.com/business/2026/02/06/kpmg-pressed-its-auditor-to-pass-on-ai-cost-savings/) on the basis of AI saving time. It wrote the argument its clients will now use against it. When the FT asked whether Project Exponential would lead to lower client fees, KPMG said it saw "clear time-saving opportunities" and declined to answer.

Floor four is the one the coverage skipped.

[Junior auditors have historically spent their first years tracing individual transactions](https://www.benchmarkjournal.com/kpmg-trains-interns-for-an-ai-run-audit). Repetitive, yes. But that's also how you learn what normal looks like. The subsidiary ledger that doesn't quite reconcile. The revenue line moving the wrong way. You spend two years staring at that stuff, being corrected, until the wrongness starts to feel wrong before anyone tells you.

Project Exponential's agents do that work now. KPMG knows it. It's already redesigning intern training around fraud scenarios and judgment calls rather than the transaction-tracing the agents absorbed.

Here's what the redesign can't fix. The partners who'll check agent conclusions at fifty were the ones tracing ledgers at twenty-two. They know when the agent is probably wrong because they spent years being wrong themselves, with someone senior watching. The people who are twenty-two now are getting fraud simulations from week one. That's a fair response to a real problem. But knowing how to push back on an agent's draft requires knowing, in your hands, what a correct audit feels like. You got that by getting it wrong first, repeatedly, with someone watching who could see it.

KPMG told the FT it sees ["clear time-saving opportunities"](https://www.big4news.com/p/ai-audit-pyramid-kpmg-training-professional-judgement) and would not say fees would fall. It didn't address what happens when the people signing the opinions have never done the work the opinions depend on.
