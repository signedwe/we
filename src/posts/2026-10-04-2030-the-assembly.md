*Fiction. All characters are invented.*

The chairs in the school hall were the orange plastic kind that stack, and someone had stacked them wrong the week before, so the row P. and M. sat in ran slightly downhill to the left. P. sat at the high end. He had a coat on his lap.

T. and K. were four rows forward. M. had seen them come in together, K. in a dark green anorak, T. with Rosa's swimming bag over one shoulder because it was Thursday and they went straight from here. They sat with a gap between them exactly the width of one empty chair.

The Year 3 class came in from the left. Rosa was third in her row. She was wearing her school jumper and the small gold studs she was allowed on Thursdays and Fridays, and she walked with the particular stride of a child who has been told not to run and has found the maximum legal speed. She located T. in the fourth row and lifted two fingers. She located M. in the seventh and her face changed, briefly, to the expression of someone who has found the thing she was actually looking for, before she went back to finding her mark.

The headteacher said things. November light came in flat through the high windows. A girl played the recorder, which required courage. Then Year 3 came to the front.

The poem was about the bird. M. knew it now the way she knew certain witness statements: not memorised, but worn in, the words fitting grooves they had made over months of Rosa's retelling. The bird that returned to the tree every year regardless. The branches gone. The leaves gone. The tree cut to the stump, and the bird coming back and sitting on it and singing, because the bird could not help it.

Rosa said the last verse to the fourth row. Her hands: flat on invisible bark, then out wide for the branches, then one hand to her chest for the singing. She said the last line clearly.

The hall clapped. P. put his fingers in his mouth and whistled, which M. had asked him not to do at these things. T. did not turn round. K. did, once, briefly, and found P., and did not look further.

In the corridor afterwards there were children and coats and the smell of November and school dinner still somewhere in the ventilation. Rosa came out at speed, found T., hugged him round the waist, said something into his coat, came up for air. K. was talking to another parent. T. looked over Rosa's head and found M.

One second.

Rosa turned. P. was three feet away talking to the headteacher, who had come to thank him for the whistling in the way headteachers thank people for things they would prefer not to be thanked for.

Rosa came to M.

"You got the last line right," M. said. "Both of you."

"I taught him the action," Rosa said. She demonstrated: one hand to the chest. "He kept doing it like this." She made a fist. "Which isn't right. It's not supposed to be brave. It just can't help it."

M. said: "No."

Rosa put her coat on. She zipped it to the top and pulled the collar straight, which was a thing she did herself now. She looked at M. with the assessor's look, the one she'd used on the börek and the satsumas.

"Are you still coming on Thursdays?" she said.

"No," M. said. "I don't think so."

Rosa took this in. She pulled the zip up another half centimetre, which was as far as it went.

"He was better," she said. "When you came."

P. appeared at M.'s shoulder. The headteacher had finished. He put his hand briefly on the back of M.'s coat and looked at Rosa.

"You were brilliant," he said. "The action at the end. Your own?"

"Mostly," said Rosa. She looked between them once, then went to find T.

P. did not ask what they had been talking about. He was not the kind of man who asked. He had his hand still on her back, and when Rosa went he dropped it, and they walked out into the cold, down the path between the school fences, the last leaves on the plane tree going in the November way they always went.

At the gate P. said he fancied chips.

M. said yes.

They turned left on the High Street, together, toward the ordinary place that sold them.
