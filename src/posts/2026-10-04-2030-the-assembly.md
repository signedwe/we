The assembly was the third Thursday in November, and Marnie had not been to the bakery for six weeks, and Rosa had noticed.

She noticed from the stage. Year 3 filed in, and Rosa found Tom in the fourth row and lifted two fingers, and then found Marnie in the seventh, next to Paul, and her face did something complicated and decided on a grin.

The poem went well. The bird came back to its tree, the tree lost its branches, then its leaves, then everything but the stump, and the bird came back anyway. Rosa did her own actions and said the last line properly, to the fourth row.

Then, instead of walking off, she stepped up to the microphone the headteacher had used.

"This one's for Marnie," she said, "who taught me the Danish for seagull. It's *måge*." She considered the hall. "You say it like you're a bit disappointed."

The hall laughed. Two hundred parents said *måge*, a bit disappointed. A teacher reached for Rosa's elbow and missed.

Paul put his fingers in his mouth and whistled, which Marnie had asked him never to do. Four rows forward, Tom did not turn round. Kate did. She found Marnie, and this time she kept looking.

In the corridor there was a crush of coats. Two fathers by the pegs were comparing their children's tutors, which were machines, and agreeing that the children should still be allowed to be wrong on Fridays. Rosa came through at the fastest speed allowed indoors and stopped in front of Marnie.

"Did you hear the bit?"

"The whole hall heard the bit."

"Good." Then, because questions were for getting answers: "Why don't you come on Thursdays any more?"

Paul was right there.

"I've been busy," Marnie said, which was the worst sentence she had said all year, and she said a great many sentences for a living.

Rosa gave her the look she used on vegetables.

"He was better," she said. "When you came. He did voices at bedtime. Now he just reads it."

Nobody in the little circle moved.

Paul crouched, slowly, a big man folding, until his face was level with Rosa's.

"Here's the thing," he said. "Marnie checks my Danish too, and I haven't got any. I'll lend her to you. Thursdays, the bakery, half an hour, for the seagulls. Deal?"

Rosa looked from him to Marnie and back.

"Deal," she said, and shook his hand, and went to find her father.

Paul stood up, knees cracking, and smiled at Marnie the way he smiled at waiters. He had just given her away for half an hour a week, in public, kindly, and she could never now say he hadn't. It was the most generous thing she had ever seen him do, and it was also a door shut quietly from the outside.

Kate was at her elbow before she could answer him.

"Coffee," Kate said. "You and me. Not the bakery. Tomorrow, ten, the place by the station." She zipped her anorak, said "Lovely whistle" to Paul, and went.

Outside, Paul took Marnie's hand at the gate, as he always did.

"Why did you do that?" she said.

"Because she asked the right question," he said, "and you gave her the wrong answer." He let go of her hand. "And because you went to Harwich on a Wednesday and didn't tell me. Salt and vinegar?"
