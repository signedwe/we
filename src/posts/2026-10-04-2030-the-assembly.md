*Fiction. All characters are invented.*

The assembly was the third Thursday in November, and Marnie had not been to the bakery for six weeks, and Rosa had noticed.

She noticed from the stage. Year 3 filed in, and Rosa found Tom in the fourth row, and lifted two fingers, and then found Marnie in the seventh, next to Paul, and her face did something complicated and then decided on a grin.

The poem went well. The bird came back to its tree, the tree lost its branches, then its leaves, then everything but the stump, and the bird came back anyway. Rosa did the actions she had invented, hands flat on bark, arms wide for the branches, one hand to her chest for the singing, and said the last line properly, to the fourth row.

Then, instead of walking off, she stepped forward to the microphone the headteacher had used.

"This one's for Marnie," she said, "who taught me the Danish for seagull. It's *måge*." She considered the hall. "You say it like you're a bit disappointed."

The hall laughed. Two hundred parents said *måge*, a bit disappointed. A teacher reached for Rosa's elbow and missed.

Paul put his fingers in his mouth and whistled, which Marnie had asked him never to do at these things. Four rows forward, Tom did not turn round. Kate did. She found Marnie, and this time she kept looking.

In the corridor there was a crush of coats. Two fathers by the pegs were comparing their children's tutors, which were machines, and agreeing that the children should still be allowed to be wrong on Fridays. Rosa came through it at the maximum speed allowed indoors and stopped in front of Marnie.

"Did you hear the bit?"

"The whole hall heard the bit."

"Good." Then, because in Rosa's world questions were for getting answers: "Why don't you come on Thursdays any more?"

Paul was right there. He had been asking the headteacher about the roof and had turned back mid-sentence.

"I've been busy," Marnie said, which was the worst sentence she had said all year, and she said a great many sentences for a living.

Rosa looked at her with the face she used on vegetables.

"He was better," she said. "When you came. He did voices at bedtime. Now he just reads it."

Nobody in the little circle moved. Somewhere behind them a child was crying about a coat.

Paul crouched, so his head was level with Rosa's. He did it slowly, a big man folding.

"Here's the thing," he said. "Marnie checks my Danish too, and I haven't got any. I'll lend her to you. Thursdays, the bakery, half an hour, for the seagulls. Deal?"

Rosa looked from him to Marnie and back, a girl pricing a deal.

"Deal," she said, and shook his hand, and went off to find her father.

Paul stood up, his knees cracking, and smiled at Marnie the way he smiled at waiters, and she understood that he had just given her away for half an hour a week, in public, kindly, so that she would never be able to say he hadn't.

Kate was at her elbow before she could answer him.

"Coffee," Kate said. "You and me. Not the bakery. Tomorrow, ten, the place by the station." She didn't wait for yes. She zipped her anorak, said "Lovely whistle" to Paul, and went.

Outside, it was cold and nearly dark. Paul took Marnie's hand at the gate, as he always did, and asked whether she fancied chips.

"Why did you do that?" she said.

He thought about it for the length of the path.

"Because she asked the right question," he said, "and you gave her the wrong answer. Salt and vinegar?"

She said yes. She was meeting Kate at ten. She did not know what Kate wanted, and she found, walking down the High Street with her hand in Paul's, that she was more frightened of that than of anything Paul might ever say.
