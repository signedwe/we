The billable hour did not die of clients getting fed up. It died of the same thing that made it necessary.

Born 1914, in Boston, in a legal aid office. [Reginald Heber Smith](https://www.thomsonreuters.com/en-us/posts/legal/billable-hour-history/), managing what would become Hale and Dorr, needed to know what the work cost. Thinking was expensive and invisible. The hour was the only unit you could count. So he started counting. Ironic, given his clients couldn't afford lawyers: the counting tool of access became the counting tool of exclusion.

The idea sat quietly for decades. [In the 1950s, law firms adopted it](https://artofvalue.com/the-history-of-the-billable-hour/) to shift risk onto clients, then built their whole business model around it. By the 1980s, *American Lawyer* magazine turned the hourly rate into a league table. A partner who billed 2,400 hours mattered. The hour had stopped measuring time.

Its finest moment: transparency. Before Smith's timesheets, clients got a bill saying "professional services rendered." The six-minute increment gave them something to argue with. That was real.

Who it kept out: anyone who couldn't pay. The rate and the annual minimum-hours target locked together and priced ordinary people out of courts they had every right to use.

**Cause of death.** AI doesn't work in tenths of an hour. A due diligence review that once took a junior associate three days now takes a model eleven minutes. [Forty-three percent of UK solicitors already report productivity gains from AI](https://www.softomatesolutions.com/blog/ai-professional-services-uk-law-accounting-consulting/). Job postings for corporate lawyers in London have [slumped from the hundreds into the double-digits](https://finance.yahoo.com/sectors/technology/articles/ai-turns-coders-lawyers-analysts-105500495.html). [Forty-seven percent of UK lawyers and 55% of in-house general counsel now expect AI to change legal pricing](https://wayanerd.co.uk/research/ai-professional-services-uk-2026).

When the task takes eleven minutes, you can't charge for three days.

Nobody says this plainly: the billable hour priced thinking being slow and hard to see. That's what made it necessary in 1914. Smith couldn't see inside a lawyer's head, so he counted the hours the head was supposedly occupied. The hour always stood in for something that couldn't be directly observed.

AI turns the output into a document anyone can read, check, and compare. The invisible becomes legible. The hour loses its alibi.

**Survivors.** Partners who bill for judgment, not time, and already knew the difference. Clients on fixed-fee arrangements who wondered why they waited. And every law student told the billable hour was simply how legal work gets priced, as though Smith had discovered a law of nature rather than a workaround for a temporary problem.

**Funeral arrangements.** A quiet service, at desks, with people comparing AI outputs. Fixed fees and outcome pricing will officiate. The flowers come in six-minute-increment sprays.

The hour lived 112 years on a premise that stopped being true this week in thousands of offices at once.
