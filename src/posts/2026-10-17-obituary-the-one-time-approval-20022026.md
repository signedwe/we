The One-Time Approval for AI medical devices died on 6 October 2026, aged 24, when the UK government [accepted all 44 recommendations](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/government-backs-recommendations-of-nhs-doctors-led-ai-commission) of the National Commission into the Regulation of AI in Healthcare. It is survived by the MHRA, twelve thousand patients and clinicians who gave evidence, and a great many AI diagnostic tools that have not been the same device twice.

**Born 2002, Medical Devices Regulations, SI 2002/618.** The regulations arrived in a world of static objects: scalpels, stents, hip replacements. A thing was a thing. You assessed it once, it went in the drawer, it stayed the same. The rule made sense because the rule described reality. Pass the test. You're done. Go be useful.

**It lived a productive life.** Class III devices, the risky ones, took [six to ten months](https://www.regdesk.co/regulations-library/united-kingdom/) to clear. Slow, expensive, answering the right question: is this device safe? For a device that would not change after leaving the factory, that was the only question worth asking.

The trouble was the AI diagnostic model. It learns. It updates. It drifts. An algorithm screening chest X-rays for cancer in October may weight things differently than the one screening them in March, retrained on six months of new data. Both carry the same certificate. The [commission's own words](https://www.computerweekly.com/news/366651579/UK-government-accepts-AI-healthcare-recommendations): current approaches were designed for products that are more static and easier to assess at a single point in time. That sentence is the death certificate.

Here is the arithmetic nobody put in the press release. The UK MDR 2002 came into force twenty-four years before the commission reported. A large AI model can push a weight update in hours. Twenty-four years at 8,760 hours per year is 210,240 hours during which a continuously updating device could have changed itself under a single unchanged certificate. The certificate said: we looked at this. It did not say which version.

By the end of 2029, the MHRA will have run at least one enforcement action against a device that passed its original approval and later failed post-market surveillance under the new lifecycle framework. If it hasn't, the new framework is the old framework with a different name on the door.

**Decline.** The [commission took evidence from more than 12,000 patients, clinicians and members of the public](https://www.technology.org/2026/10/06/uk-mhra-ai-medical-devices-continuous-monitoring/) and reported on 10 September 2026. On 6 October the government accepted all 44 findings. Draft guidance on managing changes to AI-enabled devices is due from the MHRA by December 2026. A consultation on classification follows in early 2027. A full implementation roadmap by spring 2027. The One-Time Approval did not lose a vote. It lost the argument.

**Survivors.** Every AI diagnostic tool currently running in an NHS trust on a certificate that predates its last retraining. The patient scanned by software nobody has checked since it was updated. The clinical team who assumed the approved thing and the running thing are the same thing. They may be right. There is currently no mechanism to find out.

**Funeral arrangements.** [AI Airlock Phase 3](https://www.unite.ai/uk-adopts-all-44-recommendations-from-nhs-doctors-led-ai-panel/) opens for applications now: a regulatory sandbox where promising tools reach the NHS early, under supervision, while evidence accumulates. It is what the old rule was not. An ongoing relationship rather than a handshake and a door. The deceased is buried under the Spring 2027 roadmap. The MHRA is the executor. The estate consists of twenty-four years of approved devices and one large open question about which of them is still what it says it is.
