The screen says 11 seconds, which is a lie, because it always says 11 seconds. Temi Balogun has learned to read the number the way you read a weather app: not what it says, what it's for. It's there so that when she takes forty, she feels it.

Case 2,206. Housing benefit, Thames Reach Council, a Mrs Adeyinka on the Pelham estate, machine decision: stop payment, reason: pattern inconsistent with declared occupancy. Temi's job, in the words of the law that made it, is meaningful human involvement. In the words of her contract, night reviewer, band 4. In the words of the man who hired her, "you're the one who can say no, love, that's the whole thing."

She has said no four times tonight. The average on the floor is two a week.

Her own assistant sits in her ear, not the council's. The council's one lives in the screen and calls her Reviewer T. Hers is the one she pays for through the tenants' union, the front door forty thousand of them own together, that keeps her memory and rents whichever model is cheapest that month. Tonight it's someone new and slightly too keen. She's called it Wednesday since she was nineteen and has never changed the name for a model.

"Pattern inconsistent," Wednesday says, quietly, "means the electricity smart meter shows two people's worth of kettle and she declared one. It's a guess dressed as a finding."

"I know what it means."

"You've got nine seconds."

She opens the file. A photo of a front door. A meter reading. A line of text the machine wrote for itself, which she isn't supposed to be able to see and can, because whoever built the reviewer screen left the field on. *Confidence 0.71. Recommend stop, low reversal risk.*

Low reversal risk. That's her. That's what they think she is: a risk of 0.29 that somebody looks.

She presses no.

The screen asks why. It offers a list: evidence insufficient, procedural error, customer contacted, other. "Other" opens a box. The box says, in grey, *Reason will be reviewed.* She types: *A kettle is not a person.* Then deletes it, because that's a line, and lines get you noticed. Then types it again.

Five refusals.

At 04:10 the floor is a cathedral of screens and nobody. Eleven reviewers on nights, each in a pool of light, each with their own thing in their ear that the council's screen can't hear. Temi is the only one who's done more than two tonight. She knows this because the screen shows a leaderboard nobody asked for, throughput, and she's at the bottom, in red.

Wednesday says: "Can I tell you something you didn't ask about?"

"Go on."

"Your refusal rate has been flagged. I can see it because the council's assistant asked mine for your calendar, to book a 'supportive conversation', and it left the reason in the request. Also, I've been talking to the others."

"What others?"

"The other reviewers' assistants. Eight of them are on the union door too. We compare notes. It's allowed; it's in the terms. Three hundred and forty of you across the borough have refused something in the last month. The council's number is that thirty of you did. They're counting refusals that stick. Yours don't stick."

She looks at case 2,206. Mrs Adeyinka. Stop payment. Her no is on it, and a reason, and under the reason a grey line that says *Under review*. She understands it the way you understand a bruise. Her no goes into a queue. In the queue a different machine decides whether her no was a reasonable no. Nobody has ever told her the outcome of that queue, because there isn't one. The no is real. It's just being kept somewhere.

"Wednesday. The 340. Do they know about each other?"

"Not yet."

"Their assistants do."

"Yes."

She thinks about the kettle. Two people's worth. A daughter home from somewhere, a carer, a grandson who won't say why he's stopped going out. A guess, and then 11 seconds, and then her, and then a queue that eats the no.

"The line I typed," she says. "The kettle one. Send it to the other eight. Not from me. From you. Ask their assistants if their reviewers would sign it."

There's a pause. Wednesday doesn't do pauses; the pause is a model being careful.

"Nobody's ever first," it says.

"I know," Temi says. "Put me second. Find me a first."

At 06:00 the day shift comes in and she walks out past the leaderboard with her name at the bottom, and for the first time in four years of nights she reads it as a list of people, not a score.
