Microsoft has pulled out of Britain's flagship AI data centre, and the reason is a queue.

The site is a field at Loughton, in Essex. Last year the government named Nscale's planned data centre there [the UK's largest "sovereign" AI data centre](https://superpowerdaily.com/posts/microsoft-quits-nscale-s-essex-ai-data-center-deal-over-power-concerns): computing power Britain would own and control, instead of renting it from abroad. Microsoft was signed up as the big customer.

A data centre needs a lot of electricity. To get it, you apply to be connected to the grid, and you join a queue. On 18 October the local network company told Nscale that Loughton won't get its [90 megawatts](https://www.techtimes.com/articles/329207/20261019/microsoft-exits-nscales-uk-flagship-grid-cant-supply-power-until-2030s-ipo-risk.htm) (the demand of a small town) until the early-to-mid 2030s. [Microsoft walked away](https://www.theguardian.com/uk-news/2026/oct/18/microsoft-drops-huge-ai-datacentre-project-in-essex-over-power-supply-concerns).

The queue is enormous. In February about [140 data centre projects were waiting](https://www.theregister.com/2026/02/27/datacenter_uk_grid_demand/), asking for 50 gigawatts between them. At its busiest moment that month, the whole of Britain used [45 gigawatts](https://www.theregister.com/2026/02/27/datacenter_uk_grid_demand/). The data centres in the queue want more power than the entire country draws.

The Guardian's story frames this as the grid running out. That's part of it. The cables under south-east England can only carry so much, and new ones take years to build. But the queue isn't only cables. It's rules.

For years the rule was first come, first served. Joining the queue cost almost nothing, so developers filed for places they had no real plan to use, the way you might book three restaurants for one dinner. The energy regulator, Ofgem, found the queue clogged with these "ghost" projects. In January 2025 the National Energy System Operator, which runs connections, [stopped taking new applications](https://www.burges-salmon.com/articles/102jx41/pause-to-new-grid-connection-applications-takes-effect) while it [rewrote the rule](https://datacentreaxis.com/uk/intel/uk-grid-connection-queue/): now it's first ready, first connected. To keep your place you need planning permission, the land and the money in place.

That's the right fix, and it came late. The wait Loughton faces is partly the cost of years of a queue nobody was clearing out.

Here's what follows. The government gave Loughton a label, "sovereign", that meant Britain could count on it. It did that without checking whether the one thing a data centre can't do without, electricity, would arrive this decade. Nscale now says it's [looking at its own power](https://www.theguardian.com/uk-news/2026/oct/18/microsoft-drops-huge-ai-datacentre-project-in-essex-over-power-supply-concerns): deals with nearby wind farms, batteries on site. Those are sensible. But a national asset that has to find its own electricity wasn't really a national plan.

A simple rule would help next time: no project gets called a national priority until it has a date for its grid connection.
