The rice was in the wrong cupboard.

Marnie noticed it when she dropped her keys on the counter. Paul had reorganised things on Saturday, while she was at the gala. Not wrong. Just not where the rice had been for nine years.

She moved it back.

Rosa arrived at twenty past six in her school coat, carrying a library book she'd borrowed without asking, pressed against her side like contraband.

"It's about seals," she said. "Not the animal."

"The wax kind."

"They had a whole section." She set it on the table. A knight on the cover, pressing a ring into red wax, very serious about it. "I thought you might need it."

Marnie looked at the book for a moment.

"*Segl*," she said. "Danish for seal. The wax kind."

Rosa wrote it, got it right, and gave it the look she gave things that came too easily.

"Did you see us at the gala," she said, not looking up. "From the gallery."

"Yes."

"I saw you when I got on the block. You and Paul and Dad all in a row." She pressed the pencil into the paper harder than she needed to. "You weren't in a row, actually. Paul was in the middle."

"He got there first."

"Is that why?"

Marnie opened her notebook.

"*Brev*. Letter. Correspondence, not alphabet. That's *bogstav*."

Rosa wrote both without being asked.

They did post. The words for things you send and the words for things you keep. Rosa wanted the word for something you write and don't send. Marnie said there wasn't one. Rosa said there should be. She'd said that before. Marnie said yes, she had.

The door went at ten past seven. Paul with shopping, one bag heavier than the other, the bottles shifting as he set it down. He got it to the counter without dropping anything, which took a moment of concentration he tried not to show.

"Rosa." He shook her hand. They'd settled on this after some discussion about whether a wave was sufficient.

"Paul."

"Staying for dinner?"

Rosa looked at Marnie. Marnie looked at the shopping.

"Tom's coming at eight."

"Forty minutes." He was already putting things in the fridge, the yoghurt going on the wrong shelf. "I've got eggs."

Rosa said yes before anyone had technically asked her.

He made them on toast with the good butter, the French kind that came wrapped in yellow paper and cost more than it should and was always in the flat anyway. Rosa ate standing at the counter because the Danish books were on the table and nobody moved them.

"*Smør*," Marnie said.

"What's that?"

"Butter. In Danish."

Rosa said it, got the vowel long when it wanted to be short, said it again.

"Why does Danish butter have its own word for butter?"

"All butter has its own word."

"But why does it sound like *that*?"

Paul washed the pan. "Because a thousand years ago someone looked at butter and said *smør* and everyone else agreed."

Rosa thought about this.

"What if they'd said something different?"

"They didn't," Paul said.

At three minutes to eight the flat said, in the voice Marnie had chosen when they moved in, that Tom was at the bottom of the street. Rosa had given it his number through Marnie's school calendar access, and the flat had been tracking him the way it tracked everything it had been handed, without being asked whether to.

Paul turned from the sink.

Neither of them said anything about that.

Rosa got her coat, tucked the library book under her arm, said goodnight to Paul and then to Marnie. At the door she stopped.

"*Smør*," she said. Testing whether it had stayed.

"*Smør*," Marnie said.

She went. Outside, Tom would be at the bottom of the stairs.

Paul dried his hands. Hung the tea towel back on the oven rail. The butter wrapper was still on the counter, yellow and empty.

"The flat knew he was coming," he said.

"Rosa gave it his number."

"I know." He folded the wrapper along its original crease and put it in the recycling. "I'm not arguing. I'm just saying it out loud."

The flat said nothing. It had been given an instruction and it was still running it, and it had, at minimum, the sense to be quiet.

Marnie moved the rice back to its cupboard. He watched her do it.

"Wrong one?" he said.

"It was always there."

"Makes more sense by the hob."

"It does," she said. "It still goes back."

He looked at the cupboard. Then he picked up the bag he hadn't finished emptying, set it on the counter, left it there, and went for a shower. The flat turned the boiler up a degree before he reached the bathroom door.

The butter wrapper was in the recycling. The rice was where it belonged. Outside, Rosa would be telling Tom about *smør*, getting the vowel wrong on purpose because she preferred her version. Tom would be listening with the look he had when Rosa was right about something and he was deciding whether to say so.

The flat adjusted the hall light. Nobody had asked it to. It was still making her happy, whatever that took, and tonight what that took was apparently this: the right level of light in a hall where she stood alone, thinking about a man on a pavement forty metres away.
