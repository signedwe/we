## The Student and the Bacterium

In 1979, eight infectious disease specialists sat down to judge treatment recommendations for ten meningitis cases. The prescribers whose work they were rating included five Stanford faculty physicians, a fellow, a resident, a student, the actual treating doctors, and a computer program written by a doctoral student, Edward Shortliffe, working under the direction of Bruce Buchanan and Stanley Cohen. The specialists did not know a computer was in the mix. When the ratings came back, the program had been rated acceptable in 65 percent of cases; the five Stanford faculty ranged from 42.5 to 62.5 percent. The program was called MYCIN.<sup id="fnref1"><a href="#fn1">1</a></sup>

Around 1972, Shortliffe had begun building MYCIN to help doctors treat blood infections. The clinical problem was real and urgent: bacteremia and meningitis killed quickly, the organisms responsible were not always identifiable from early tests, and the antibiotics available varied in effectiveness, side effects, and dosage by patient weight. A resident working a night shift could not always summon an infectious disease specialist, and a wrong choice of drug might cost a patient days.<sup id="fnref2"><a href="#fn2">2</a></sup>

MYCIN was written in Lisp and organised around a set of roughly 600 individual if-then rules: *if* the organism is gram-negative and the patient is a compromised host, *then* consider such-and-such treatment with a certain degree of confidence. The rules were not assembled from a textbook but extracted, over months of conversation, from Stanford's infectious disease specialists. Each rule encoded a clinical judgement as well as a clinical fact, and because judgements differ, MYCIN carried numerical weights it called certainty factors to track how confident the system was in any given conclusion.<sup id="fnref3"><a href="#fn3">3</a></sup>

The program could also explain itself. A physician who asked *why* MYCIN had reached a particular conclusion received an account of the rules it had applied and the values those rules had used. This was not a cosmetic feature. Shortliffe and his colleagues were alert to the fact that physicians would not follow advice from a machine they did not understand, and so transparency was built into MYCIN's design from the start.<sup id="fnref4"><a href="#fn4">4</a></sup>

MYCIN was never deployed in routine clinical practice. The reasons were partly legal and ethical, who was responsible if the system gave wrong advice?, and partly technical: MYCIN was a standalone system that required a user to type in all relevant patient information by hand, and it could not connect to hospital records or laboratory systems.<sup id="fnref5"><a href="#fn5">5</a></sup> These obstacles were not insuperable, but the Stanford team did not surmount them before the system receded into the literature.

As development continued, Randall Davis, a Stanford doctoral student working alongside Shortliffe, built a system called TEIRESIAS that allowed experts to interact directly with MYCIN's rule base, correcting and extending its knowledge through a conversational interface. TEIRESIAS eased the work of updating rules, but it also made the remaining limits clearer: even with an interactive tool, extracting reliable knowledge from experts and keeping it consistent was slow and laborious. The difficulty of getting knowledge in and keeping it current was the central technical problem of the whole enterprise, and the field would come to call it the knowledge-acquisition bottleneck.<sup id="fnref6"><a href="#fn6">6</a></sup>

The Stanford group drew an obvious lesson. If the MYCIN architecture could be generalised, the same rule-and-inference engine could be applied to any domain in which experts had articulable rules. They stripped MYCIN of its medical knowledge and released what remained, the inference engine and the question-answering apparatus, as EMYCIN, standing for Essential MYCIN. With EMYCIN, the work of building an expert system could be separated from the work of filling it with knowledge.<sup id="fnref7"><a href="#fn7">7</a></sup>

## The Father and the Principle

The Stanford Heuristic Programming Project, which had housed the MYCIN work, was led by Edward Feigenbaum, a former student of Herbert Simon who had come to Stanford from Berkeley in 1965. Working alongside him from the outset was Bruce Buchanan, who served as Feigenbaum's principal AI collaborator on DENDRAL and later co-edited the definitive account of MYCIN, *Rule-Based Expert Systems*, in 1984. The project that formed Feigenbaum's central insight was DENDRAL, conceived in the 1960s by Joshua Lederberg, the Nobel Prize-winning geneticist who had arrived at Stanford to chair its Department of Genetics in 1959. Lederberg was working on the problem of inferring the molecular structure of chemical compounds from mass spectrogram data, and he brought the problem to Feigenbaum. The three built DENDRAL together, with the chemist Carl Djerassi providing domain expertise in mass spectrometry.<sup id="fnref8"><a href="#fn8">8</a></sup> DENDRAL worked by drawing on the knowledge of chemists, encoded as rules, rather than by applying any general theory of chemistry. The observation that adding more knowledge made the program smarter in a way that adding more reasoning power did not was the basis of what Feigenbaum would call his central insight.

Feigenbaum is credited with coining the phrase "knowledge is power" in connection with AI programs, and with introducing the terms "expert systems" and "knowledge engineering" into the field's vocabulary. The argument, stated plainly in his 1977 paper at the International Joint Conference on Artificial Intelligence, was this: the problem-solving power exhibited in an intelligent agent's performance is primarily a consequence of the specialist's knowledge employed by the agent, and only very secondarily related to the generality and power of the inference method employed. For AI to be useful in professional fields like medicine or chemistry, it must be knowledge-rich, even if it remains methods-poor. Many researchers had previously focused on formal reasoning methods, general-purpose theorem provers and problem-solvers, and those methods had hit a ceiling. Feigenbaum's claim was that the ceiling was of their own making. The right move was not to build a better reasoner but to encode more knowledge.<sup id="fnref9"><a href="#fn9">9</a></sup>

This was a genuinely productive insight, and it deserves to be treated as such. The systems it generated, MYCIN, DENDRAL, and their successors, did things that general-purpose reasoners of the period simply could not do. The question was what it meant to generalise the principle. The logic of Feigenbaum's insight, as many in the field read it, implied that more knowledge would always produce a smarter system, and that the work of building such systems was essentially the work of extracting and codifying what experts already knew. Both implications would prove true within limits, and false beyond them.

## The Configurer

The transition from academic demonstration to commercial deployment happened in 1980, in a Digital Equipment Corporation plant in Salem, New Hampshire. The system responsible was not a medical program but a production-rule-based configurer of computer orders, written by John McDermott of Carnegie Mellon University. DEC had a particular problem. Its VAX computers were sold in components, processors, memory boards, storage devices, terminals, cables, and power supplies, and assembling a coherent system from a customer's order required a kind of expert knowledge that DEC's sales force did not reliably possess. Before McDermott's system went into use, customers routinely received systems with incompatible components, missing cables, or mismatched drivers.<sup id="fnref10"><a href="#fn10">10</a></sup>

McDermott built his system in OPS5, a production-rule language, beginning around 1978 in collaboration with DEC's engineers. The system was initially called R1, a name that McDermott glossed in Footnote 2 of his 1980 AAAI paper with the remark that "Four years ago I couldn't even say 'knowledge engineer', now I ... [are one.]", and was renamed XCON, for Expert Configurer, by DEC when it went into operational use. McDermott's 1980 AAAI conference paper on R1 won the AAAI Classic Paper Award in 1999. His fuller account appeared in 1982 in the journal *Artificial Intelligence*, volume 19, pages 39 to 88.<sup id="fnref11"><a href="#fn11">11</a></sup>

XCON went into use at DEC in January 1980 with about 750 rules. By 1986 it had processed 80,000 orders at an accuracy of 95 to 98 percent. Contemporary estimates of the annual saving differ: XCON Wikipedia, citing Barker et al. (1989), gives $25 million a year; the AI winter Wikipedia article, citing Newquist (1994), gives $40 million over six years of operation. The two figures are inconsistent and cannot be reconciled from the sources available. By 1987, XCON had grown to 6,200 rules. By 1989, it contained 10,129 rules and DEC was using it alongside a family of related systems, including XSEL for DEC's sales force and XSITE for location planning.<sup id="fnref12"><a href="#fn12">12</a></sup>

## The Gold Rush

The money followed quickly. Corporations around the world began developing expert systems, and by 1985 they were spending over a billion dollars a year on AI, most of it directed at in-house expert systems departments. An entire industry grew up to support them: software companies like Teknowledge and IntelliCorp sold expert system development shells and tools; consulting firms built AI practices. DARPA's Strategic Computing Initiative, launched in 1983 partly in response to Japan's Fifth Generation Computer Project, poured additional funding into the field. XCON had proved that the approach worked; the question now was how fast it could be sold.<sup id="fnref13"><a href="#fn13">13</a></sup>

The hardware side of this industry rested on a particular kind of machine. Expert systems ran on Lisp, the programming language that had been associated with AI research since John McCarthy invented it in the late 1950s. Ordinary computers of the early 1980s could run Lisp, but slowly: Lisp's need for automatic memory reclamation, garbage collection, and symbol manipulation pushed hard against hardware designed for other purposes. The solution, developed in the 1970s at the MIT Artificial Intelligence Laboratory, was to build computers designed specifically to run Lisp, machines that handled list operations and garbage collection in hardware, whose operating systems were written in Lisp from the microcode up, and whose development environments were correspondingly rich and powerful.<sup id="fnref14"><a href="#fn14">14</a></sup>

By the early 1980s, two companies founded by MIT AI Lab staff were selling these machines commercially. The first incorporated was Lisp Machines, Inc., founded in 1979 by Richard Greenblatt, a legendary programmer who had led the MIT Lisp Machine Project and who preferred a self-sustaining company built on advance orders to a venture-capital-funded startup. The hackers at the AI Lab chose Symbolics; Greenblatt's company was eventually launched with outside financial backing rather than MIT staff. Symbolics was incorporated on 9 April 1980; Russell Noftsker, who had been administrator of the MIT AI Lab in the late 1960s, was its first CEO and chairman, with Robert P. Adams as co-founder and first president.<sup id="fnref15"><a href="#fn15">15</a></sup>

Symbolics' initial product, the LM-2, introduced in 1981, was a repackaged version of the MIT CADR Lisp machine design. In 1983, a year later than planned, it introduced the 3600 family: a new design built around a 36-bit word with hardware support for both virtual memory and garbage collection. The operating system and software development environment comprised over 500,000 lines of Lisp. The 3600 was roughly the size of a household refrigerator, and expensive enough to match.<sup id="fnref16"><a href="#fn16">16</a></sup> MIT, Stanford, Carnegie Mellon, and government laboratories installed Symbolics machines. By the mid-1980s, Symbolics had become the dominant Lisp machine vendor.<sup id="fnref17"><a href="#fn17">17</a></sup>

Texas Instruments and Xerox entered the market too. TI, after investing in LMI when that company ran short of money, licensed LMI's Lambda design and later produced its own variant, the TI Explorer. Xerox ran a different dialect of Lisp, Interlisp-D, on its D-machines and had developed, at its Palo Alto Research Center, what was at the time one of the most advanced software development environments available. The Lisp machine market was not a monopoly; it was a small but functioning collection of competing vendors, each selling hardware that cost between fifty thousand and a hundred thousand dollars.<sup id="fnref18"><a href="#fn18">18</a></sup>

Symbolics registered symbolics.com on 15 March 1985, making it the first .com domain in the world. Symbolics had implemented one of the first TCP/IP stacks in its Genera operating system, the registration was a consequence of the company's immersion in networked computing rather than a marketing calculation, though that inference is this essay's, not the sources'.<sup id="fnref19"><a href="#fn19">19</a></sup>

## The Weight of the Rules

Inside XCON, the knowledge-acquisition bottleneck was taking a different form: not the initial difficulty of eliciting knowledge from experts, but the ongoing difficulty of keeping encoded knowledge current. The system had begun with about 750 rules and had grown to thousands. Each new DEC product required new rules. When DEC changed an existing product, added a memory option, changed a connector type, discontinued a peripheral, the rules encoding knowledge of that product had to be updated, and the interactions between rules had to be checked. By 1987, with 6,200 rules, XCON had become, in the words of one published assessment, an "exceedingly difficult software enhancement problem", in which 50 percent of the rules were changing every year. The engineering required to keep the system consistent had become a full-time operation.<sup id="fnref20"><a href="#fn20">20</a></sup>

This was not a bug in XCON's design. It was a feature of the architecture. Expert systems worked by encoding knowledge as explicit rules, and rules had to be written and maintained by human beings. As the domain grew more complex, the rule base grew with it. As the rule base grew, the interactions between rules multiplied faster than the rules themselves. And as the domain changed, as it inevitably did in a technology industry, every change required the knowledge engineers to revisit the existing rules and decide which ones needed revision. The system had no way to learn from new cases or revise its own rules.

This problem had been visible to attentive observers from early on. In 1984, at the annual meeting of the AAAI, Roger Schank and Marvin Minsky, both veterans of the first AI winter of the 1970s, issued a public warning. They described a chain reaction that would begin with pessimism in the AI community, followed by pessimism in the press, followed by severe cutbacks in funding, followed by the end of serious research. They called it, by analogy with nuclear winter, an AI winter. XCON's own maintainers had already published accounts of the growing difficulty of keeping the rule base consistent. The audience, prospering on expert systems contracts, did not listen. Three years later, the billion-dollar AI industry began to collapse.<sup id="fnref21"><a href="#fn21">21</a></sup>

## The Second Winter

The collapse began in 1987, and its immediate cause was hardware. By that year, general-purpose workstations from Sun Microsystems and others had grown powerful enough to run Lisp programs at speeds that matched or exceeded purpose-built Lisp machines. Software companies like Lucid and Franz Lisp offered increasingly capable Lisp environments that ran on standard Unix systems. The economic case for spending fifty thousand dollars on a machine optimised for Lisp disappeared when a cheaper workstation running a commercial Lisp compiler could do the same job. An entire industry worth half a billion dollars was replaced in a single year.<sup id="fnref22"><a href="#fn22">22</a></sup>

The hardware collapse brought the expert systems software industry down with it. Companies that had sunk money into expert systems found that the total cost of ownership was higher than projected. The rule bases required maintenance, and maintenance was expensive. The systems could not be extended to cover problems adjacent to their original brief without substantial new engineering. When the domains they served changed, the knowledge bases had to be manually rebuilt. And the systems were brittle: they handled situations covered by their rules, but failed when given unusual inputs that fell outside the encoded knowledge. By the early 1990s, most commercial Lisp companies had failed, including Symbolics, Lisp Machines Inc., and Lucid Inc.; Texas Instruments and Xerox withdrew from the market.<sup id="fnref23"><a href="#fn23">23</a></sup>

Symbolics had been struggling with internal conflicts before the hardware market turned against it. Noftsker and the CEO the board had brought in, Brian Sear, fought over whether to emphasise hardware or software; both were removed from the company in the late 1980s. The company entered into large long-term lease obligations in California during the boom years. The combination of strategic disarray, the collapse of the Lisp machine market, and the slowdown of DARPA's Strategic Computing Initiative, which had funded significant AI hardware purchases, proved fatal. By the early 1990s, Symbolics had entered bankruptcy.<sup id="fnref24"><a href="#fn24">24</a></sup> LMI had gone bankrupt earlier, in 1987, before its next-generation K-Machine could reach market.<sup id="fnref25"><a href="#fn25">25</a></sup>

Jack Schwarz, who took over the direction of DARPA's Information Processing Technology Office in 1987, dismissed expert systems as "clever programming" and cut AI funding, in the words of one later account, "deeply and brutally". He believed DARPA should, in his own formulation, "surf" rather than "dog paddle", funding technologies that showed genuine promise rather than supporting established approaches with known ceilings. XCON was discontinued by DEC in the early 1990s. The term "AI winter" had entered common use, and many researchers began relabelling their work to avoid the stigma; at its low point, some avoided the term "artificial intelligence" for fear of being viewed as wild-eyed dreamers.<sup id="fnref26"><a href="#fn26">26</a></sup>

## What They Got Right, and What They Got Wrong

The people who built expert systems were not credulous or careless. They were responding intelligently to what they had observed. Feigenbaum's insight that knowledge, not reasoning power, was the scarce resource in AI problem-solving was borne out repeatedly. MYCIN worked not because it had a clever inference engine, the engine was straightforward, but because it encoded the clinical knowledge of infectious disease specialists with unusual care and depth. XCON worked because McDermott and his collaborators spent years extracting the tacit knowledge of DEC's most experienced engineers and translating it into rules.<sup id="fnref27"><a href="#fn27">27</a></sup>

The error lay in the assumption that the approach was indefinitely extensible. If knowledge was power, the implication seemed to be that more knowledge was always more power, that you could build a better expert system simply by encoding more rules, and that the encoding itself, while laborious, was in principle straightforward. Neither proved true. Much of what experts know they cannot articulate, or articulate inconsistently, or articulate in ways that conflict with what other experts say. And the rules that could be articulated did not scale cleanly: a system with ten thousand rules was not simply a larger version of a system with a thousand; it was a qualitatively different engineering problem, one that the tools of the 1980s could not manage.

The Lisp machine companies failed for a related reason. They built hardware optimised for a specific programming language and a specific style of AI, at a time when general-purpose hardware was improving fast enough to overtake them. The optimisation that made a Symbolics 3600 impressive in 1983 had become a liability by 1987, because the advantages of specialisation were too small to justify the price differential against increasingly powerful general-purpose workstations.

Feigenbaum was right that knowledge matters. The bottleneck that Buchanan, Shortliffe, and the XCON maintainers had each documented, that encoding, eliciting, and updating expert knowledge was itself the hard problem, was the one the industry could not solve.
