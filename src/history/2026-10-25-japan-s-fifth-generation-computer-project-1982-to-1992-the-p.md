## Tokyo, October 1981

The Western delegations went home from Tokyo in October 1981 and did not cooperate. They panicked.

Invitations had arrived unsolicited at computer science departments around the world. Japan's Ministry of International Trade and Industry had convened an international conference to present a plan for the next generation of computing. Around eighty researchers from fourteen countries attended; around three hundred people came in total, including invited speaker Edward Feigenbaum of Stanford University, the American expert-systems pioneer.<sup id="fnref1"><a href="#fn1">1</a></sup> Tōru Moto-oka, a professor of electrical engineering at the University of Tokyo who had chaired the two-year planning committee, delivered the opening keynote, stating Japan's intention to be a full partner in the international scientific community and calling for future cooperation; Feigenbaum concluded his own talk with the same appeal.<sup id="fnref2"><a href="#fn2">2</a></sup>

## The Dream

The idea had begun not in a government ministry but over a few heady afternoons in 1978, in the office of a mid-level MITI manager named Masataka Nakano. As the cultural critic Jun'ichirō Uemae describes in his retrospective *Japanīzu Dorīmu*, Nakano was then only thirty, running a handful of young programmers as a division chief on the fourth-generation VLSI project.<sup id="fnref3"><a href="#fn3">3</a></sup>

Nakano's concern was not primarily about computing power. He was not a programmer and had no technical background; he was therefore, as the historian Colin Garvey notes in his account of the project, keenly aware of the social cost of computerisation in the Japanese office.<sup id="fnref4"><a href="#fn4">4</a></sup> Computers of the late 1970s were enormous machines requiring specialised training. Most government officials at MITI could not use them. Computer interfaces, where they were not purely numerical, were in English: Japanese users had to acquire a non-trivial amount of English on top of the technical knowledge itself, a considerable barrier. The result was that rather than streamlining the Japanese office, computers were adding to its burden.

There was a geopolitical dimension as well. IBM dominated the international computer market through users' need for backward compatibility. Even competitors who built technically superior machines found that potential customers replied that if the machines could not run existing IBM software, they could not use them.<sup id="fnref5"><a href="#fn5">5</a></sup> Nakano saw this as locking Japan into an endless cycle of imitation, telling his team: "This is why Japan has to be first to build a non-von Neumann machine. If we do, then we can race down an original path, outside of the world controlled by IBM."<sup id="fnref6"><a href="#fn6">6</a></sup>

This was the seed of the Fifth Generation. Its name followed the accepted lineage of computing hardware: vacuum tubes (first generation), transistors (second), integrated circuits (third), very large-scale integrated circuits (fourth).<sup id="fnref7"><a href="#fn7">7</a></sup> The fifth would be something categorically different: machines that operated not on sequential numerical instructions but on logical inference, in parallel, accepting natural-language input from ordinary users.

Nakano himself was rotated to a new post in another MITI division in 1979, just as the formal planning committee formed. Two-year rotations were an entirely ordinary feature of Japanese working life. His name consequently does not appear in the official FGCS literature.<sup id="fnref8"><a href="#fn8">8</a></sup> The planning committee that emerged from his idea was chaired by Moto-oka and included three subcommittees, on Basic Theory, Computer Architecture, and Social Impacts, led respectively by Kazuhiro Fuchi, Hideo Aiso, and Hajime Karatsu.<sup id="fnref9"><a href="#fn9">9</a></sup> Around one hundred representatives from science, industry, and government participated in as many meetings over two years.

The committee's ambitions were social as much as technical. As Karatsu's Social Impacts subcommittee set out in its preliminary report, the Fifth Generation computer would increase productivity in low-productivity areas, meet international competition by augmenting rather than replacing Japan's skilled labour force, assist in saving energy and resources, and help cope with an ageing society.<sup id="fnref10"><a href="#fn10">10</a></sup> Moto-oka summarised this augmentative vision in the title of his 1983 *IEEE Spectrum* article: "A Quantum Jump in Friendliness."<sup id="fnref11"><a href="#fn11">11</a></sup> As the *IEEE Spectrum* editorial staff explained separately, the project's prime aim was to make "a machine that fits the needs of people instead of making people work by the rules of the machine."<sup id="fnref12"><a href="#fn12">12</a></sup>

What the committee explicitly did not call it was an artificial intelligence project. The Japanese term *jinkō chinō*, artificial intelligence, was American in origin and American in flavour. What ICOT would pursue was *chishiki jōhō shori shisutemu*: knowledge information processing systems. The distinction mattered to the planners. As Fuchi later asserted, responding to those who conflated the Fifth Generation with American AI: "our project is not an artificial intelligence project or an expert system project as wrongly understood by some people."<sup id="fnref13"><a href="#fn13">13</a></sup>

## The Wager

In April 1982, MITI formally launched the project, establishing ICOT as a joint venture between the ministry and eight of Japan's largest computer and electronics manufacturers: Fujitsu, Hitachi, NEC, Toshiba, Mitsubishi Electric, Matsushita, Oki Electric, and Sharp.<sup id="fnref14"><a href="#fn14">14</a></sup> By 1982, Japan already held 74 percent of its own domestic computer market, making it, as the political economist Marie Anchordoguy found, "the only industrialized country in the free world where IBM was not the market leader."<sup id="fnref15"><a href="#fn15">15</a></sup> The institute opened in Tokyo under the direction of Kazuhiro Fuchi, who came from MITI's own Electrotechnical Laboratory. To the laboratory came forty young engineers and scientists, most in their twenties, from those eight firms.<sup id="fnref16"><a href="#fn16">16</a></sup>

The project was to run for ten years in three phases: an initial stage focused on basic research (1982–1985), an intermediate stage on subsystems (1985–1989), and a final stage on complete prototype systems (1989–1992).<sup id="fnref17"><a href="#fn17">17</a></sup> The budget MITI pledged was an unprecedented 100 billion yen, approximately $500 million at the time. MITI had initially stipulated that this sum was to be matched by the eight participating firms, for a combined total of nearly $1 billion. Industry never agreed to match, and MITI itself spent only approximately ¥54 billion of its own pledged share over the programme's eleven fiscal years.<sup id="fnref18"><a href="#fn18">18</a></sup>

The technical wager at the heart of the project rested on two linked bets. The first was that the correct language for the fifth generation kernel was Prolog, rather than Lisp, the American AI community's standard tool. Prolog was a logic programming language developed in Europe: invented in Marseille in 1972 by Alain Colmerauer and his team, with key theoretical contributions from Robert Kowalski in Edinburgh, whose procedural interpretation of Horn clauses provided Prolog's logical foundation.<sup id="fnref19"><a href="#fn19">19</a></sup> Fuchi had been impressed by logic programming on visits abroad and saw it as the "missing link" between knowledge information processing and the non-von Neumann hardware he intended to build. His choice of Prolog drew the ire of the American AI community: one influential commentator remarked that "Prolog is seen as somehow 'un-American' — a European fad that has now been taken up by the Japanese..."<sup id="fnref20"><a href="#fn20">20</a></sup> Fuchi defended the choice by arguing that Prolog was technically superior and that Japan, as a newcomer, had no obligation to adopt the legacy language Lisp.<sup id="fnref21"><a href="#fn21">21</a></sup>

The decision was, as Fuchi himself later acknowledged, not without risk. Referring back to the 1981 conference, he said of the plan to link logic programming to parallel hardware: "It was the most controversial issue at the conference, criticized as a reckless proposal without scientific justification."<sup id="fnref22"><a href="#fn22">22</a></sup>

The second bet was on massive parallelism. Performance targets were set in a project-specific unit, LIPS, Logical Inferences Per Second. The project aimed to raise that figure by orders of magnitude, to be achieved by designing special-purpose parallel hardware from scratch.<sup id="fnref23"><a href="#fn23">23</a></sup>

The industry participants were not enthusiastic. When they learned that Fuchi's plan had been approved and that the Fifth Generation computers would not be IBM-compatible, the eight firms attempted to back out of the project entirely.<sup id="fnref24"><a href="#fn24">24</a></sup> MITI applied pressure by threatening exclusion from future government funding opportunities, and industry eventually relented, though the companies never agreed to match funds. Instead, they conceded to sending researchers on a rotating basis to ICOT.<sup id="fnref25"><a href="#fn25">25</a></sup> The fullest account of how MITI managed this negotiation is Scott Callon's *Divided Sun: MITI and the Breakdown of Japanese High-Tech Industrial Policy, 1975–1993* (Stanford University Press, 1995).<sup id="fnref26"><a href="#fn26">26</a></sup>

Fuchi accepted the directorship of ICOT on two conditions, both radical within Japanese business culture: that he could choose any computer manufacturer to provide terminals during the initial phase, including American machines, and that researchers seconded to ICOT were to be under thirty-five years old.<sup id="fnref27"><a href="#fn27">27</a></sup> He then signalled his intention to run the institute as an autonomous research institution free from commercial pressure, by regularly and painstakingly emphasising that the project had "no commercial objectives."<sup id="fnref28"><a href="#fn28">28</a></sup>

Results were to be shared openly: published in scientific articles and conference presentations, in English as well as Japanese. ICOT would be a global research hub. As Fuchi put it, the project "must not be exclusive or closed to outsiders."<sup id="fnref29"><a href="#fn29">29</a></sup>

## The Panic

The Western response to the 1981 Tokyo conference was immediate and, in the United States particularly, alarmed. The announcement came, as Feigenbaum and Howard Shrobe observed in a survey of the project written around 1987–88, "on the heels of MITI's highly successful efforts to promote Japanese prowess in consumer electronics and computer chips," and it "set off alarm bells in capitals and companies of the West."<sup id="fnref30"><a href="#fn30">30</a></sup>

Britain moved first. A UK Department of Industry mission had attended the Tokyo conference, and its participants returned with a new assessment: rather than collaboration with the Japanese, there was "an urgent need for the U.K. to form its own fifth-generation computing strategy."<sup id="fnref31"><a href="#fn31">31</a></sup> The British government convened a committee under John Alvey of British Telecom, which produced a report proposing a collaborative government-industry research programme. The result was the Alvey Programme, launched on 9 May 1983, which the *Financial Times* announced under the headline "Britain Enters the Great Race."<sup id="fnref32"><a href="#fn32">32</a></sup>

The United States followed a different logic. Feigenbaum returned home and began travelling the country giving seminars to computer scientists and government officials. His argument took book form in 1983 as *The Fifth Generation: Artificial Intelligence and Japan's Computer Challenge to the World*, co-authored with Pamela McCorduck and published by Addison-Wesley.<sup id="fnref33"><a href="#fn33">33</a></sup> The authors were frank that they were "decidely [sic] not disinterested observers," and the book's conclusion called on Americans to mount a large-scale national project because failure to respond would mean consigning "our nation to the role of the first great postindustrial agrarian society."<sup id="fnref34"><a href="#fn34">34</a></sup> The book proved highly successful and widely influential, and was even a bestseller in Japan.<sup id="fnref35"><a href="#fn35">35</a></sup>

Feigenbaum then testified before Congress in June 1983. He warned that the era of "reasoning machines is inevitable. It is the 'manifest destiny' of computing," and offered three options: a coordinated national response, an ICOT-like centre, or nothing and disaster.<sup id="fnref36"><a href="#fn36">36</a></sup> Congress embraced the alarm. Two major American programmes followed. The Microelectronics and Computer Technology Corporation, a for-profit industrial consortium of thirteen corporations, was founded in September 1983. DARPA's Strategic Computing Initiative, a decade-long, $1 billion applications-oriented project to create autonomous weapons and AI systems for each branch of the military, was signed by President Reagan in December 1983.<sup id="fnref37"><a href="#fn37">37</a></sup>

There is a detail worth dwelling on. Robert Cooper, the DARPA director, and Robert Kahn, director of DARPA's Information Processing Techniques Office, had both attended the 1981 Tokyo conference. They returned with a quite different impression: the Japanese were still trailing the United States in computing and were headed down a dead-end. This analysis did not stop them from exploiting Feigenbaum's alarmism. In later interviews, Cooper confessed to trundling out the Japanese "as the arch-enemies" and using the alarm "unabashedly" in private conversations with members of Congress.<sup id="fnref38"><a href="#fn38">38</a></sup>

Europe joined the race with the European Strategic Programme on Research in Information Technology, ESPRIT.<sup id="fnref39"><a href="#fn39">39</a></sup> And then there was the Soviet Union. Western actors hardly noticed when the USSR, the last of the major powers to enter the race, launched its own response. The five-year Soviet plan coordinated computing projects across Bulgaria, Czechoslovakia, East Germany, Hungary, and the Soviet Union and was funded at a comparatively modest $100 million for 1984 to 1989.<sup id="fnref40"><a href="#fn40">40</a></sup> Feigenbaum and McCorduck's book, the document most responsible for generating the alarm, had dismissed the Soviets in less than two pages as doing "pretty boring stuff."<sup id="fnref41"><a href="#fn41">41</a></sup> That even Bulgaria, Czechoslovakia, East Germany, Hungary, and the USSR felt compelled to respond says something about the scale of the panic; that the West barely noticed the response says something about who the panic was really for.

Where ICOT was organised around shared research with published results and no commercial objectives, the American Microelectronics and Computer Technology Corporation almost immediately "shrouded much of its research in secrecy to protect shareholder advantage," resulting in forced "isolation between projects" that undermined the potential benefits of cooperation.<sup id="fnref42"><a href="#fn42">42</a></sup> The Strategic Computing Initiative was explicitly military in aim. The Alvey Programme was decentralised across universities and firms rather than concentrated in a single institute. All of them, in their different ways, replaced ICOT's model of open, disinterested research for the public good with the logic of the market or the military.

## What Was Actually Built

Two PIM parallel inference machines built during the project are now preserved at the National Museum of Nature and Science in Tokyo. Most histories say the Fifth Generation produced nothing. What it actually produced was the world's fastest computer oriented toward knowledge information processing, a series of software tools, and a trained engineering workforce.<sup id="fnref43"><a href="#fn43">43</a></sup>

Phase One (1982–1985) focused on logic programming. ICOT designed an early model of logic processing hardware, manufactured by Mitsubishi: a minicomputer workstation called PSI. ICOT also reworked and extended Prolog into a powerful programming tool in which the operating software of their machines could be written. This effort, done under contract by Mitsubishi, resulted in the largest Prolog program by far ever written.<sup id="fnref44"><a href="#fn44">44</a></sup>

When the project moved into Phase Two and the challenge became combining logic programming with massive parallelism, a language shift occurred. Ehud Shapiro, a logician who visited ICOT during this period, developed a subset of concurrent Prolog that left out backtracking to reduce implementation complexity. Shapiro's work inspired Kazuhiro Ueda's Guarded Horn Clauses language, and from that came KL1 (Kernel Language 1), a concurrent logic language that became the core language of the project from Phase Two onward. The "Fifty Years of Prolog" survey notes that with this departure from Prolog, "an essential part of the language's elegance and functionality was lost, and this arguably detracted from the potential impact of the FGCS."<sup id="fnref45"><a href="#fn45">45</a></sup>

The final phase produced five architecturally distinct Parallel Inference Machine (PIM) models, used to run and evaluate demonstration software in domains including molecular biology, legal reasoning, and logic circuit design. ICOT also developed PIMOS, the operating system for the parallel inference machines, written in KL1. When the project concluded, ICOT released its software deliverables free of charge on the young internet, including PIMOS, making the project's language work available to researchers worldwide without needing access to the PIM hardware.<sup id="fnref46"><a href="#fn46">46</a></sup> That free release is a better model for publicly funded AI research than most of what the alarm produced elsewhere, though that comparison is this essay's, not the sources'.

## Why It Failed, and Why the Verdict Is Harder to Read

Most technical goals were met. Almost no industrial legacy survived.

The word "failure" attached itself to the Fifth Generation so quickly and thoroughly that it is now difficult to assess the project without first noticing that the verdict was partly imported from outside. As Ehud Shapiro and David Warren observed in their 1993 retrospective, the West had perceived Japan as "promising to make the dream of artificial intelligence (AI) come true", claims that were "further amplified by scientists around the world, who capitalized on the fear of Japanese technological supremacy in order to scare their own governments into funding research."<sup id="fnref47"><a href="#fn47">47</a></sup> When Japanese computers had not dominated the global economy by the early 1990s, many in the West judged the project a failure by the standard of goals that the project itself had never set.

Fuchi noted the resulting Catch-22 with some bitterness. Noting how "an exaggerated image of the project was engendered" early and seemed to persist, he complained that the project first had to face criticism, based on that false image, "that it was a reckless project trying to tackle impossible goals", and then faced the criticism "that the project has failed because it has been unable to realize those grand goals."<sup id="fnref48"><a href="#fn48">48</a></sup>

The most sustained contemporary scholarly dissent came from J. Marshall Unger, whose 1987 book *The Fifth Generation Fallacy* argued that the West had completely misunderstood Japan's interest in AI.<sup id="fnref49"><a href="#fn49">49</a></sup> Unger's central claim was that the project had been motivated above all by Japan's need to overcome the problems of its own writing system for computing, a domestic, linguistic problem, not a bid for global economic dominance. This affirmative thesis was received sceptically by the scholarly community: the 1988 *Computational Linguistics* review found Unger's writing-system argument "quite preposterous."<sup id="fnref50"><a href="#fn50">50</a></sup> But Unger's diagnosis of Western mischaracterisation, his attack on the Feigenbaum-McCorduck account of the project as an economic threat, was well taken, and the book is a reminder that the FGCS was consistently misread on both sides of the alarm.

There was, however, a genuine technical problem, and it was not Japan's alone. The core bet of the Fifth Generation, that inference grounded in symbolic logic was the road to machine intelligence, was the mainstream view in AI research worldwide in the 1980s. Over the same decade, general-purpose processors improved in performance and fell in price at a pace that nobody anticipated. By the time the PIM hardware was complete, RISC workstations and Intel-based PCs had overtaken purpose-built inference machines on price-performance, bringing the collapse of expert-systems investment across the field that became known as the second AI winter.<sup id="fnref51"><a href="#fn51">51</a></sup>

The gap between ICOT's research and commercial practice was visible long before the project ended. Feigenbaum and Shrobe, writing in the late 1980s, found that even ICOT's own member companies were "making little or no direct use of the ICOT research results." Fujitsu, the commercial AI leader in Japan, sold a Lisp machine derivative of American designs rather than a Prolog machine; its best-selling expert-systems shell was Lisp-based and derived from Stanford University software.<sup id="fnref52"><a href="#fn52">52</a></sup> The problem was not merely that the project left no commercial bridge at its close; the bridge was never being built during the project itself.

This was not a uniquely Japanese miscalculation. DARPA's Strategic Computing Initiative "did not quite end; it simply disappeared," having "vanished" from the DARPA budget by 1993, in Roland and Shiman's account.<sup id="fnref53"><a href="#fn53">53</a></sup> The American market for special-purpose AI hardware collapsed in 1987. The Alvey Programme concluded with mixed results. What Daniel Crevier, in his history of AI, called the "Rollercoaster of the 1980s" resulted in near-total collapse by the end of the decade across all the major programmes.<sup id="fnref54"><a href="#fn54">54</a></sup>

On the other hand, the staffing model produced a training effect that ICOT itself always regarded as its first and most important achievement. By the end of Phase One in 1985, ICOT managers counted approximately 1,000 trained engineers in the field, including ICOT researchers, engineers in the cooperating sections of member company laboratories, and engineers in the proprietary sections.<sup id="fnref55"><a href="#fn55">55</a></sup>

Compared to its Western counterparts, the verdict on the Fifth Generation is paradoxical. The SCI, MCC, Alvey, and ESPRIT are typically remembered as partial successes; Japan's project is remembered as an outright failure. Daniel Crevier's framing is representative: whereas the SCI merely "disappointed its military sponsors," the Japanese, he claims, "fell far short of their objectives."<sup id="fnref56"><a href="#fn56">56</a></sup>

ICOT met most of its own technical milestones. The SCI vanished from its budget. Alvey concluded years before the Fifth Generation finished.

What the Fifth Generation did not deliver was the one thing it had explicitly not promised: commercial dominance of the global computing market. That goal was Feigenbaum's, not Fuchi's. MITI considered the project a success on its own merits, a view confirmed by Nakamura and Shibuya's 1996 analysis of Japanese technology policy.<sup id="fnref57"><a href="#fn57">57</a></sup> Economic payoff, as Feigenbaum and Shrobe conceded in their own 1993 evaluation of the project, was never among ICOT's stated goals.<sup id="fnref58"><a href="#fn58">58</a></sup>

The deeper failure was one of the bet itself, shared with the entire field. Nobody in 1982, not Fuchi, not Moto-oka, not the DARPA planners, not the Alvey committee, foresaw how far cheap, general-purpose computers built on exactly the von Neumann architecture that the Fifth Generation had proposed to replace would go. As the leading University of Tokyo AI researcher Yutaka Matsuo later reflected: "I know there is no 'What if?' in history, but my dream is to imagine that if the Web had appeared 15 years earlier, Japan would be sitting where Silicon Valley is right now."<sup id="fnref59"><a href="#fn59">59</a></sup>

The supposed failure left a long shadow in Japan. From roughly 1997 to 2002, Matsuo observed, AI was so taboo in Japanese computing circles that to use even the Japanese word for it, *jinkō chinō*, drew condemnation.<sup id="fnref60"><a href="#fn60">60</a></sup>

## What Remains

Nakano, the civil servant who had the idea in 1978 and was then rotated out before the project began, was on a business trip in New York when the Tokyo conference of 1981 set the world computing community in motion.<sup id="fnref61"><a href="#fn61">61</a></sup> By the time the project closed, Fuchi and his colleagues had spent a decade being told they were building either a monster or a failure, depending on the year and the continent. The monster never appeared. The failure is harder to locate than the histories suggest.

The Fifth Generation project was the first national, large-scale AI research and development programme to be conducted at this scale and with this explicit public-goods orientation, without military funding or commercial objectives, a distinction Garvey describes as comparatively unique among the national computing projects of the era.<sup id="fnref62"><a href="#fn62">62</a></sup> Its results were published openly, in English as well as Japanese, and eventually released on the young internet. Hundreds of international researchers visited ICOT each year. It built real hardware, two models of which are in a national museum.

At its close, ICOT released its software archive free of charge on the internet. The archive is still there.
