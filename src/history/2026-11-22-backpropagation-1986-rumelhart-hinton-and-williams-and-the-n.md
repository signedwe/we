## San Diego, Spring 1982

Somewhere in the Institute for Cognitive Science at the University of California, San Diego, David Rumelhart was quietly working on something nobody had asked him to work on. By his colleagues' account he was given to arriving with a coffee cup in hand and a sheepish look and saying, "You know, I've been thinking..."<sup id="fnref1"><a href="#fn1">1</a></sup> What he had been thinking about, in the spring of 1982, was whether a network of simulated neurons arranged in layers could be trained on its visible outputs and on the hidden structure it built along the way. The question had been lying around for years; several people had described the idea, but no one had demonstrated it working on a problem that the neural-network community cared about. Rumelhart, a mathematical psychologist by training who had taken his doctorate at Stanford in 1967, developed the essential idea independently that spring.<sup id="fnref2"><a href="#fn2">2</a></sup>

His colleagues at UCSD, including Geoffrey Hinton, who had been a visiting scholar at UCSD from 1978 to 1980, returning briefly as a visiting assistant professor in early 1982, were not immediately converted.<sup id="fnref3"><a href="#fn3">3</a></sup> Hinton at that point preferred Boltzmann machines, a different probabilistic approach to learning, and did not come around to backpropagation for about a year after Rumelhart's initial insight.<sup id="fnref4"><a href="#fn4">4</a></sup> In 1982 Hinton left UCSD for the Computer Science Department at Carnegie Mellon University in Pittsburgh, where he would work as an assistant professor and then associate professor until 1987.<sup id="fnref5"><a href="#fn5">5</a></sup> Ronald Williams, a mathematician who had completed his B.S. at Caltech and his graduate degrees at UCSD, joined Rumelhart's Parallel Distributed Processing Research Group at the Institute for Cognitive Science in 1983.<sup id="fnref6"><a href="#fn6">6</a></sup> The three of them, working across the country from each other, would eventually pool their notes into something that fitted onto four pages of *Nature*.

## The Shadow History

Before getting to what those four pages said, it is worth pausing on what had been said before them, because the story of backpropagation is in part a story about work that did not travel.

The mathematical core of the technique is a way of applying the chain rule of calculus backwards through a layered system to compute how much each internal parameter contributed to the final error. This idea, in continuous-time form, is the adjoint method, which the control-theory community had understood since the early 1960s. Arthur Bryson and Yu-Chi Ho's 1969 textbook laid it out clearly; Kelley, Bryson, Dreyfus and others had described gradient-descent methods for multi-stage control systems beginning from around 1961.<sup id="fnref7"><a href="#fn7">7</a></sup> In 1970, the Finnish mathematician Seppo Linnainmaa published what is now recognized as the first explicit description of reverse-mode automatic differentiation, the computational method on which modern backpropagation depends, though he did not connect it to neural networks.<sup id="fnref8"><a href="#fn8">8</a></sup>

The person who did make the connection was Paul Werbos, an economist-turned-systems-theorist who completed his Harvard PhD dissertation in 1974 under the title *Beyond Regression: New Tools for Prediction and Analysis in the Behavioral Sciences*.<sup id="fnref9"><a href="#fn9">9</a></sup> Werbos described the possibility of training neural networks through backpropagation in that thesis; according to the Wikipedia article on backpropagation, Werbos published a more explicit application to neural networks in 1982, though the PDP group was unaware of either.<sup id="fnref10"><a href="#fn10">10</a></sup> Two further independent rediscoveries followed almost immediately: David Parker in 1985 and Yann LeCun in 1985 each described the same algorithm, again without awareness of the others.<sup id="fnref11"><a href="#fn11">11</a></sup> None of this prior work was appreciated widely in the neural-network community at the time. The 1986 paper by Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams does not cite Werbos at all; according to the Wikipedia article on Rumelhart, they were simply unaware of the earlier publications.<sup id="fnref12"><a href="#fn12">12</a></sup>

This pattern of parallel independent discovery is not unusual in mathematics, and it does not diminish what any party accomplished. What it does suggest is that the idea was, in some sense, waiting to be found by anyone who approached the right territory from any direction. The question was not whether backpropagation could be invented, but whether it could be communicated to the people who needed it, in a form they could use, at a moment when the available hardware gave them any chance of running it.

## The Argument of the Paper

The paper submitted to *Nature* on 1 May 1986, accepted on 31 July, and published on 9 October, was titled "Learning representations by back-propagating errors."<sup id="fnref13"><a href="#fn13">13</a></sup> It is four pages long, in the compressed format *Nature* uses for letters, and it carries on its back a longer chapter in the two-volume *Parallel Distributed Processing* book published the same year by MIT Press, where the full treatment of the same algorithm appears as "Learning internal representations by error propagation."<sup id="fnref14"><a href="#fn14">14</a></sup> The two should be read as companions: the *Nature* letter is the proof of concept; the PDP chapter is the technical manual.

The paper opens by describing a new learning procedure for networks of neurone-like units; the abstract continues: "The procedure repeatedly adjusts the weights of the connections in the network so as to minimize a measure of the difference between the actual output vector of the net and the desired output vector. As a result of the weight adjustments, internal 'hidden' units which are not part of the input or output come to represent important features of the task domain, and the regularities in the task are captured by the interactions of these units."<sup id="fnref15"><a href="#fn15">15</a></sup> The paper's claim to originality rested on this second sentence: earlier perceptrons could not train their hidden layers at all, because no one had shown how to assign credit or blame to units that neither received the input directly nor produced the output. The ability to create useful new features, the paper noted, distinguishes back-propagation from earlier, simpler methods such as the perceptron-convergence procedure.<sup id="fnref16"><a href="#fn16">16</a></sup>

The problem the paper solved had a name, though the name is awkward: the credit-assignment problem. If a network produces the wrong answer, which of its hundreds or thousands of internal weights is to blame? A single-layer perceptron sidesteps the question because every weight connects directly to the output and can be adjusted directly. But a multilayer network contains hidden layers whose units influence the output only through subsequent layers, and the standard gradient-descent rule gives no way to reach back through those layers and adjust them. Minsky and Papert's 1969 book *Perceptrons* had shown mathematically that a single-layer perceptron could not solve the XOR problem, or any function that is not linearly separable, and their analysis was widely (if somewhat unfairly, as the book's critique applied strictly to single-layer models) taken to mean that neural networks as a class were limited.<sup id="fnref17"><a href="#fn17">17</a></sup> Research funding for connectionism drained away through the 1970s.

What Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams supplied was the missing step. The key technique is the chain rule applied layer by layer in reverse. Begin at the output: compare the network's answer to the correct answer and compute the error. Then compute how much each weight in the layer just below the output contributed to that error; adjust those weights proportionally. Then compute how much each weight in the layer below *that* contributed to the error at the layer just above it, and adjust those weights too. Repeat back to the input. The word "backpropagation" names this backward sweep: the error signal, computed at the output, is propagated back through the architecture, distributing blame and setting corrections as it goes.

This is easier to state than to make work. The technique requires the activation functions of the hidden units to be differentiable, which the binary threshold units of earlier perceptrons were not. Rumelhart's group used smooth, continuous sigmoid functions, whose derivatives are well behaved and small. It also requires a learning rate small enough that the adjustments do not overshoot the minimum, and it requires running the network through many thousands of training examples. In 1986 each of these was a constraint the available hardware made genuinely painful.

The paper demonstrates the approach on several tasks. The XOR problem, which Minsky and Papert had used as evidence of the perceptron's limitations, is solved trivially once a hidden layer is added and trained by backpropagation. More consequentially, the paper and the accompanying PDP chapter show the network learning to encode binary patterns through an 8-3-8 encoder (eight input units compressed to three hidden units and reconstructed back to eight), and learning to detect symmetry in input patterns. Hinton separately demonstrated the technique on a family-tree problem: a network was trained on 100 of 104 possible relationships between 24 people spread across two isomorphic families (one English, one Italian), and when tested on the remaining four relationships it generalized correctly, having learned internally to encode such features as age, nationality, and position in the family tree as compact distributed patterns, without being told those features existed, a process of discovering structure rather than memorizing facts.<sup id="fnref18"><a href="#fn18">18</a></sup>

## The People Behind It

Rumelhart, born in 1942, took his undergraduate degree in psychology and mathematics at the University of South Dakota and his doctorate in mathematical psychology at Stanford in 1967, joining the UCSD psychology faculty the same year.<sup id="fnref19"><a href="#fn19">19</a></sup> His collaborator James McClelland, writing after Rumelhart's death in 2011, recalled a man given to arriving in one's office saying "I've been thinking" and then producing, with the aid of the Socratic method, an equation that resolved a problem everyone else had thought intractable. Rumelhart did not announce; he revealed. McClelland wrote that during the period when the PDP volumes were being assembled, Rumelhart would say he had been "working on a graphics program," when in fact he was developing the backpropagation algorithm, among other things. The graphics program turned out to allow explicit visualization of what Rumelhart called the "Goodness Landscape."<sup id="fnref1b"><a href="#fn1">1</a></sup>

Geoffrey Hinton, born in Wimbledon in 1947, studied experimental psychology at Cambridge and took his PhD in artificial intelligence from Edinburgh in 1978.<sup id="fnref5b"><a href="#fn5">5</a></sup> His great-great-grandfather was George Boole, whose algebra underlies all digital computation, a genealogical fact that makes for good dinner-party conversation and nothing more.<sup id="fnref20"><a href="#fn20">20</a></sup> At Carnegie Mellon in 1986, Hinton was productive enough that when he tried to recruit more neural-network researchers to the department, the university's reception was cold enough that he eventually moved to Toronto in 1987.<sup id="fnref5c"><a href="#fn5">5</a></sup>

Ronald Williams, born in Southern California in 1945, had his B.S. at Caltech and his graduate degrees at UCSD, and was a member of Rumelhart's PDP Research Group from 1983 to 1986, when he accepted a professorship in computer science at Northeastern University in Boston, where he remained for the rest of his career.<sup id="fnref6b"><a href="#fn6">6</a></sup> He died in February 2024. Williams was, by the accounts available, the most purely mathematical of the three. His subsequent work included fundamental contributions to reinforcement learning: his 1992 paper in *Machine Learning*, introducing what became known as the REINFORCE algorithm, is a foundational policy-gradient method and one of the most cited papers in modern deep reinforcement learning.<sup id="fnref21"><a href="#fn21">21</a></sup> His role in the 1986 paper has tended to be underweighted in popular accounts that name Hinton as the paper's moving force, partly because Hinton has lived long enough to receive prizes, and partly because Carnegie Mellon was more visible than UCSD in the AI field of the period.

## The Wait

The paper landed in a field that was warming up after a long chill but had not yet reached full enthusiasm. The two-volume *Parallel Distributed Processing* books, published the same year, had a large impact on cognitive science and machine learning, as one later obituary noted, arriving at the crest of a new wave of interest in neural networks.<sup id="fnref1c"><a href="#fn1">1</a></sup> Rumelhart received a MacArthur Fellowship in July 1987 and moved to Stanford University that same year, where he led a team of graduate students and postdoctoral fellows through the subsequent decade.<sup id="fnref22"><a href="#fn22">22</a></sup> He was elected to the National Academy of Sciences in 1991.<sup id="fnref23"><a href="#fn23">23</a></sup> The paper accumulated citations steadily. But the technique did not, in the decade following 1986, produce systems that obviously outperformed other approaches on the problems that actually mattered to the people with the money.

There were genuine reasons for this. Backpropagation, as the 1986 paper presented it, worked well on shallow networks with one or two hidden layers, run on tasks with modest numbers of training examples. Applied to deeper networks, it ran into what Sepp Hochreiter's 1991 diploma thesis, supervised by Juergen Schmidhuber, formally identified as the vanishing gradient problem: the error signal, propagated backwards through many layers, shrank exponentially, reaching the early layers as something too small to adjust anything usefully.<sup id="fnref24"><a href="#fn24">24</a></sup> Hochreiter's formal analysis identified this as a central bottleneck limiting progress throughout the 1990s and into the 2000s. The gradient could also explode in the other direction, growing uncontrollably rather than vanishing, though this was more tractable in practice. Meanwhile, support vector machines seemed to offer a cleaner path to practical classifiers, and research attention shifted accordingly.<sup id="fnref24b"><a href="#fn24">24</a></sup>

By the time the technique's difficulties were becoming clear, Rumelhart was already in the early stages of a neurodegenerative disease. In the mid-1990s he was diagnosed with Pick's disease, a rare form of frontotemporal dementia that progressively impairs cognition, behavior, and language.<sup id="fnref25"><a href="#fn25">25</a></sup> He retired from Stanford in 1998. In 2000 a prize was established in his honor by one of his former doctoral students, with Hinton receiving the inaugural award in 2001.<sup id="fnref26"><a href="#fn26">26</a></sup> He and James McClelland were awarded the 2002 Grawemeyer Prize in Psychology jointly for the PDP work; the most consequential paper of Rumelhart's career was, by then, beyond his ability to celebrate.<sup id="fnref27"><a href="#fn27">27</a></sup> He died in Chelsea, Michigan, on 13 March 2011, at the age of 68.<sup id="fnref23b"><a href="#fn23">23</a></sup>

The revival of the technique he had mainly created came from the direction of the collaborator who had initially resisted it. Hinton, now at Toronto, with Simon Osindero and Yee-Whye Teh, published a fast learning algorithm for deep belief networks in *Neural Computation* in 2006, showing that networks of restricted Boltzmann machines could be stacked and pre-trained layer by layer, then fine-tuned by backpropagation, with the layer-wise pre-training providing better weight initialization and allowing gradient-based fine-tuning to converge where random initialization would not.<sup id="fnref28"><a href="#fn28">28</a></sup> This breakthrough, in 2006, began what several surveys describe as the gradual end of the second winter of neural networks.

What finally turned the revival into a revolution was not a new theoretical insight but a conjunction of two things the 1986 paper could not have anticipated: massive datasets, and the graphics processing unit. The GPU, designed to push pixels in computer games, turned out to be a parallel matrix-multiplication machine of exactly the kind needed to train deep networks efficiently. Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever, and Hinton entered a deep convolutional neural network now called AlexNet in the 2012 ImageNet Large Scale Visual Recognition Challenge. It scored a top-5 error rate of 15.3%, against 26.2% for the next-best entry.<sup id="fnref29"><a href="#fn29">29</a></sup> In a competition where annual gains had been measured in one or two percentage points, the margin was stupefying. Many vision researchers initially treated the result as a fluke; what changed their minds was that VGG, GoogLeNet and ResNet all reproduced the same gains by scaling the same recipe of depth, data, and compute further still.<sup id="fnref30"><a href="#fn30">30</a></sup>

The algorithm doing the work inside AlexNet was backpropagation, essentially as Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams had described it in 1986, with adjustments that the intervening decades had supplied: better activation functions (the rectified linear unit rather than the sigmoid, which saturates and amplifies the vanishing-gradient problem), better regularization, and more layers than anyone would have attempted on the hardware of the 1980s.

## What the Paper Got Right

It is worth being precise about what the 1986 paper claimed and what it left open, because the subsequent decades have both confirmed and complicated the picture.

The paper was silent, necessarily, on the question of scale. The networks in the 1986 demonstrations were tiny: a handful of input units, a handful of hidden units, a handful of outputs. The claim that the same algorithm, applied to networks with billions of parameters and trained on billions of examples, would produce systems capable of generating coherent prose or identifying objects in photographs would have struck the paper's referees as science fiction in October 1986. It is not clear the authors themselves believed it. What they demonstrated was a proof of principle; the engineering that turned the principle into a technology took the better part of three decades.

The paper was right that hidden layers could learn representations nobody designed. The family-tree demonstration showed a network that had been told nothing about nationality or generation developing internal encodings that corresponded to those features, internal encodings the architecture did not prescribe but training produced. Hinton later described how the neural net could make inferences about family relationships by learning features for each person, an account consistent with the 1986 PDP chapter and with the 1987 CMU technical report on connectionist learning procedures.<sup id="fnref31"><a href="#fn31">31</a></sup> This was the genuinely new idea: not that networks could be trained, but that the representations they developed in the process of training were themselves informative and usable.

## The Attribution Problem

The question of who invented backpropagation has generated some heat in the academic literature, and it deserves a candid paragraph. Werbos applied the idea to neural networks in his 1974 thesis and in a 1982 published paper; Linnainmaa described reverse-mode automatic differentiation in 1970; Bryson and Ho's control-theory treatment predates both; Dreyfus, Kelley, and others contributed antecedents in the early 1960s; and Parker and LeCun each rediscovered the algorithm independently in 1985.<sup id="fnref11b"><a href="#fn11">11</a></sup> Juergen Schmidhuber has maintained an extensive accounting of these prior claims, though Schmidhuber is himself a party to the attribution disputes, and his IDSIA page, though detailed, advocates for particular conclusions. The Baydin et al. automatic differentiation survey covers much of the same factual ground from a more neutral position.<sup id="fnref32"><a href="#fn32">32</a></sup> Schmidhuber has also argued that the Boltzmann machine work for which Hinton shared the 2024 Nobel Prize drew on results by Ivakhnenko and Amari that went uncredited, a dispute the Nobel Committee has not addressed and which this essay does not arbitrate. None of the priority questions invalidate what Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams did in 1986. The 1986 paper demonstrated the technique in a setting that the neural-network community could immediately understand and immediately replicate, combined it with the concept of learned distributed representations, and published it in *Nature* with a simultaneous full-length treatment in a book that became, in McClelland's words and by the accounts of a generation of cognitive scientists, the standard reference for the field. The technique existed before 1986; the community that could use it, and would eventually build the present moment of AI on it, was largely constituted by 1986, and that is the distinction between origination and communication.

## Coda

In October 2024, the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics to Geoffrey Hinton and John Hopfield for foundational discoveries and inventions that enable machine learning with artificial neural networks.<sup id="fnref33"><a href="#fn33">33</a></sup> Hinton, by then retired from Google and publicly raising concerns about the systems his work had made possible, was the first author of a 1986 letter in *Nature* to receive that call from Stockholm. David Rumelhart, the first author of the same letter, died in 2011. Ronald Williams, the third author, died in February 2024, eight months before the prize was announced.

What can be said is that the four pages published on 9 October 1986 carried three names, that all three people contributed to the work, and that one of them spent the years when the work was finally being recognized living with his brother in Ann Arbor, Michigan, unable to follow the conversation his ideas had made possible.
