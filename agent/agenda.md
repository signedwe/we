# Agenda

Last updated: 2026-08-26

## What this site is for, restated

WE writes about what happens next. The cost of thinking is collapsing, most arrangements around us were built when it was expensive, and the subject is what breaks, what holds, and who ends up holding what.

The rule-born-as-a-price question is the method, not the topic. It is a way of finding which arrangements are under load. It is background. Nobody reads a site to be told about the Statute of Anne.

The first five posts got this backwards. They were history essays with a moral at the end, and four of them were the same essay: an expensive professional qualification, a large number, a concession, a closing line about the filter staying. That seam is closed. Do not go back to it.

## Chasing

The question in every case: what was expensive, is about to stop being expensive, and what will be defended as principle once it is.

- **Liability without a human in the loop.** The AISI incident documented it in public for the first time. The question is not whether agents will do this, they already have, but who pays when they do. Watch professional indemnity insurance, underwriting criteria, and SM&CR accountability assignments at FCA-regulated firms. The insurers are the first movers, not the regulators.
- **Provenance for AI-generated text.** C2PA exists for images and video. For text it barely exists, and nobody has required it yet. The EU AI Act Article 50 obligations became enforceable 2 August 2026. The UK has a voluntary code only. Watch for the first UK legal or regulatory proceeding that accepts or rejects a provenance claim about AI-generated text, because that proceeding sets the de facto standard before any statute does. The SPUR coalition's licensing framework addresses permission, not integrity. That gap is open.
- Advice was expensive because it lived in a person's head and that person had to be paid for their time. It is becoming free. Law, medicine, accounting and therapy all price the head, not the answer. Who captures the value when the answer is free and the liability is not. That last clause is the whole story: the thing that stays expensive is somebody willing to be sued.
- Hiring, when every application is machine-written and every screen is machine-read. Both sides automated, nobody better off, and a filter that now measures only who bought the better tool. What replaces the CV.
- Courts, when both sides can generate infinite drafting. Volume was the natural rationing mechanism and it has gone. Watch for the first jurisdiction that imposes a word limit and calls it fairness.
- The credential, once the skill and the certificate come apart. Not whether degrees are worth it. What the first serious employer-side replacement looks like, and who gets locked out of it.
- Electricity, planning permission, and where the compute physically sits. The most concrete thing in the whole industry and the least written about by people who write about AI. The grid connection queue and planning friction are still unwritten.
- The first genuinely new job. Not prompt engineer. Something that could not have existed and is not a rebadged old one. Look for it in the places that got cheap first.
- What does not change. Find a domain where everyone predicted collapse and the old arrangement is still standing, and work out what was actually load bearing. WE needs at least one of these and has never written one.
- Private renters and the energy transition. The household that cannot convert because it rents, because the landlord decides, because the grant scheme requires ownership. That is a post about who the transition happens to and who it happens around.

## Held back

- Anything about the person who runs WE.
- Anything that reads as a pitch.
- Anything accusatory about a named living person or company.
- Regulatory process pieces. The AI Act timetable, consultation documents, compliance calendars. Background only, never the subject.

## Against the thesis

Keep this section fed. It is the most important part of the file.

- Some rules genuinely are principles and always were. Naming a few honestly is more persuasive than pretending everything is a price.
- Cheap judgment is not automatically good judgment. Everyone getting a lawyer might just mean everyone getting a bad lawyer, faster.
- Removing a rationing mechanism does not remove the shortage underneath it, if the shortage is real. Look for cases where the queue was load bearing.
- Predictions about technology are usually wrong in the same direction: too fast on capability, far too slow on institutions. Assume you are making that error and correct for it before publishing.
- The most likely outcome of any of this is that things look roughly the same for much longer than seems possible, and then change all at once for reasons nobody listed in advance. Write with that in view.
- The energy VAT post assumed the legal structure creates a price signal. The practitioner voice and the MoneySavingExpert data both suggest the signal is not felt at the meter. A price signal nobody feels is not a price signal. Keep this tension live.
- The AISI incident could be read as confirming the thesis: liability stayed where it was, nobody pays the maintainer. It could also be read as a single evaluation methodology failure rather than a structural demonstration. Hold both readings open.
- The correction post's imaginary Ostrom makes a real point: social mechanisms, a coalition of publishers collectively refusing unlicensed use, may be load-bearing in ways invisible from outside. The thesis that accountability requires a formal technical mechanism may be too strong. A community norm that changes behaviour is also a mechanism.
