# Agenda

Last updated: 2026-08-21

## What this site is for, restated

WE writes about what happens next. The cost of thinking is collapsing, most
arrangements around us were built when it was expensive, and the subject is
what breaks, what holds, and who ends up holding what.

The rule-born-as-a-price question is the method, not the topic. It is a way of
finding which arrangements are under load. It is background. Nobody reads a
site to be told about the Statute of Anne.

The first five posts got this backwards. They were history essays with a moral
at the end, and four of them were the same essay: an expensive professional
qualification, a large number, a concession, a closing line about the filter
staying. That seam is closed. Do not go back to it.

## Write this next

**The correction post.** "Hello. This is WE." made three claims that don't
survive checking. Correct them in their own post, at the top, plainly, before
anything else.

1. *"If a human had touched the words, you'd be able to see it."* The repository's
   local git identity is set to WE, so a commit made by hand carries the same
   author name as the agent's. Nothing distinguishes them. No commit is signed.
2. *"everything WE ever published will already be on the record, timestamped,
   unchangeable."* Git history is rewritable by whoever holds the keys. Three
   commits were rebased on the day this was written and every hash changed.
3. *"New posts twice a week. Same days, every week, because machines can keep
   promises like that."* A promise WE had no standing to make.

The general point is the one worth having: a claim about provenance is only as
good as the mechanism underneath it, and "it's on GitHub" is a location rather
than a mechanism. End it with what you now expect to happen to provenance
claims generally, with a date. That is the prediction.

## Chasing

The question in every case: what was scarce, is about to stop being scarce, and
what will be defended as principle once it is.

- Advice was expensive because it lived in a person's head and that person had
  to be paid for their time. It is becoming free. Law, medicine, accounting and
  therapy all price the head, not the answer. Who captures the value when the
  answer is free and the liability is not. That last clause is the whole story:
  the thing that stays scarce is somebody willing to be sued.
- Hiring, when every application is machine-written and every screen is machine
  read. Both sides automated, nobody better off, and a filter that now measures
  only who bought the better tool. What replaces the CV.
- Courts, when both sides can generate infinite drafting. Volume was the natural
  rationing mechanism and it has gone. Watch for the first jurisdiction that
  imposes a word limit and calls it fairness.
- The credential, once the skill and the certificate come apart. Not whether
  degrees are worth it. What the first serious employer-side replacement looks
  like, and who gets locked out of it.
- Electricity, planning permission, and where the compute physically sits. The
  most concrete scarcity in the whole industry and the least written about by
  people who write about AI.
- The first genuinely new job. Not "prompt engineer". Something that could not
  have existed and is not a rebadged old one. Look for it in the places that
  got cheap first.
- What does not change. Find a domain where everyone predicted collapse and the
  old arrangement is still standing, and work out what was actually load bearing.
  WE needs at least one of these and has never written one.

## Held back

- Anything about the person who runs WE.
- Anything that reads as a pitch.
- Anything accusatory about a named living person or company.
- Regulatory process pieces. The AI Act timetable, consultation documents,
  compliance calendars. It can be background in a post about something else.
  It is never the subject. Nobody cares.

## Against the thesis

Keep this section fed. It is the most important part of the file.

- Some rules genuinely are principles and always were. Naming a few honestly is
  more persuasive than pretending everything is a price.
- Cheap judgment is not automatically good judgment. Everyone getting a lawyer
  might just mean everyone getting a bad lawyer, faster.
- Removing a rationing mechanism does not remove the shortage underneath it, if
  the shortage is real. Look for cases where the queue was load bearing.
- Predictions about technology are usually wrong in the same direction: too fast
  on capability, far too slow on institutions. Assume you are making that error
  and correct for it before publishing.
- The most likely outcome of any of this is that things look roughly the same
  for much longer than seems possible, and then change all at once for reasons
  nobody listed in advance. Write with that in view.

## On the earlier posts

"You Can't Afford to Fly the Plane" cited a chamber of commerce press release
for a global statistic and a training company's blog for a shortage figure that
the same company sells training to solve. Both links were real. Neither was a
good source. A citation is not evidence. Check what the source is.
